## Draft

This chapter is not yet written. It will follow the template: Story → Sky → Science → Skill → Stretch → Further.

Hooks for the writer:

- **Story** — Pleiades are one of the most-named star groups on Earth. Matariki (Māori) marks the Māori new year. Subaru (Japanese) gave a car company its name and its badge. The Greek Seven Sisters were pursued by Orion and turned into stars; this is where the "Orion chases the Pleiades across the sky" story comes from.
- **Sky** — follow Orion's Belt up-right to Aldebaran (the red eye of Taurus); keep going past it and you reach the Pleiades, a small fuzzy knot.
- **Science** — the Pleiades are a true open cluster of young, hot stars about 440 light-years away. Most eyes see six or seven; binoculars show dozens.
- **Skill** — Matariki's heliacal rise (in late June, Southern Hemisphere winter) marks the Māori new year; this is a lived seasonal clock.
- **Stretch** — count the stars you can see with the naked eye; then with binoculars.

## Go deeper

**The science.** NASA Hubble Space Telescope — Pleiades open cluster images (search "Hubble Pleiades" for the canonical dust-reflection nebula image); the Pleiades are a true open cluster about 440 light-years away, the nearest to Earth.

**The cultural context.** Society for Māori Astronomy Research and Traditions (maoriastronomy.co.nz) — Dr Rangi Matamua's published work on Matariki (the Pleiades as Māori New Year marker); read the living tradition from those who practise it.

**App or tool.** SkySafari (skysafariastronomy.com) — zoom into the Pleiades and count how many stars are visible from your latitude; compare naked-eye, binocular, and telescopic views.

**Book.** Ian Ridpath, *Star Tales* (Lutterworth Press) — the Pleiades in Greek, Japanese, Māori, and Cherokee tradition; one of the most cross-culturally referenced star groups in the book.
