## Story

*Read one or more aloud on a winter evening. Orion is perhaps the most storied pattern in the sky — it’s visible from everywhere on Earth, which is why almost every culture named it.*

**The Greek hunter.** Orion was a giant hunter, a son of Poseidon, so tall he could walk on the sea floor. He boasted he would kill every animal on Earth. Gaia, the Earth herself, sent a scorpion to sting him. He died, and both he and the scorpion were placed in the sky — but on opposite sides, so that when Orion rises in winter, Scorpius sets, and they never meet. Watch for this through the year. It is true.

**Sah — the soul of Osiris (Egypt).** To the ancient Egyptians, the three stars of Orion’s Belt were **Sah**, the heavenly form of Osiris, god of the afterlife and rebirth. When Sirius rose just before dawn in midsummer, it signalled the Nile flood was coming — the flood that made Egypt possible. Orion and Sirius together were the calendar of a civilisation. (Some writers claim the Giza pyramids are laid out to mirror Orion’s Belt. The alignment is debated; the *idea* that the sky is a map of the divine is older and deeper than any one monument.)

**Tautoru — three in a canoe (Māori, Aotearoa New Zealand).** The three Belt stars are the bottom of a great canoe, a celestial vessel crossing the Milky Way. Its rising marks the turning of seasons.

**Al-Jauzā’ (Arab astronomy).** To medieval Arab astronomers, Orion was *Al-Jauzā’*, the Central One. Nearly every named star in Orion has an Arabic name — and in fact most named stars in our sky do. **Betelgeuse** is a contraction of *Yad al-Jauzā’*, “the hand of the Central One,” which got garbled in Latin transcription. **Rigel** is *Rijl*, “the foot.” **Mintaka** is “the belt.” **Alnilam** is “string of pearls.” **Alnitak** is “the girdle.” **Saiph** is “the sword.” When you point at Orion you are reading, in translation, a thousand-year-old Arabic map.

**Djulpan — three brothers in a canoe (Yolŋu, Arnhem Land, Australia).** The Yolŋu people of northern Australia see the Belt as three brothers fishing from their canoe; the stars of the sword are the fish. This is their story, not ours to retell as teaching. Seek out Yolŋu voices directly — the Australian Indigenous Astronomy project is a good starting point.

## Sky

Orion is the easiest bright pattern in the winter sky. Look south on any clear evening from December to March.

- **The Belt** — three bright stars in a tidy row: Alnitak, Alnilam, Mintaka.
- **The Shoulders** — Betelgeuse (red, upper left) and Bellatrix (blue-white, upper right).
- **The Feet** — Rigel (blue-white, lower right) and Saiph (lower left).
- **The Sword** — a short line of stars hanging down from the Belt. The middle “star” of the sword is actually not a star — it’s the **Orion Nebula**, a cloud of gas and dust where stars are being born *right now*. With binoculars it looks like a fuzzy smear of pale green.

**The Belt is a signpost.** Follow the Belt down and to the left and you hit **Sirius**, the brightest star in the entire night sky. Follow the Belt up and to the right and you hit **Aldebaran**, the red eye of Taurus. Keep going and you hit the **Pleiades**, a small knot of stars that most people mistake for the Little Dipper at first. This is the single most useful signpost in the winter sky.

## Science

- **Betelgeuse** is a red supergiant 500–700 times the diameter of the Sun. If you put it where our Sun is, it would swallow Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars. It will go supernova “soon” in astronomical terms — meaning sometime in the next hundred thousand years. When it does, it will briefly be visible in daylight.
- **Rigel** is a blue supergiant, younger, hotter, even more luminous.
- **The Orion Nebula** (M42) is about 1,344 light-years away — close, by cosmic standards. It’s one of the nearest stellar nurseries to Earth. The light you see left it while Charlemagne was alive.
- Orion is made mostly of very young, very hot stars. It’s a new neighbourhood.

## Skill — Navigating by Orion

- **Orion is on the celestial equator.** This means it rises close to due east and sets close to due west, from anywhere on Earth. In the middle of a clear dark field, a rising or setting Orion gives you east-or-west to within a few degrees.
- **The Belt points to Sirius (down-left) and Aldebaran (up-right).** Memorise this once and you can find a quarter of the winter sky from the Belt alone.
- **Orion is a seasonal clock.** In the Northern Hemisphere, Orion high in the south at 9pm means deep winter. Low in the west at 9pm means spring is here. Gone from the evening sky means summer. The sky rotates a full turn through the year, and Orion is the easiest hand on that clock.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Seven invitations under winter skies. Take them in the order the weather gives you. Hot drinks encouraged.*

1. **Find Orion’s Belt.** On any clear winter evening between 8pm and 11pm, slip outside and face south. See if you can pick out the three bright stars in a row. Note the date, the time, and what the weather was doing. *Grade: Starter. Unlocks the rest of this chapter’s field triggers.*
1. **Belt-to-Sirius.** On the same evening, follow the line of the Belt down-left until you land on the brightest star in the sky. That’s Sirius. Give the ancient Egyptians a wave. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Name the seven.** Over a quiet evening, see if you can learn the seven named stars of Orion (Betelgeuse, Bellatrix, Mintaka, Alnilam, Alnitak, Rigel, Saiph) and what each of those Arabic names actually means. On the next clear night, point them out to someone. *Grade: Core.*
1. **The Nebula.** With a pair of binoculars, have a careful look at the middle “star” of the sword. What do you see? Sketch it in the Fieldbook in pencil — no photos. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Read-aloud night.** Pick an Orion story and read it aloud to the family, outside, with Orion in view. Hot drinks allowed. See what the reading-out-loud does that silent reading cannot. *Grade: Core. Unlocks the Winter Capstone.*
1. **Orion-clock.** Over the course of winter, sketch Orion’s position at the same time (say, 9pm) on the first clear night of each month. Notice how it drifts westward across the weeks. You’ve just drawn a seasonal clock. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Belt direction check.** Head out just after sunset in January and find Orion in the east. See if you can use it to confirm east without a compass. Check afterwards — how close were you? *Grade: Stretch.*

## Go deeper

**The science.** NASA Hubble images of the Orion Nebula (M42) — search "Hubble Orion Nebula" for the canonical image of the stellar nursery 1,344 light-years away where star formation is happening right now; also ESA's page on Betelgeuse as a red supergiant candidate for eventual supernova.

**The cultural context.** Australian Indigenous Astronomy (aboriginalastronomy.com.au) — Ray Norris and colleagues publish Yolŋu and other First Nations astronomical traditions from those who hold them; read the Djulpan story in their own words rather than through a retelling.

**App or tool.** Stellarium (stellarium.org, free) — find Orion from any latitude and time of year; use the Belt-to-Sirius and Belt-to-Aldebaran signpost lines in the app before going outside.

**Book.** Ian Ridpath, *Star Tales* (Lutterworth Press, 2nd ed.) — global mythology of every constellation, sourced to primary texts; the Orion chapter alone covers Greek, Egyptian, Māori, and Arab traditions.
