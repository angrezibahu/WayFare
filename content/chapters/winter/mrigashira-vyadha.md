## Story

*A winter chapter, paired with Orion. In Hindustani astronomy the same stars carry living names that have been used for at least three thousand years and are still used now in calendars, almanacs and weddings. Read this with Orion in view.*

**Mṛgaśīrṣa — the deer's head.** The fifth **nakshatra** (lunar mansion) of the Hindustani sky is *Mṛgaśīrṣa*: "the deer's head." The pattern is the small triangular knot of three stars at the head of Orion — the stars we now call Meissa (λ Orionis) and the two faint companions either side of it — together with the three of the Belt, read as the body of the deer. Look at Orion in winter and the deer is plain: a delicate triangular head above a long body, the Belt a row across the deer's flank.

**Vyādha — the hunter.** Down and to the left, brightest star in the entire night sky, sits **Vyādha** — the hunter. We meet this same star in the Greek tradition as *Sirius*, the dog of Orion. In the Hindustani sky there is no dog: there is a hunter pursuing the deer. The deer flees across the sky; the hunter chases. The pattern of "Belt to Sirius" is the trajectory of the chase.

**Rohiṇī — the red one, beloved of Chandra.** Above and to the right is **Rohiṇī** — the red star we also know as Aldebaran. She is the fourth nakshatra, and the most beloved of the wives of *Chandra* (the Moon). In one telling — the one the **Aitareya Brāhmaṇa** records in its older form — *Prajāpati* (the lord of beings) pursued his own daughter Rohiṇī across the sky in the form of a stag. The gods, troubled, charged the hunter Vyādha to shoot him. The hunter's arrow — the three stars of the Belt — flew across the sky and pierced the deer. The deer's head, the arrow, the hunter, the pursued: all of Orion and a quarter of the winter sky, a single tableau of pursuit and consequence, written into the stars in the order the Vedic texts describe.

**A note on framing.** This is not "ancient mythology." The nakshatra system is the spine of the Hindu calendar today: every wedding, festival, naming ceremony, and farming almanac across South Asia uses the same 27 lunar mansions, computed daily, published in millions of copies of the *pañcāṅga* every year. When a family in Pune checks the nakshatra for an auspicious date in 2026, they are reading a sky that the Vedic poets read in 1500 BCE. The continuity is unbroken.

## Sky

The Hindustani Orion has the same stars as the Greek Orion — but the cuts of the figure are different, and that is the point of looking again.

- **Mṛgaśīrṣa (the deer's head).** Three faint stars at the very top of Orion: **Meissa** (λ Ori) at the centre, with **φ¹** and **φ²** Orionis flanking it. They make a small, delicate triangle. The medieval Arabs, like the Hindustani astronomers, focused here too — Meissa is from Arabic *al-Maysān*, "the shining one," but only after Latin transliteration scrambled it.
- **The arrow.** The three stars of the Belt — Mintaka, Alnilam, Alnitak — read here not as a belt but as **the arrow in flight**. Watch them lined up perfectly in the sky and you will see the geometry: a straight shaft, level, pointed.
- **Vyādha (the hunter).** Sirius. Down-left from the Belt by about two Belt-lengths. The brightest star in the sky, blue-white, twinkling violently when low because the atmosphere bends starlight more at low angles. The arrow points at him; he points at the deer.
- **Rohiṇī (Aldebaran).** Up-right from the Belt by about one and a half Belt-lengths. Visibly red — not as red as Betelgeuse, but unmistakably warm. The eye of the deer (in the Hindustani figure) and also the eye of Taurus the bull (in the Greek). Same star, two cuts of the sky.
- **Krittikā (the Pleiades).** Keep going up-right, past Rohiṇī, and you reach a small fuzzy knot of stars: the **Krittikā**, the third nakshatra, the six visible "cutters" or sisters who fostered Kārttikeya, the war-god. We meet them in their own chapter.

**The Belt-as-signpost works in both traditions.** Belt-down-left to Sirius. Belt-up-right to Aldebaran. Whether you read the line as Greek "the Belt points to" or Hindustani "the arrow flew through," the geometry is the same. The skies do not contradict each other; they are read.

## Science

- **The nakshatra system divides the ecliptic into 27 segments of 13°20' each**, named by the prominent star or asterism in each segment. The Moon, taking about 27.3 days to circle the sky, sits in roughly one nakshatra per night. This is a true sidereal lunar calendar — and it is older than any zodiac. The 27-fold division shows up in the *Rigveda*, with explicit star-mansion lists in the *Taittirīya Saṃhitā* (c. 1100 BCE) and the *Atharvaveda*.
- **Mṛgaśīrṣa as a nakshatra spans 23°20'–6°40' of sidereal Gemini** in the Hindustani reckoning — but the *junction star* (the bright star that marks it) is Meissa in Orion. Junction stars are the anchors of the system; everything else is computed from them.
- **Sirius, our Vyādha**, is 8.6 light-years away — the seventh-nearest stellar system to the Sun. It is a binary: bright Sirius A and a tiny white dwarf, Sirius B, discovered telescopically in 1862. The white dwarf is a dead star, an Earth-sized cinder of carbon and oxygen, the future of our own Sun.
- **Aldebaran, our Rohiṇī**, is a red giant 65 light-years away. The Moon, drifting along the ecliptic, occults Aldebaran in cycles of about 18 years. Watching Chandra eclipse Rohiṇī is a genuinely useful astronomical observation; the *Sūrya Siddhānta* (c. 4th–5th century CE) gives methods for predicting these occultations that work to within minutes.
- **The Pleiades and the precession of the equinoxes.** The Krittikā are listed *first* in the oldest nakshatra lists — placing them at the spring equinox of around 2300 BCE. By the time of the *Sūrya Siddhānta* the equinox had drifted, and modern Hindustani astronomers either correct for *ayanāṃśa* (the precessional offset) or use a different junction star. The same word, "Krittikā," names the same stars, but the calendar has been rebuilt around them as the sky has slowly turned. Astronomy in continuity with itself for three thousand years.

## Skill — Reading the Same Sky in Two Languages

- **Two cuts of one figure.** Step outside on a clear winter night with someone, find Orion, and trace it twice: once as the Greek hunter (head, shoulders, belt, sword, knees) and once as the Hindustani deer-and-arrow (small head triangle, body, arrow, hunter Vyādha at the destination). The exercise is not about choosing — it is about realising that the sky is one thing and the stories are many. Doing this once changes how you look at every constellation thereafter.
- **The nakshatra of tonight.** The Moon sits in one of the 27 lunar mansions every night. A Hindustani family checking the *pañcāṅga* knows which one without thinking. You can do the same in two minutes: an online pañcāṅga (Drik Panchang is reliable) gives you tonight's nakshatra, the deity associated with it, and the auspicious / inauspicious times. Try this for one whole lunar month — twenty-seven nights — and you will have walked the Moon through the entire system.
- **Rohiṇī as a calendar mark.** A full Moon in Rohiṇī (Aldebaran) is *Vaiśākha pūrṇimā*, a particular auspicious full moon in late spring. It happens once a year. If the festival you read about online seems "late" or "early" — it is not; it is anchored to the sky, not the Gregorian month.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations under winter skies. Take them gently — this is a chapter about reading the sky in another language, and another language goes slowly.*

1. **Find the deer's head.** On a clear winter evening with Orion in the south, find the small triangle of three stars *above* the shoulders — not the Belt, the head. That triangle is Mṛgaśīrṣa. Sketch it in the Fieldbook with the three names: Meissa, φ¹, φ². *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Belt as arrow.** Trace the three Belt stars and read them as a single straight arrow. Note where the arrow points (down-left to Sirius, up-right to Aldebaran). Sketch the arrow in flight. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Tonight's nakshatra.** Look up which nakshatra the Moon sits in tonight on a pañcāṅga website. Note the name, the deity, and one star associated with it. Step outside and find that star with binoculars or the naked eye if you can. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Three names for one star.** Take Aldebaran. Learn its three names — *Aldebaran* (Arabic, "the follower"), *Rohiṇī* (Sanskrit, "the red one"), *Tascheter* (Persian, "royal star"). Say each aloud once outside, with the star in view. *Grade: Core.*
1. **A lunar month in nakshatras.** Over one whole month, write down the nakshatra of each night the Moon is visible. Twenty-seven nights, twenty-seven mansions. By the end, the Moon will have walked the whole sky in front of you. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Rohiṇī occultation.** Once every few years the Moon passes directly in front of Aldebaran and the star blinks out. Look up the next occultation visible from your latitude (in-the-sky.org publishes them) and put the date in the Fieldbook. Some night, you will catch Chandra eclipsing Rohiṇī. *Grade: Stretch.*
