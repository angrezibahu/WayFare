## Story

*Two short stories, for a cold evening indoors before a cold walk tomorrow.*

**Skaði (Norse).** Skaði was the giantess of winter, a hunter on skis. She came down from the mountains to demand justice from the gods for her father's death, and they offered her a husband instead. She was told to pick from the gods' feet alone, behind a curtain. She chose the most beautiful pair of feet, expecting beautiful Baldr, and got old Njörd of the sea. The sea and the mountain could not live together — he could not sleep for the howling of wolves, she could not sleep for the crash of surf — so they parted. Skaði went back to her snow. This is why winter is lonely and bright and holds itself apart.

**Sápmi (referenced, not taught).** The Sámi people of northern Scandinavia have many words for snow, not because the legend is true in the way you've been told — English has plenty too, if you count *slush*, *sleet*, *powder*, *crust*, *drift*, *névé*, *firn*, *graupel* — but because snow that you must live in demands precise names. The Sámi distinguish snow by how reindeer can dig through it, by whether it will hold a sled, by whether it is safe. We are not going to list their words here. If you want to learn, read Sámi writers and researchers directly.

## Land

**Snow remakes a landscape.** Paths disappear. Fences become knee-high. A familiar wood can become unrecognisable in an afternoon. Before you walk in fresh snow, look at a map in clear weather and know your handrails — a wall, a stream, a road — because cairns and waymarks vanish.

**Snow keeps a record.** Every creature that crossed since the last fall has signed the page. A straight line of prints with no drag is a fox or a cat (they place their back foot in the print of the front). A bounding pair-pair-pair is a rabbit or squirrel. A heavy shuffle with a tail-drag is a badger. A hoof-split in two is a deer. Humans leave a trail of one boot then the other, stride-length about half your height. Look at your own prints. That is how you look to an animal.

**Ice on water is a liar.** Do not trust the edges. Ice is thinner where current runs — near inlets, outlets, banks, reeds, and under bridges. Clear blue-black ice is strongest; white opaque ice is air-trapped and weaker; grey ice is wet and dangerous. A rule from Canadian ice guides: three inches for a person, ten inches for a car, and if you're not sure, don't.

**Frost hollows.** On a clear still night, cold air pools in valleys. The lowest field can be ten degrees colder than the ridge above it. If you're planning a dawn walk, expect the valley to be frozen and the high ground to be merely cold. The white line of frost on a hill shows you the cold-air level like a tide mark.

**Black ice.** On a road or path, ice can be invisible — wet tarmac and black ice look identical under a torch. On a slope, test with a boot-heel before committing a step. On a pavement, walk on grass verges when you can. Falls on ice are how most winter injuries happen.

## Science

- Snow is mostly air. Fresh powder is roughly 90–95% air by volume, which is why an inch of rain becomes roughly ten inches of snow. The air is also why fresh snow absorbs sound — there are millions of tiny pockets to swallow echo. A wood after snowfall is genuinely quieter, not just imagined.
- Ice floats because water is one of the very few substances that *expands* when it freezes. The hydrogen bonds lock into a hexagonal lattice with gaps in it. This is the reason fish survive winter: lakes freeze from the top down, not the bottom up.
- Snowflakes are six-sided because of the same hexagonal lattice. No two are identical, but all are six-sided. If someone shows you a four- or five-pointed snowflake, they drew it wrong.
- Frost forms when water vapour goes directly from gas to solid, skipping liquid. It needs a cold surface and a humid, still night. Grass frosts before tarmac because grass loses heat faster to the sky.

## Skill — Wayfaring in cold

- **Check the forecast twice.** Met Office for the region, and a mountain-specific service (MWIS — Mountain Weather Information Service — is free and excellent) if you're going high. The temperature at a summit can be 10°C colder than in the car park, before wind chill.
- **Dress in layers.** Base, mid, shell. Cotton kills — it holds sweat against the skin. Wool and synthetics keep warmth when wet. Spare gloves and a hat in the bag, always.
- **Timings shrink.** A route that takes two hours in June can take three in snow. Darkness comes at four. Turn back when you've used half your daylight, not all of it.
- **Follow your own prints home.** If weather closes in, retrace your steps while the prints are still visible. That's your breadcrumb trail. In a whiteout, stop where you can see shelter or a known handrail rather than pushing on.
- **Tell someone where and when.** Route, expected return, backup time to raise the alarm. This is not paranoia; this is the one thing Mountain Rescue asks for every winter.

## Stretch — Challenges

1. **Frost Line.** On a clear cold morning, find the height on a hill or valley side above which there is no frost. Mark it on a sketch. You have drawn the top of last night's cold air. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Track Register.** After fresh snow, walk 200m of a local path and list every set of tracks you cross. Identify at least three. Sketch one in the Fieldbook. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Prints Home.** Walk out somewhere familiar in fresh snow, loop back by a different route, then next day walk only by following your own prints from the day before. What did you learn about your own stride? *Grade: Core.*
1. **Ice Test — with a grown-up.** On a safe, shallow, frozen puddle (not a pond), poke with a stick until it breaks. Note how thick the ice was and how hard you had to push. Never test a real pond or river. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Cold Layer Diary.** For a week in winter, record the temperature at ground level at dawn in three places — low, middle, high. Watch how the cold settles. *Grade: Stretch.*
