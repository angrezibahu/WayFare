## Story

*Three short stories, for a cold evening indoors before a cold walk tomorrow.*

**Skaði (Norse).** Skaði was the giantess of winter, a hunter on skis. She came down from the mountains to demand justice from the gods for her father's death, and they offered her a husband instead. She was told to pick from the gods' feet alone, behind a curtain. She chose the most beautiful pair of feet, expecting beautiful Baldr, and got old Njörd of the sea. The sea and the mountain could not live together — he could not sleep for the howling of wolves, she could not sleep for the crash of surf — so they parted. Skaði went back to her snow. This is why winter is lonely and bright and holds itself apart.

**Yuki-onna (Japan).** The snow-woman. She walks the high passes on nights of heavy fall, a tall pale figure in a white kimono. She is no villain — she is winter made a person. If you meet her and keep your promise, she lets you live. Break your word and the mountain keeps you. Every generation of Japanese children has been told some version of her story on the first heavy snow of the year.

**Sápmi (referenced, not taught).** The Sámi people of northern Scandinavia have many words for snow, not because the legend is true in the way you've been told — English has plenty too, if you count *slush*, *sleet*, *powder*, *crust*, *drift*, *névé*, *firn*, *graupel* — but because snow that you must live in demands precise names. The Sámi distinguish snow by how reindeer can dig through it, by whether it will hold a sled, by whether it is safe. We are not going to list their words here. If you want to learn, read Sámi writers and researchers directly.

## Land

**Snow remakes a landscape.** Paths disappear. Fences become knee-high. A familiar wood can become unrecognisable in an afternoon. Before you walk in fresh snow, look at a map in clear weather and know your handrails — a wall, a stream, a road — because cairns and waymarks vanish.

**Snow keeps a record.** Every creature that crossed since the last fall has signed the page. A straight line of prints with no drag is a fox or a cat (they place their back foot in the print of the front). A bounding pair-pair-pair is a rabbit or squirrel. A heavy shuffle with a tail-drag is a badger. A hoof-split in two is a deer. Humans leave a trail of one boot then the other, stride-length about half your height. Look at your own prints. That is how you look to an animal.

**Ice on water is a liar.** Do not trust the edges. Ice is thinner where current runs — near inlets, outlets, banks, reeds, and under bridges. Clear blue-black ice is strongest; white opaque ice is air-trapped and weaker; grey ice is wet and dangerous. A rule from Canadian ice guides: three inches for a person, ten inches for a car, and if you're not sure, don't.

**Frost hollows.** On a clear still night, cold air pools in valleys. The lowest field can be ten degrees colder than the ridge above it. If you're planning a dawn walk, expect the valley to be frozen and the high ground to be merely cold. The white line of frost on a hill shows you the cold-air level like a tide mark.

**Black ice.** On a road or path, ice can be invisible — wet tarmac and black ice look identical under a torch. On a slope, test with a boot-heel before committing a step. On a pavement, walk on grass verges when you can. Falls on ice are how most winter injuries happen.

## Historical — three ways of knowing the snow

<div class="vibe vibe-japan">

### Japan — *yukimi* and the snow-viewing almanac

In Japan, snow is a subject of study and pleasure before it is a problem. **Yukimi** — literally *snow-viewing* — is the winter cousin of *hanami* (cherry-blossom-viewing). Since the Heian period (794–1185), courtiers and later poets and farmers have kept seasonal almanacs called **saijiki**, which catalogue *kigo* — season-words — by month. Snow alone has more than thirty kigo: *hatsu-yuki* (first snow), *awa-yuki* (soft bubble-snow), *wata-yuki* (cotton-snow), *sasa-yuki* (snow clinging to bamboo leaf), *zansetsu* (lingering snow in spring), *yuki-aoi* (the hollow left under a leaf by warm breath in snow).

The snow lanterns, **yukimi-dōrō**, have a flat wide stone cap. They were designed not to melt the snow that landed on them — the snow was meant to build up and reflect a cold white light into the garden. The great **haiku** poets wrote to an instrument-less calendar: Bashō in 1689 walked north for months with his pupil Sora, and the snows of Dewa and Nikkō are fixed in the poems they wrote that winter.

**Yukimi taught a discipline that is useful to any navigator: look at the snow and name it.** Is today's snow *kata-yuki* (the hard crust you can walk on) or *shin-setsu* (the deep fresh fall that swallows your boot)? The decision to go or stay home turns on the answer.

</div>

<div class="vibe vibe-ww2">

### Cold-weather wayfaring — the Commando handbook (1941–44)

When British No. 12 (Norwegian Independent) Commando raided the **Lofoten Islands** in 1941, and when the **2nd Mountain Division** trained in the Cairngorms for Norway in 1944, the British Army had to rewrite its navigation manual for snow. The MI9 silk maps were no use if the landmarks were three metres of white.

Three Commando rules survived into post-war Mountain Leader training:

1. **Pace in snow is half pace on grass.** A route that Naismith's Rule says is four hours in summer may be eight in knee-deep snow. Turn back at a time, not at a place.
1. **Navigate by the ridge and the wind, not the path.** In whiteout, the path is gone. The ridge is still there under your feet, and the wind that sculpted the snow is still blowing from the same quarter. Keep a known wind on a known cheek, and you are holding a bearing the ground cannot hide.
1. **Read the *sastrugi*.** Sastrugi are the wind-carved ridges on a snow surface. They line up with the prevailing wind. In the Cairngorms the sastrugi point north-east (because the wind is south-westerly). Walk across them at a known angle, and they become a compass that works when your compass has frozen.

The Commandos also carried a **ski-stick compass** — a small liquid-damped compass clipped to the top of a ski-pole, always in the eye-line, always level. A hundred small innovations like that turned winter hill-walking into a trade you could be taught. Most of the UK's Mountain Rescue winter protocols come directly from this lineage.

</div>

### Modern — the cryosphere seen from space

The **cryosphere** — all the frozen water on Earth — is mapped today by satellite. **Sentinel-1** (European Space Agency) fires synthetic-aperture radar at the ground every six days; because radar cuts through cloud, it sees snow depth in winter where optical satellites see only white. **MODIS** on NASA's Terra and Aqua measures the *albedo* of snow — how much sunlight it reflects — to about 500 m resolution. **ICESat-2** fires a green laser at the ice caps and measures their height to the centimetre.

On the ground, automatic weather stations log **snow water equivalent** — the amount of liquid water a column of snow contains when melted. This is the number that matters for floods: 500 mm of snow across a valley *is* 500 mm of rain on a fortnight's delay, and the reservoirs downstream plan on knowing it.

**Avalanche forecasting** — now standard in Scotland (SAIS, the Scottish Avalanche Information Service) and in every alpine country — reads six things: new snow, wind, temperature, old-snow layering, terrain, and the human load on the slope. Check the forecast the morning of any winter hill day. It is free, written by people who have dug pits in the snow that morning, and it has turned the Cairngorms from a guessing game into a manageable risk.

## Science

- Snow is mostly air. Fresh powder is roughly 90–95% air by volume, which is why an inch of rain becomes roughly ten inches of snow. The air is also why fresh snow absorbs sound — there are millions of tiny pockets to swallow echo. A wood after snowfall is genuinely quieter, not just imagined.
- Ice floats because water is one of the very few substances that *expands* when it freezes. The hydrogen bonds lock into a hexagonal lattice with gaps in it. This is the reason fish survive winter: lakes freeze from the top down, not the bottom up.
- Snowflakes are six-sided because of the same hexagonal lattice. No two are identical, but all are six-sided. If someone shows you a four- or five-pointed snowflake, they drew it wrong.
- Frost forms when water vapour goes directly from gas to solid, skipping liquid. It needs a cold surface and a humid, still night. Grass frosts before tarmac because grass loses heat faster to the sky.

## Skill — Wayfaring in cold

- **Check the forecast twice.** Met Office for the region, and a mountain-specific service (MWIS — Mountain Weather Information Service — is free and excellent) if you're going high. The temperature at a summit can be 10°C colder than in the car park, before wind chill.
- **Dress in layers.** Base, mid, shell. Cotton kills — it holds sweat against the skin. Wool and synthetics keep warmth when wet. Spare gloves and a hat in the bag, always.
- **Timings shrink.** A route that takes two hours in June can take three in snow. Darkness comes at four. Turn back when you've used half your daylight, not all of it.
- **Follow your own prints home.** If weather closes in, retrace your steps while the prints are still visible. That's your breadcrumb trail. In a whiteout, stop where you can see shelter or a known handrail rather than pushing on.
- **Tell someone where and when.** Route, expected return, backup time to raise the alarm. This is not paranoia; this is the one thing Mountain Rescue asks for every winter.

## Try It — wayfaring from the historical pages

<div class="vibe vibe-try">

**Name the snow the *yukimi* way.** On the next snowy morning, before you touch it, sit at a window with the Fieldbook and write down three words in three languages that describe the snow *as it is today*. Is it the Scots *feefle* that swirls, the Welsh *lluwch* that drifts, the Hindustani *kohrā* that freezes in air? Is it the Japanese *awa-yuki* (bubble-soft) or *kata-yuki* (hard crust)? Only *then* go out and walk in it. You will find your eye looks harder when your tongue is ready.

**Walk by the sastrugi (after the Commandos).** On a hillside or beach or playing field after a windy snow or sand, stop and look at the surface. You will see small wind-cut ridges running in parallel. Note the direction with a phone compass. Set off across them at a right angle and keep them *under the same foot* all the way — left heel cutting across left edge of each ridge. When you return, the direction of the sastrugi should be unchanged. You have walked a bearing using nothing but the wind-carving on the ground. (In the Cairngorms, this can save your life.)

</div>

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six winter invitations. Wrap up, go slowly, and take your time — the cold rewards noticing.*

1. **Frost Line.** On a clear cold morning, walk a hill or valley side and see if you can find the height above which the frost disappears. Mark it on a sketch. You have just drawn the top of last night's pool of cold air. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Track Register.** After fresh snow, walk 200m of a local path and see how many different sets of tracks you cross. Try to name at least three, and sketch the clearest one in the Fieldbook. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Prints Home.** Walk out somewhere familiar in fresh snow, loop back by a different route, then next day see if you can retrace the whole walk by following only your own prints from the day before. What did you learn about your stride? *Grade: Core.*
1. **Ice Test — with a grown-up.** On a safe, shallow, frozen puddle (not a pond), poke with a stick until the ice breaks. See how thick it was and how hard you had to push. Never, ever test a real pond or river. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Cold Layer Diary.** For a week in winter, take the temperature at ground level at dawn in three places — low, middle, high. Watch where the cold settles, and see if it always pools in the same spot. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Snow Saijiki.** Keep a winter page in the Fieldbook with one snow-word entry for each day of snowfall this winter, each from a different tongue. *Grade: Stretch.*
