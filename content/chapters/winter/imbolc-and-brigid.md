## Story

*A late-winter chapter. Read between Candlemas and the spring equinox — early February to late March — when the days are visibly lengthening and the first green is rising. Imbolc falls on the first of February.*

**Là Fhèill Brìghde — Brigid's feast day (Gaelic Ireland and Scotland).** The Gaelic year is cut into four by **cross-quarter days** that fall halfway between the solstices and the equinoxes: **Imbolc** (1 February), **Bealtaine** (1 May), **Lughnasadh** (1 August), **Samhain** (1 November). They mark, respectively, the first stirring of spring, the start of summer, the first harvest, and the start of winter — an older calendar than the equinoctial one, anchored to the lived weather of these islands rather than the Sun's astronomical extremes.

**Imbolc** comes from Old Irish *i mbolc*, "in the belly" — the time when the ewes were heavy with the first lambs of the year. The Hebridean Gaelic name is *Là Fhèill Brìghde* — Brigid's feast day. Brigid is older than Christianity in Ireland: *Cormac's Glossary*, written about 900 CE, names her as a goddess of poets, healers, and smiths, daughter of the Dagda. The Christian *Saint* Brigid of Kildare took up the same date, the same patronage, and many of the same gestures — a tidy example of how one tradition can carry its sacred figures into the next when the new tradition is wise enough to leave them be.

**The hag and the maiden (Scottish Gaelic).** In Scottish Gaelic tradition, on Là Fhèill Brìghde the **Cailleach** — the old woman of winter, who gathered her firewood in the long darkness — must come down off the high hills and lay aside her staff. If Imbolc dawns bright and clear, the Cailleach is out gathering more firewood and winter will be long. If Imbolc dawns wild and stormy, she has gone to sleep and winter is breaking. The weather augury is upside-down from what you might guess: a cold, sunny Imbolc means cold weeks ahead. Punxsutawney got the idea from somewhere — the same logic, with a groundhog rather than a goddess.

**Carmina Gadelica.** In the late 19th century, Alexander Carmichael walked the Outer Hebrides collecting prayers, charms, and songs from old Gaelic-speaking households. He gathered, among them, blessings and incantations for Brigid's day — verses people sang in the byre with the lambing ewes, on the threshold of the house, at the well in the morning. *Carmina Gadelica* is the source: a primary, bilingual record of an oral tradition that survives because it was set down. One Imbolc charm calls Brigid in:

> *Brìghde, ban-altrum Chrìosda,*
> *Each ìm 's gach càise, gach uan 's gach laogh,*
> *Each maoin 's gach mìr, gach iasg 's gach feòil,*
> *Bi thusa, a Bhrìghde naoimh, leinn.*

> Brigid, foster-mother of Christ,
> Each butter and cheese, each lamb and calf,
> Each store and morsel, each fish and meat,
> Be thou, holy Brigid, with us.
> *— after Carmichael, Carmina Gadelica I (1900)*

You read it aloud. It is meant to be read aloud.

**A note on framing.** Imbolc is not a re-enactment. The Gaelic-speaking communities of Ireland, the Hebrides, and the western seaboard kept this date alive through famine, clearance, and language loss. There is a contemporary revival, with both Christian and pre-Christian framings, with its own teachers. Read the *Carmina Gadelica* directly; visit Kildare on Là Fhèill Brìghde if you can; and treat this as a living calendar — not a dead one — that names something the body knows in February: the year has turned.

## Land — What to Look For Outside

The first of February lies almost exactly halfway between the winter solstice and the spring equinox. By this date, in southern Britain, the Sun rises about an hour earlier than at the solstice and sets an hour later — two hours of new daylight already in the bank.

- **The Sun's rising point is moving north along the horizon, fast.** At the solstice it is rising in the southeast. By Imbolc it has moved several degrees north already. By the equinox it will rise due east. If you mark the sunrise position against a fixed horizon landmark (a tree, a chimney, a fence post) once a fortnight from January through March, you will see the Sun walking northward visibly between marks.
- **Snowdrops.** Often the first wildflower of the year in Britain. Galanthus nivalis blooms from mid-January in the south, late February in the north. Look in the dappled shade under deciduous trees, in old churchyards, along stream banks. They are an old grave-yard plant; their nodding white heads were the first sign of returning life.
- **Lambs.** The Old Irish *i mbolc* means "in the belly." By Imbolc many ewes have lambed. Watch for ewes with two-tone coats — their bellies still wet, their lambs new — in the lower fields.
- **Hazel catkins.** The little yellow tassels on bare twigs are male hazel flowers, releasing pollen on dry mild days from January onward. The tiny red female flowers, like spider-thread, hide on the same twigs. Hazel is one of the first trees to flower; it does not wait for leaves.
- **Birdsong shifts.** From around Imbolc, the dawn chorus stretches and gains. Robins and wrens add full song. Great tits start their two-note "see-saw" territorial calls. By the equinox the chorus is complex; in February it is just beginning, audible at first light.
- **First bees on warm days.** Bumblebee queens emerge from hibernation on the first sunny days above about 10°C, often well before Imbolc in mild years. They feed on willow catkins and crocuses.

## Science — Cross-Quarter Days

- **The astronomical year has four key dates** (two solstices, two equinoxes); the agricultural year of these islands has eight, with cross-quarter days inserted at the midpoints. The cross-quarters are not arbitrary: they sit close to the inflection points of daylight change, when the daily *rate* of lengthening (or shortening) days is at its peak. Around Imbolc the day is gaining roughly 2 minutes 20 seconds per day at a UK latitude — almost the fastest gain of the year. The body feels this as much as it sees it.
- **The Coligny Calendar.** A Gaulish bronze calendar from around 200 CE, found in fragments at Coligny in eastern France, sets out a 62-month luni-solar reckoning. It is the earliest material evidence of a Celtic calendar and shows that the Gauls — and likely the broader Celtic world — operated on a lunar-counted, solar-corrected year, with months named in Gaulish (some still recognisable). The cross-quarter dates aren't directly named on the Coligny tablet, but the system shows that pre-Roman Celtic peoples had a sophisticated calendar of their own. See Garrett Olmsted's reconstruction.
- **Why the dates "drift" in some sources.** Some modern Gaelic calendars place Imbolc at the *astronomical midpoint* between the solstice and the equinox — around 4 February — rather than the calendrical 1 February. Both dates have textual support; both are within the same week. Either is correct; neither is "the" date. The point is the midpoint, not the precision.
- **A note on "the wheel of the year."** The eight-fold wheel (four equinox/solstice + four cross-quarter days) as commonly drawn today is a 20th-century synthesis from older fragments, popularised in the modern Pagan revival. The four Gaelic cross-quarter days are well-attested in pre-modern Irish and Scottish sources; the four sun-festivals were observed across Europe in many forms; whether they were *combined* into a unified eight-fold scheme before the 20th century is uncertain. We name what we know and don't fill the gaps.

## Skill — Walking the Year by Halves and Quarters

- **The eight-day calendar of these islands.** Solstice (21 Dec) → Imbolc (1 Feb) → Equinox (20 Mar) → Bealtaine (1 May) → Solstice (21 Jun) → Lughnasadh (1 Aug) → Equinox (22 Sep) → Samhain (1 Nov). Eight roughly equal slices of the year, each marking a real shift in the weather and the light. Pencil all eight onto the Year Map page of your Fieldbook. You'll have a calendar that names what you can see without a phone.
- **Sunrise as horizon clock.** Pick a sunrise viewpoint with a clear, distant horizon — a hilltop, a beach, an east-facing window with a long view. Mark the Sun's rising point against a fixed landmark once a fortnight from December through June. By June you will have a horizon-arc that *is* the year, and you'll know which fortnight it is from the Sun's position alone.
- **Auguring weather from the day.** The Cailleach's rule: a fine, bright Imbolc means more winter to come; a wet, wild Imbolc means winter is breaking. The science behind it is real — high-pressure cold-clear systems often persist; the breakdown of those systems brings warm, wet, stormy weather as Atlantic fronts return. Logging your Imbolc weather year on year, and what followed, is one of the best long-running Fieldbook entries you can keep.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations to walk into the year's first quarter. The light is winning.*

1. **First snowdrop.** Between mid-January and Imbolc, find and sketch the first snowdrop of the year. Note where, when, and how cold it was. *Grade: Starter. Counts toward the Imbolc field trigger.*
1. **Sunrise mark.** Pick an east-facing viewpoint and mark the Sun's rising position on the morning closest to Imbolc that gives you a clear horizon. Mark it again at the equinox. Note the distance the Sun has walked along the horizon between the two marks. *Grade: Core.*
1. **A Brigid blessing read aloud.** Find a Carmina Gadelica blessing for Brigid's day (an copy lives on archive.org). Read it aloud — once in Gaelic if you're brave, once in English — at the kitchen door on the morning of 1 February. *Grade: Core.*
1. **The eight-day wheel.** On the Year Map page of your Fieldbook, mark the eight calendar dates (solstices, equinoxes, cross-quarters). Add a note for each as the year reaches it: weather, what was flowering, what was lambing, who was singing. *Grade: Core.*
1. **The Cailleach's augury.** On Imbolc morning, write down the weather. At the spring equinox, write down whether the seven weeks between were "more winter" or "winter breaking." Repeat next year, and the year after. *Grade: Stretch — a multi-year entry.*
1. **Hazel-catkin watch.** From early January, walk past the same hazel tree weekly until it flowers. Note the date the catkins start releasing pollen on a dry day (tap a twig over a dark cloth — the yellow dust is unmistakable). This date moves earlier in mild years and later in cold ones; it is a personal climate record. *Grade: Stretch.*
