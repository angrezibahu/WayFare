## Story

*Two pieces, for a windy evening.*

**The Shipping Forecast.** Every night at 00:48, Radio 4 broadcasts a list of sea areas — *Viking, North Utsire, South Utsire, Forties, Cromarty, Forth* — with wind, weather and visibility for each. It has been broadcast, in some form, since 1861, when a storm off Anglesey killed 450 people and Admiral FitzRoy, the first Chief Meteorologist, was told to do something. Generations of British families have fallen asleep to *Dogger, Fisher, German Bight*. The forecast is a prayer and a practical document at the same time. If you listen tonight, you are part of an unbroken line of people listening for a storm they cannot see.

**Named winds.** Great winds have their own names. The **Mistral** tears down the Rhône valley from the Alps to the Mediterranean, cold and dry, bending the cypresses into permanent lean. The **Harmattan** blows Saharan dust south across West Africa from November to March. The **Sirocco** comes the other way — hot and wet, from North Africa across Italy. The **Loo** is the north-Indian furnace wind that kills in May and June, dry and dust-laden off the Thar. The **Föhn** drops warm dry air down the lee side of the Alps and, local legend says, makes everybody cross. England has the softer **Helm Wind**, which howls down the western side of the Cross Fell scarp in Cumbria and is the only named wind in Britain. To name a wind is to live with it long enough to know its character.

## Land

**Wind tells you where the weather is coming from.** In Britain, weather mostly arrives from the west — the Atlantic lies that way, and our prevailing wind is the south-westerly. That is why exposed trees lean north-east. That is why the west coast gets the rain and the east coast gets the cold. Knowing your prevailing wind is knowing which shoulder the rain will hit you on.

**Wind changes with height.** The wind you feel on the ground is slowed by friction — hedges, buildings, your own body. Fifty metres up, it's faster and often in a different direction. Watch the clouds and the treetops, not the grass. If the clouds are moving one way and the surface wind another, weather is changing.

**Reading the trees.**
- *Flagged* trees — branches almost all on one side — grow where the wind is so strong and steady that growth on the windward side dies back. You see this on sea cliffs and high ridges.
- *Wedge-shaped* canopies — tapering into the wind — are the same story, softer.
- A single tree in a field can be a compass. A hedge cannot — it's been cut.

**The clouds are a clock.** High thin cirrus (the "mare's tails") often arrives a day ahead of a front. Mid-level altostratus thickens the sky grey. Low nimbostratus is the rain itself. In that order — thin, thick, raining — you have watched a warm front cross you, and you know roughly how long until the weather changes again.

**Rhymes that are actually true.**
- *"Red sky at night, shepherd's delight. Red sky in the morning, shepherd's warning."* In mid-latitudes weather moves west to east. A red evening sky means clear air to the west — fair weather coming. A red morning sky means clear air to the east — the good weather has just passed, rain is coming.
- *"Mackerel sky and mare's tails make tall ships take in their sails."* Cirrocumulus (mackerel) and cirrus (mare's tails) together often mean a front within 24 hours.
- *"When the wind is in the east, 'tis neither good for man nor beast."* In Britain an easterly brings continental air — cold in winter, dry in summer, stale any time, because it has travelled from land not sea.

## Historical — three ways of knowing the wind

<div class="vibe vibe-greece">

### Ancient Greece — the Tower of the Winds

In the first century BC, the astronomer **Andronicus of Cyrrhus** built an octagonal marble tower in the Roman Agora of Athens. It is still standing. On each of its eight faces a god is carved in flight, each one a wind: **Boreas** (the cold north wind, in boots and a heavy cloak), **Notos** (the humid south, pouring water from an urn), **Zephyros** (the west, scattering flowers), **Euros** (the east, unkempt and surly) — and the four half-winds between them. Above each god was a sundial; inside, a water clock; on the roof, a bronze Triton on a spindle that turned in the wind and pointed a wand at the reigning god.

The Tower was a public instrument. Any Athenian could walk up and read the wind, the hour by shadow, and the hour by water all at once. It is the oldest surviving **weather station**, and it encoded a belief the Greeks held seriously: that each wind had a character, a province, a temperament — and that knowing which of the eight was blowing told you more than a direction.

Aristotle, a few centuries earlier, had written the *Meteorologica* — the first Western book to try to explain weather without gods. He was wrong about many things (he thought wind was "dry exhalation from the earth") but he gave us the *word* and the *project*: meteor-ology, the study of what hangs in the middle air.

</div>

### The Met Flight — pre-radar upper-air winds (1941–)

Before satellites, before weather balloons carried radio, the only way to know the wind at ten thousand feet was to send someone up to fly in it. The **RAF Meteorological Flight**, based at Duxford and then Bircham Newton, flew modified Gloster Gladiators and later Mosquitoes every day of the war, in every weather, up to 30,000 feet. The observer, in an open or unpressurised cockpit, wrote down temperature, humidity and wind drift at fixed altitudes, then brought the paper back to the Met Office.

Wind drift was measured by a technique called the **triangle of velocities**. The pilot flew a known heading at a known airspeed. The navigator, watching a mark on the ground, read the *actual* track the aircraft made. The difference between where the aircraft was pointed and where it was going *is* the wind. Draw it as a triangle on the chartboard and you have the wind speed and direction at altitude. No instruments needed except a compass, a clock, and honest eyes.

These flights fed the forecasts for Bomber Command, for the D-Day window (the famous **Stagg forecast** of 5 June 1944 that Eisenhower trusted), and for every convoy across the Atlantic. When you see a jet contrail arrowing across a blue sky today, remember that the wind at that altitude was first measured by a man in a sheepskin jacket, with a wax pencil and a chart-board, freezing.

</div>

### Modern — radiosondes, satellites and numerical weather prediction

Twice a day, at 00:00 and 12:00 GMT, hundreds of weather stations around the world release a **radiosonde** — a small instrument package under a latex balloon. It climbs to about 30 km, transmitting temperature, humidity, pressure and (from its GPS drift) wind, once every second. When the balloon bursts, the package falls on a parachute, and if you find one in a field, there is usually a freepost address to return it.

**Geostationary satellites** (36,000 km up, one a third of the way round the world from the next) watch cloud motion continuously; their frame-by-frame tracking gives winds aloft where no balloon flies. Low polar-orbiters carry microwave instruments that read temperature and humidity through cloud.

All of this pours into a **numerical weather prediction model** — at the Met Office, the *Unified Model* — which solves the fluid-dynamics equations of the atmosphere on a grid of cells about 1.5 km across over the UK. Every small improvement in the model has come from a small improvement in the measurements: better balloons, more satellites, more aircraft sending automatic reports from their pitot tubes. The forecast you read on your phone tomorrow morning is the direct descendant of the Met Flight's chartboard.

</div>

## Science

- Wind is air flowing from high pressure to low pressure, deflected by the Earth's rotation (the Coriolis effect). In the Northern Hemisphere, the deflection is to the right, which is why a depression rotates anticlockwise over us and a high rotates clockwise.
- **Buys Ballot's Law.** Stand with your back to the wind. In the Northern Hemisphere, low pressure is on your left. This is a real, quick rule used by generations of sailors. In the Southern Hemisphere, the low is on your right.
- The **Beaufort Scale** was written by Admiral Francis Beaufort in 1805 so that naval logs would be comparable. It goes from 0 (calm, smoke rises straight) to 12 (hurricane, trees uprooted, visibility nil). You can estimate wind strength to one Beaufort number just by looking at leaves, smoke, and flags. The scale was designed to be read without instruments.
- Gusts on land are typically 1.4× the mean wind. A forecast of "25 mph" usually means gusts around 35 mph.

## Skill — Reading weather in the field

- **Face the wind every half hour.** On a walk, stop and feel where the wind is coming from. If it has backed (shifted anticlockwise) a full sector — south-west to south, say — weather is usually getting worse. If it has veered (shifted clockwise) — south-west to west — a front has passed and things are usually improving.
- **Pressure tells fast.** A barometer that is dropping fast means bad weather soon. Even without an instrument, you can feel it — older people's joints ache, cows sit down in the field, swifts fly low to catch flies driven down by humidity.
- **Smoke shows.** Every chimney is a wind vane. Watch the smoke from three chimneys and you see the wind direction, the wind speed, and — if the smoke drops flat instead of rising — a likely temperature inversion, which is often foggy or still.
- **When a squall is coming.** The air goes oddly still first. Then the tops of the trees start to thrash before anything reaches you at ground level. Then a cold gust, then rain. You have roughly two minutes from the tree-thrash to decide where to shelter.
- **Lightning.** If you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck. The *five-second rule* — count seconds between flash and thunder, divide by five for miles — is real. Closer than thirty seconds means take shelter now. Indoors is best; crouched low in a ditch away from tall trees, second best.

## Try It — wayfaring from the historical pages

<div class="vibe vibe-try">

**Build a garden Tower of the Winds (after Andronicus).** Draw an octagon on an A4 page. Put **N** at the top and fill in the eight Greek wind-gods in order clockwise: Boreas (N), Kaikias (NE), Apeliotes (E), Euros (SE), Notos (S), Lips (SW), Zephyros (W), Skiron (NW). For two weeks, at the same time each morning and evening, take the page outside and mark which wind is blowing. At the end of two weeks, which god has the most marks? That god is your local prevailing wind. (Compare with the UK Wind Atlas at the end — you will usually find Zephyros or Lips winning, because Britain is a westerly country.)

**Fly a paper triangle of velocities (after the Met Flight).** On a day with any steady wind, go out with a roll of crepe paper or a bubble wand and a phone compass. Walk a straight line for 30 paces on a *fixed bearing* — say, due north. Release a small bubble or a streamer at the start, watch where it finishes after you have walked. Draw the triangle: (1) the way you walked, (2) where the bubble actually ended, (3) the difference. The third side is the wind. You have done what the navigator in the Mosquito cockpit did, with a compass and a streamer instead of an airframe and a chart-board.

</div>

## Stretch — Challenges

*Seven invitations to read the wind. Autumn is the best teacher — it brings all the weathers through.*

1. **Shipping Forecast Night.** As a family, listen to the late-night Shipping Forecast together, once. Pick out three sea areas you haven't heard before. In the morning, find them on a map. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Prevailing Wind Map.** For one week in autumn, note the wind direction twice a day — from smoke, flags, leaves, or the feel on your cheek. At week's end, decide for yourself: what is the prevailing wind where you live? Then see if a UK wind atlas agrees. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Beaufort Walk.** On a windy walk, see if you can estimate the Beaufort number by sight alone every fifteen minutes — from trees, smoke, and what things are doing. Keep the log in the Fieldbook. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Cirrus to Rain.** Next time you see cirrus (mare's tails) early in the day, make a prediction: will rain arrive within 24 hours? Write it down. Check tomorrow. Keep scoring yourself over the season. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Buys Ballot in the Garden.** Stand with your back to the wind. Which way, then, is low pressure? Check against a weather chart or app and see how you did. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Name a Local Wind.** Ask three older neighbours or family members: do they know a name for the wind that comes from *that* direction here? Write down what they say. Some of those names are the oldest words still in use in your village. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Tower of the Winds, Two Weeks.** Keep the octagon log above. Name your own prevailing wind in your own family language. *Grade: Stretch.*
