## Story

*An autumn chapter, paired with the wind and weather chapter. The Norse sailed in some of the cloudiest, foggiest skies on Earth — and they found their way. The chief instrument they may have used was a stone. Read this on a grey October day with no sun visible.*

**The problem.** From around 800 to 1100 CE, Norse seafarers crossed the North Atlantic in open longships: from Norway to Iceland (about 1,000 km), from Iceland to Greenland (1,500 km), from Greenland to *Vinland* — the eastern coast of North America — at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland (a further 2,000 km). They carried trade goods, livestock, and families. They did this without compasses, without sextants, without charts, and in a North Atlantic that is overcast or fog-bound a good half of the year. The night sky, when visible, was useful: Polaris stood about 60° high; the Milky Way ran familiar paths. But on cloud-covered days at sea — many days — the sun was invisible, the stars were not yet up, and there was no reliable horizon. How did they steer?

**The saga reference.** The Old Norse word **sólarsteinn** — "sun stone" — is named in three medieval Icelandic texts, all preserved in 13th–14th century manuscripts but referring to events of the 11th and 12th centuries.

The longest reference is in *Hrafns saga Sveinbjarnarsonar* (chapter 17), describing Saint Olaf's voyage in winter weather:

> *Þá var veðr þykkt ok drífandi, sem Sigurðr hafði sagt. Síðan lét konungr kalla á sik Sigurð ok Dag. Lét konungr þá sjá út, ok sá hvergi himin skíran. Þá bað hann Sigurð segja, hvar sól myndi þá komin. Hann kvað skýrt á. Þá lét konungr taka sólarsteininn, ok hélt upp, ok sá hann, hvar geislaði ór steininum, ok markaði svá beint til sólar.*

> The weather was thick and snowing, as Sigurd had said. The king then called Sigurd and Dag to him. The king let them look out, and they could see no clear sky. Then he told Sigurd to say where the sun would be. Sigurd answered. The king then had the sun stone brought, and held it up, and he saw where light was radiating from the stone, and from this he could see directly to the sun.
> *— Hrafns saga Sveinbjarnarsonar, ch. 17, my translation*

Two more references occur: a brief one in **Heimskringla** (the *Saga of Olaf the Holy*) and one in **Rauðúlfs þáttr**. None of the three describes the stone in detail. There is no surviving artefact unambiguously identified as a sólarsteinn from a Viking-age archaeological context.

**The science catches up.** For most of the 20th century, the sólarsteinn was treated as a literary device — perhaps a reference to a magic charm, perhaps a misremembered tool. In 1967 the Danish archaeologist **Thorkild Ramskou** suggested that the sólarsteinn could have been a crystal of **Iceland spar** (a transparent variety of calcite) used as a polariser. Calcite has a property called *birefringence*: it splits a single beam of light into two beams polarised at right angles to each other. If you hold a calcite crystal up to the sky and rotate it, the relative brightness of the two beams changes depending on the polarisation of the sky-light coming through. The sky's polarisation pattern, in turn, depends on the direction of the sun — even when the sun itself is hidden behind cloud or below the horizon. By rotating the crystal until the two beams match in brightness (a technique well-known in 19th- and 20th-century optics), one can determine the direction of the sun to within a degree or two, even on a heavily overcast day.

**Ramón Hegedüs and colleagues (2007)** tested this hypothesis at sea on Arctic voyages, in real Norse-Atlantic conditions. They found that calcite crystals can indeed locate the sun under partial overcast and even under thick haze, with practical accuracy. **Guy Ropars and colleagues (2014)** then reported on a calcite crystal recovered from the 1592 wreck of the **Alderney**, an Elizabethan English warship — five centuries after the Norse — and showed it had the right optical properties to be a sun-finding tool. The use of calcite as a backup to magnetic compasses persisted into the early modern period.

The literary record is real. The physics is real. Whether the actual Norse navigators used a sólarsteinn routinely, occasionally, or only ceremonially is not settled. But the sense of "we cannot have done this; therefore the sagas exaggerate" is gone. The Norse may have read the sun through cloud with a stone, and the stone may have been Iceland spar — a mineral common in their territory, mined at Helgustadir on Iceland's east coast since the Middle Ages.

**A note on framing.** The Norse seafaring tradition is well-documented in Old Norse-Icelandic sagas, in Old Norwegian didactic writing (*Konungs skuggsjá*), and in archaeology (the L'Anse aux Meadows site, the Skuldelev ship excavations, the Oseberg ship). It is a literary culture, not a covert one — *Heimskringla* is a foundational text in European medieval literature, freely available, much-read. We can describe it directly. We do not need the framing of "referenced not taught" because there is no living-tradition keeper of secret knowledge to defer to: the sagas are public; modern Iceland teaches them in school; the techniques are reconstructible by anyone who reads the texts.

## Land — Reading the Sun Without Seeing It

Even without a calcite crystal, you can read the sun through cloud — to less precision but with real accuracy — by techniques the Norse certainly used.

- **Brightness gradient.** On an overcast day, walk slowly in a circle outside, looking up. The sky is brighter in one direction than the others. The brightest patch is where the sun is. Try this. From your back garden on the next grey day, you can pick the sun's compass bearing to within about 30°, sometimes better.
- **Cloud shadow direction.** When clouds are not uniform, you can sometimes see *cloud shadows* — patches where the lower cloud is in the shadow of an upper cloud. The shadows fall away from the sun. The geometry takes a few minutes to read; once you have it, the sun's direction is unmistakable.
- **A stick on a piece of paper.** On a grey day with diffuse light, set a thin stick (a pencil) vertically on a sheet of white paper outdoors. Even on a "shadowless" overcast day, you can usually see a faint shadow if you look carefully. The faint shadow points away from the sun.
- **A polariser.** The cleanest modern proxy for a sólarsteinn. A polarised sunglass lens, held up to the sky and rotated, brightens and darkens depending on its angle to the sky's polarisation pattern. The maximum-darkening angle is at right angles to the sun. Try this on the next clear day; once you can do it under a clear sky, try it under cloud — the polarisation pattern persists through thin and moderate cloud.

**The Norse latitude method.** The Norse used direct sun-altitude measurement at noon to estimate latitude. A simple method, attested in *Konungs skuggsjá*: hold a knotted cord at arm's length, with the lower end touching the horizon and the upper end touching the sun's centre at noon. The number of knots the cord rises or falls between voyages tells you the latitude difference. Combined with east-west bearings (magnetic compasses arrived in Iceland by the 13th century but the Norse seafaring system predates them), this is enough to get from Norway to Iceland reliably, season after season. The sun stone was the cloud-day backup; the cord was the clear-day workhorse.

## Science — Polarisation, Birefringence, and Why a Stone Can Find the Sun

- **Sky polarisation.** Sunlight scattered by the atmosphere is partially **polarised** — its electric field oscillates preferentially in one direction. The pattern of polarisation across the sky forms a fixed geometry centred on the sun: at 90° from the sun, the polarisation is at maximum; at the sun itself and directly opposite, it is zero. The pattern persists, somewhat weakened, through cloud and fog. Bees, ants, octopuses, and migratory birds all use sky polarisation for navigation; humans do not naturally see it but can measure it with the right tool.
- **Calcite (Iceland spar) as a polariser.** A clear calcite crystal exhibits **birefringence**: a single beam of light entering it splits into two beams polarised at right angles to each other and travelling slightly different paths. If you hold a calcite crystal over a dot on a page, you see two dots. Rotate the crystal: as the polarisation alignment shifts, the relative brightness of the two dots changes. By rotating until the two dots match — a moment of equal brightness — you can determine the polarisation direction of the incoming light to within a few degrees. From the polarisation, the geometry of the sky pattern, and the relative position of the sun: you can read the sun's direction even when it is hidden.
- **Iceland spar in particular.** This is the gem-quality variety of calcite, mined at the Helgustadir mine on Iceland's east coast (the world's only significant deposit until the 17th century). Crystals are clear, easily cleaved into rhombs, and reach impressive sizes. The Norse had ready access to it; the geographical match to the saga claim is striking.
- **The Hegedüs and Ropars experiments.** Hegedüs *et al.* (2007) showed that under realistic Arctic cloud conditions, a calcite crystal can locate the sun within a few degrees on at least 75% of overcast days. Ropars *et al.* (2014) measured the optical properties of the Alderney calcite and confirmed it would have served as a precise sun-finder. The papers are open-access (links in the source list above) and worth reading; the science is direct.
- **What the sagas don't say.** The sagas don't describe how the stone was used in detail. The 2007 and 2014 reconstructions are *plausible* methods, not attested ones. We know the saga reference exists; we know the physics works; we don't know exactly how a Norse navigator handled the stone day-to-day. The honesty in this chapter is in being clear about that gap.

## Skill — Finding the Sun Through Cloud

- **Brightness scan.** On an overcast day, stand in your garden and slowly turn through 360°, looking up at about 30° altitude. Note where the sky is brightest. That direction is roughly the sun. Verify with a compass and a sun-time calculator (timeanddate.com) for your location and time. With practice, you can get this to within 20° on most overcast days.
- **Polariser test.** Take a polarised sunglass lens (or any polariser — a piece of polarised film from a camera shop). On a clear day, hold it up to the sky 90° from the sun. Rotate it. Watch the sky brighten and darken. You are seeing the polarisation pattern of the atmosphere. Repeat on a cloudy day; the effect is weaker but still present.
- **The cord-and-knot latitude.** Make a 30 cm length of cord with knots tied every 2.5 cm. At local noon (check timeanddate.com for *solar* noon at your location), hold the cord at arm's length with the lower end on the horizon and the upper end on the sun. Count the knots. Repeat in the same place a week later. The number of knots will have changed slightly as the sun's noon altitude moves with the season. Over a year, you have measured Earth's axial tilt with a piece of string.
- **A homemade sólarsteinn.** A clear calcite crystal large enough to use as a sun-finder costs about £15 from a mineralogy shop. Hold it up on a cloudy day. Find the doubled image of a small dot or text under the crystal. Rotate the crystal until the two images are equally bright. The orientation of the crystal at the moment of equal brightness is at right angles to the sun's azimuth. Try it; once you have the technique you have an instrument the Norse may have carried.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations to find the sun without seeing it. Most days in October are good for this.*

1. **Brightness scan in cloud.** On the next overcast day, stand outside for a minute and identify the brightest patch of sky. Note your guess for the sun's compass bearing. Verify with a compass and timeanddate.com. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Polariser at right angles.** On the next clear day, find a polarised sunglass lens and rotate it 90° from the sun. Note the sky brightening and darkening as you rotate. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Cord-and-knot latitude.** Make the knotted cord and measure the sun's noon altitude on three different dates a month apart. Note how it changes. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Read the saga passage aloud.** Find chapter 17 of Hrafns saga Sveinbjarnarsonar online (heimskringla.no has the Old Norse and modern Icelandic; English translations are in print but harder to find online). Read it aloud — once in Old Norse / Icelandic if you're brave, once in modern translation. *Grade: Core.*
1. **A calcite crystal sun-find.** Buy or borrow a clear calcite (Iceland spar) crystal. On the next cloudy day, find the doubled image of a dot beneath the crystal. Rotate until the brightnesses match. Note the direction the crystal is oriented in at that moment, and check it against the actual sun direction (timeanddate.com again). *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Read one of the scientific papers.** Hegedüs *et al.* 2007 or Ropars *et al.* 2014 — both open access on royalsocietypublishing.org. They are dense but readable. The 2007 paper is the better starting point. *Grade: Stretch.*
