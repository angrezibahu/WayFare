## Story

*Read on an autumn evening in October or November, when the Great Square is high in the south after dark.*

**The rescue (Greek).** The autumn sky tells a single connected story — one of the only places the Greek myths sit as a continuous family around the pole. Queen **Cassiopeia** boasted that her daughter **Andromeda** was more beautiful than the sea-nymphs. The sea-god Poseidon sent a monster, Cetus, to ravage the coast. An oracle told Cassiopeia’s husband, King **Cepheus**, that the only way to save the kingdom was to chain Andromeda to a rock and offer her to the monster. He did it. The hero **Perseus** — flying home on winged sandals with the head of Medusa in a bag — saw her there, killed the monster (some versions say with the Medusa-head, turning Cetus to stone), freed Andromeda, and married her. The whole cast was placed in the sky together: Cepheus, Cassiopeia, Andromeda, Perseus, Cetus, and **Pegasus**, the winged horse that sprang from Medusa’s blood. In autumn they wheel overhead as a family portrait. Find one and you can walk to them all.

**The Great Square (across cultures).** Long before the Greek story, the square was a signpost. Persian and Babylonian astronomers used the four corners of Pegasus — *Markab* ("the saddle"), *Scheat* ("the shin"), *Algenib* ("the flank"), and *Alpheratz* ("the navel of the horse") — as a seasonal marker and as a station of the lunar month. In the Arab lunar mansion system, two of the square’s corners mark the 26th and 27th **manzil**, stations the Moon passes through on its monthly circuit of the sky. When you see the Great Square overhead, you’re looking at one of the oldest named asterisms on Earth.

**A note on the name Alpheratz.** The star at the upper-left corner of the Great Square is shared: it is both the nose of Pegasus and the head of Andromeda. Older Arab astronomy called it *al-Surrat al-Faras* (the horse’s navel); later it was reassigned to Andromeda as *al-Ras al-Mar’a al-Musalsala* — "the head of the chained woman." Two cultures, one star, two jobs. The sky does not mind.

## Sky

The Great Square of Pegasus is a giant, nearly empty square of sky — four bright-ish stars at the corners, very little inside. Once you spot the emptiness, you cannot unsee it.

- **Four corners.** Three of them are Pegasus (Markab, Scheat, Algenib). The upper-left corner is **Alpheratz**, technically the head of **Andromeda**.
- **Inside the square.** Almost nothing. On a city night you may see no stars at all inside it. On a dark-sky night you might count half a dozen faint ones. The emptiness is the signpost.
- **Out of Alpheratz, two streams of stars flow northeast** — the body of Andromeda. Follow the brighter stream (Mirach, Almach) out two stars from Alpheratz, then turn left up a shorter stream of two fainter stars. At the end, in the darkness, is a small hazy patch. That patch is the **Andromeda Galaxy** — another island of a trillion stars, the furthest thing most humans will ever see with the naked eye.

**How to find the square.**

- **From Cassiopeia.** Find the W of Cassiopeia high in the north. The deeper V of the W points roughly down towards Pegasus.
- **From the Plough (autumn).** In autumn, the Plough is low in the north and rotated. A line from the *handle* of the Plough across the pole and on down the other side lands you in the neighbourhood of the Great Square.
- **By looking for the empty square.** Honestly — it’s a square of nothing in the middle of a busy sky. Look south from 9pm in October and you’ll find it.

**Perseus hangs to the east** — a crooked line of bright stars including Mirfak. Between Perseus and Cassiopeia, a pair of faint patches appear: the **Double Cluster** — two open clusters of young stars, a short, spectacular detour for binoculars.

## Science

- **The Andromeda Galaxy (M31)** is 2.5 million light-years away. The photons hitting your retina tonight left that galaxy while our ancestors were learning to walk upright. It is the single most distant thing that ordinary human eyes can detect, and it is visible from any sufficiently dark field in Britain.
- **Andromeda and our Milky Way are on a collision course.** In about 4.5 billion years, the two galaxies will merge. Stars will not collide (space between them is vast); the combined galaxy will settle into a giant elliptical. Nobody reading this will be around to see it.
- **Pegasus 51** — a yellow star in the west of Pegasus — was the first Sun-like star found to have a planet orbiting it (1995). That planet, **51 Pegasi b**, kicked off the exoplanet revolution. There are thousands of known exoplanets now; the first was in Pegasus.
- **Alpheratz** is a chemically peculiar binary — its atmosphere is rich in mercury and manganese, elements almost absent from the Sun.
- The star **Mirach** (middle of the Andromeda chain) sits near a small faint galaxy, *Mirach’s Ghost* — a handy accident of foreground and background.

## Skill — Finding Andromeda, Reading Autumn

- **The Square is a seasonal clock.** In the Northern Hemisphere, the Great Square rising in the east at sunset means September. Straight overhead at 9pm means late October. Setting in the west after midnight means December.
- **Finding Andromeda by naked eye** is the classic autumn rite. From a rural site, lie back, find the square, find the two chains of Andromeda, and let your eyes adjust for ten minutes. Look slightly to one side of where you think the galaxy is — the eye’s peripheral vision is more sensitive than its centre. The hazy patch will appear. The Autumn Capstone in the Fieldbook is built around this moment.
- **Cassiopeia and the Plough as twin anchors.** In autumn, when the Plough is low, Cassiopeia is high. In spring, vice versa. The two asterisms sit opposite each other across the pole and take turns being the reliable northern marker. Between them — always — is **Polaris**. Learn both shapes and you have a pole-finder every night of the year.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations under autumn skies. One of them lets you look further than any other challenge in this book.*

1. **Find the Great Square.** On any clear autumn evening, see if you can find the big empty square south of overhead. Note the date, the time, and one thing that surprises you about it. *Grade: Starter. Unlocks the rest of this chapter’s field triggers.*
1. **Walk the chain.** From Alpheratz, trace the two streams of stars that make Andromeda. Sketch the shape in the Fieldbook. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Name the four corners.** See if you can learn Markab, Scheat, Algenib, Alpheratz — which is which, and what each Arabic root is saying. *Grade: Core.*
1. **The Andromeda Galaxy.** On the darkest night you can reach, see if you can find M31 with the naked eye. Hold it in averted vision for thirty seconds and describe what you see. Binoculars are fine if the sky defeats you. (This is the furthest thing your eye can see without a lens — 2.5 million light years.) *Grade: Core. Counts towards the Autumn Capstone.*
1. **The Double Cluster.** With binoculars, find the pair of fuzzy patches between Cassiopeia and Perseus. See how many stars you can resolve in each. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Autumn clock.** Over three months, sketch the Great Square at the same time (say 9pm) on the first clear night of each month. Watch it drift from the east sky through the zenith and down into the west. *Grade: Stretch.*
