## Story

*An autumn chapter and a companion to Pegasus and Andromeda. Read on a clear autumn evening with the W high in the north.*

**The vain queen (Greek).** **Cassiopeia** was the queen of Ethiopia, wife of King Cepheus, mother of Andromeda. She boasted that her daughter — some versions say she herself — was more beautiful than the Nereids, the sea-nymphs. Poseidon took offence and sent the monster Cetus to ravage the coast; an oracle required Andromeda to be chained to a rock as sacrifice. Perseus arrived in time, killed the monster, rescued Andromeda. After her death Cassiopeia was placed among the stars — but as punishment for her pride, her chair was fixed upside-down for half the year, so that she spends part of every night hanging by her legs with her skirts over her head. In Britain this is true: look at the W in spring evenings and it is, indeed, an M flipped upside-down.

**The autumn family.** Cassiopeia is the centre of the autumn family portrait: her husband **Cepheus** above her, her daughter **Andromeda** to her south, her son-in-law **Perseus** to the east, the sea-monster **Cetus** below Andromeda, and the winged horse **Pegasus** next to them all. Find one star in this family and you can walk to the others. It is the single most connected story-region in the northern sky.

**The Arab names.** Like every bright star pattern, the W has Arabic names for its stars. **Schedar** (*al-Ṣadr*, "the breast"). **Caph** (*Kaff*, "the palm of the hand"). **Ruchbah** (*al-Rukbah*, "the knee"). **Segin** (etymology less certain, probably a corruption). The W was not a W to medieval Arab astronomers — they divided the pattern differently — but the star names themselves came down through Latin manuscripts and still fit the Greek queen they now label.

**Pituaq — the lamp stand (Inuit, Arctic — referenced).** Some Inuit traditions see in the W a **lamp stand**: the *qulliq*, the stone oil lamp at the heart of every traditional home. The lamp at the centre of the stand is the star we now call Segin. This is a living tradition with its own keepers. Seek it out from Inuit writers and storytellers directly.

## Sky

Cassiopeia is a loose W (or M, depending on the season) of five bright stars high in the northern sky. Like the Plough, it is *circumpolar* from Britain — never sets, always findable, rotates around Polaris through the year.

- **The W.** Five stars in a zigzag: Caph, Schedar, Gamma Cas, Ruchbah, Segin. Trace them in order and you have the W.
- **The deep V of the W points at Polaris.** Well — not quite, but close enough for a rough check. Draw a perpendicular line from the middle star (Gamma Cas) through the V and you'll pass near Polaris. The Plough's pointer stars are more precise, but on a night where the Plough is hidden behind trees, the W will get you to north.
- **The shallow V points at Pegasus.** The opening side of the W — the one that doesn't point at Polaris — opens toward the Great Square of Pegasus, which is high in the south in autumn. The W is a double signpost: one V to north, the other to the autumn family.
- **Through the W, the Milky Way runs.** On a dark night, Cassiopeia sits directly in the band of the Milky Way. The region is rich with star clusters — small fuzzy patches in binoculars that resolve into jewelled groups of young stars. The two most famous pairs are the **Double Cluster** (technically in Perseus, just east of Cassiopeia — NGC 869 and NGC 884) and the **Owl Cluster** (NGC 457) within Cassiopeia itself, whose stars, in a small telescope, make the shape of a tiny stick-figure owl or a Christmas tree.

**How to find it — seasonal positions.**

- **Autumn (evenings)** — high in the north, right-way-up as a W.
- **Winter** — high overhead and rotated, like an angled M.
- **Spring** — low in the north, upside-down as an M.
- **Summer** — in the northeast, tipped to one side.

Learn these four and you can read the season from Cassiopeia at a glance.

## Science

- **Gamma Cassiopeiae**, the middle star of the W, is an extraordinary object. It is a fast-rotating blue star throwing off rings of gas from its equator; its brightness varies unpredictably. It was the first star ever identified as a "**Be star**" (B-type with emission lines), a whole class named after this one variable. It is also a strong X-ray source — rare for a star of its type — and it spins so fast that material peels off in sheets.
- **Tycho's supernova.** In 1572, a new star appeared in Cassiopeia, bright enough to see in daylight for a few weeks. It upended European astronomy, which had held since Aristotle that the "fixed stars" were eternal and unchanging. The Danish astronomer **Tycho Brahe** observed it carefully, proved it was beyond the Moon (and therefore *in* the supposedly changeless heavens), and wrote *De Nova Stella* — whose title gave us the modern word **nova**. We now know it was a Type Ia supernova — a white dwarf detonating in a binary system. Its remnant is still detectable in X-rays, slowly fading.
- **Cassiopeia A** is the remnant of another, more recent supernova in the same constellation: the star exploded around 1680, but was somehow not recorded at the time (cloudy week? overlooked?). It is the strongest radio source in the sky outside the Solar System.
- **The Double Cluster** (NGC 869 and 884, on the Cassiopeia–Perseus border) — two young open clusters seven thousand light-years away, drifting through space together. With binoculars from a dark site, one of the loveliest sights in the northern sky.

## Skill — The Second Northern Anchor

- **Cassiopeia and the Plough are a pair.** Polaris sits almost exactly between them. When one is high, the other is low. In autumn, when the Plough is down near the horizon and hard to see, Cassiopeia is high overhead. In spring and early summer, reverse. Learn both shapes and you have a finder for north any night of the year.
- **Rough-and-ready north from the W.** Take the middle star of the W (Gamma Cas). Draw a perpendicular line out the deep-V side of the W. Extend it roughly five times the width of the W. You'll land close to Polaris — not as precisely as the Plough's pointers, but on a night when the Plough is hidden, close enough.
- **Cassiopeia as a seasonal clock.** The four-position rhythm above (upright W, angled M, inverted M, tilted) lets you read autumn, winter, spring, summer from the shape of Cassiopeia alone. With a little practice it works from a train window at night.
- **Into the Milky Way.** Cassiopeia is the doorway to the autumn Milky Way. If you want to start hunting open clusters, this is the constellation to begin with. Sweep slowly with binoculars and the sky breaks open into groups of young stars.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Seven invitations under autumn skies. The W is a patient companion — it's up every clear night.*

1. **Find the W.** On any clear autumn evening, look north and see if you can pick out the five-star zigzag. Note the date, the time, the weather. *Grade: Starter. Unlocks the rest of this chapter's field triggers.*
1. **Cassiopeia-to-Polaris.** Try the perpendicular-from-the-middle-star trick to find Polaris from the W alone. Check with a compass — how close did you get? *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Name the W.** See if you can learn Caph, Schedar, Gamma Cas, Ruchbah, Segin — what each Arabic root means and which star is which. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Cassiopeia-and-Plough pair.** On one clear night, find both the Plough and Cassiopeia. Sketch the two together with Polaris marked between them. You are drawing the northern anchor of your entire sky life. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Cluster-hunt.** With binoculars, see if you can find the Double Cluster (between Cassiopeia and Perseus). Sketch what you see. Bonus: have a go at the Owl / Christmas Tree Cluster (NGC 457) inside Cassiopeia. *Grade: Core.*
1. **The four positions.** Over the course of a year, sketch the W at the same time of night (say 9pm) in each of the four seasons. Four sketches, four positions — one page of the Fieldbook with the year on it. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Tycho's spot.** Using a star chart, find the position where Tycho's supernova appeared in 1572. Imagine a new star, bright as Venus, appearing there tonight. What would your family do? Write a paragraph. *Grade: Stretch.*
