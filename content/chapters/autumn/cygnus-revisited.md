## Story

*A second visit to a constellation we met in summer. Read on an October evening, when the swan is flying nose-down into the western sky.*

**The Summer Triangle said goodbye.** We met Cygnus in high summer as one corner of the Triangle, along with Vega and Altair. Back then it was directly overhead on warm nights. By October, Cygnus has migrated: the Northern Cross leans westward after sunset, flying head-first toward the horizon, until by midwinter only Deneb is left above the horizon at nightfall. Cygnus is not a summer constellation; it is a constellation that **leaves in autumn**. Watching it go is part of the autumn sky lesson.

**The fall of Phaethon (Greek).** One of the Greek stories for Cygnus: **Phaethon**, the son of the sun-god Helios, begged his father to let him drive the sun-chariot for a day. Helios relented; Phaethon lost control; the chariot swerved, scorched the Earth (this is how the Greeks explained the Sahara), and Zeus struck him down with a thunderbolt to save the world. Phaethon fell from the sky into the river Eridanus. His closest friend, **Cycnus**, dove again and again into the river to recover the body, until in pity the gods turned him into a swan and placed him in the sky — flying along the celestial river, the Milky Way, where his friend had fallen. The swan flies south each autumn as if still searching.

**The swan of Zeus.** Another Greek story makes Cygnus the form Zeus took to seduce Leda, producing Helen of Troy and the twins Castor and Pollux (whom we will meet one day, in Gemini). Yet another makes Cygnus simply "the swan" — no story, just a bird in the sky. The Greeks had several candidates and did not always decide between them. The birds people know best get stacked with story.

**The magpie bridge revisited (China).** In summer we told the story of the Weaver Girl (Vega) and the Cowherd (Altair), separated by the Silver River. On Qixi, a bridge of magpies forms across the river so the lovers can meet. **Deneb**, the bright star at the tail of Cygnus, is sometimes identified with that bridge — or with the celestial guardian who watches the lovers cross. As the swan flies west in autumn, the bridge is, in a sense, being dismantled for the year.

## Sky

Cygnus is a long cross: Deneb at the top, a line of stars running down to Albireo at the foot, with crosspiece wings spread east and west. In autumn evenings it leans sideways in the west. By December it is standing upright on the western horizon, diving nose-first.

- **Deneb** is the tail of the swan — or the top of the cross — and the dimmest corner of what was the Summer Triangle. Of the three Triangle stars, Deneb is the last to leave the autumn sky. In October, Deneb at nightfall is almost overhead. In November, high in the west. In December, low in the west. In January, gone from the evening sky. Tracking Deneb's descent across autumn is one of the best ways to feel the season turn.
- **Albireo** is the foot of the cross — or the beak of the swan, depending on which way up you read it. To the naked eye it is just a point of light. In a small telescope it splits into a **gold-and-blue double star**, possibly the prettiest double in the whole sky. Autumn is a good time to catch it because Cygnus hangs west after dinner.
- **Sadr** is the centre of the cross, where the wings meet the body. The region around Sadr is one of the richest in the Milky Way: nebulae, clusters, dust lanes — a fantastic binocular field if you have a dark site.
- **The Northern Cross.** Considered alone, without the wings, Deneb–Sadr–Albireo and the two arms make a perfect Christian cross standing on the western horizon at Christmas. This is why many old astronomy books call it the Northern Cross — it is upright and cross-shaped in December.

**How to find it — from the autumn evening sky.**

- **From the Summer Triangle.** In October evenings, the Triangle is still visible in the west. Find Deneb (the northernmost corner) and trace down the line of the body of the swan to Albireo. You have the cross.
- **From the Great Square.** The Square of Pegasus rides high in the south. Cygnus flies to the west of it. Dropping west from the Square, the first bright star you hit is Deneb.
- **By watching it go.** On the first clear night of each autumn month, note where Deneb is at the same hour. It moves visibly — from overhead in late September, to high west in October, to low west in November, to very low at dusk in December. Watching this motion is what this chapter is for.

## Science

- **Deneb is a giant among giants.** Though it is the dimmest-looking of the Triangle, it is by far the most luminous — about 200,000 times brighter than the Sun — and only looks faint because it is 2,600 light-years away (a hundred times further than Vega). It is a blue supergiant nearing the end of its life. When it goes supernova, it will briefly be as bright as a half-full Moon in our sky.
- **Albireo is a colour-matched double.** The brighter star of the pair is a yellow-orange K-type giant; the fainter is a hot blue B-type. The contrast is vivid in even a small telescope. For decades, astronomers debated whether the two were a true gravitationally bound pair or just a line-of-sight accident. Recent Gaia satellite measurements suggest they are *not* bound — just a chance alignment — but the visual effect remains extraordinary.
- **61 Cygni** is a faint but historic star near Deneb. In 1838, Friedrich Bessel measured its **parallax** — the tiny annual wobble a nearby star shows as Earth orbits the Sun — and calculated its distance: about 11 light-years. It was **the first confirmed distance to another star**. Before Bessel, nobody knew how far the stars were. After Bessel, the universe had a scale.
- **Cygnus X-1**, hidden in the neck of the swan just south of Sadr, was the first object confirmed to be a **black hole**. It is not visible to the naked eye; it is detected by X-rays from gas being stripped from its companion star as it spirals in. The science was controversial enough that Stephen Hawking and Kip Thorne had a famous friendly bet about whether it really was a black hole (Hawking eventually conceded and bought Thorne a subscription to *Penthouse* — the terms were of their era).
- **NGC 7000, the North America Nebula**, sits just east of Deneb. From a dark site, with binoculars, you can see a faint glowing cloud shaped almost exactly like the continent — a gas cloud of hydrogen ionised by a nearby hot star. It is a delight.

## Skill — A Constellation as a Season Gauge

- **Deneb's descent is the calendar of autumn.** Overhead in September; high west in October; low west in November; gone by December evenings. If you pay attention to one star, let it be Deneb — it will teach you the turn of the year.
- **The Northern Cross at Christmas.** In late December, Cygnus stands upright on the western horizon, a cross shape, just after sunset. It is one of the quiet beauties of the Christmas sky. Mention this to no one; just notice it, and feel that you know something.
- **Albireo is the autumn binocular prize.** Long nights, dry autumn air, Cygnus still up — it is a good month to set up a borrowed scope or even decent binoculars and go looking. Two colours of light, from two stars, coming from the same point.
- **From Cygnus back to the year.** The swan flying out of the sky is the moment the summer Milky Way withdraws from our evenings. We look away from the centre of our galaxy in winter and out toward the edge. Cygnus leaving is the turning of that view.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations as Cygnus sinks into autumn. A chance to watch a familiar friend change shape with the season.*

1. **Find Cygnus in autumn.** On any clear autumn evening, find the cross sinking into the west. Note Deneb at the top, Albireo at the bottom, the wings running east-west. Note the date, the time, and where Deneb is sitting — overhead? high west? low west? *Grade: Starter. Unlocks the rest of this chapter's field triggers.*
1. **Three-sketch descent.** On the first clear night of September, October, and November, sketch Cygnus at the same time each night (say 9pm). Three sketches on one page of the Fieldbook. You will have drawn, in pencil, the orbital motion of the Earth. *Grade: Core. This is the best version of this chapter's lesson.*
1. **Albireo.** With binoculars or a small telescope on a steady night, find the foot of the cross. Describe the two colours in the Fieldbook in your own words. *Grade: Core.*
1. **North America Nebula.** From a dark site, with binoculars, sweep east from Deneb. Can you find a faint glow shaped like the continent of North America? Sketch what you see — or what you don't, as the case may be. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Read Bessel.** Find 61 Cygni near Deneb — a faint pair of stars about 11 light-years away. Think about what Bessel did in 1838, and what it meant that, for the first time, a human being knew how far *any* star was from the Earth. One paragraph in the Fieldbook, in your own words. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Christmas cross.** On Christmas week, if the sky is clear, step outside at dusk and see if you can find the cross standing upright in the west. No photo, no phone — just look. Note the date, the time, and the weather. *Grade: Stretch.*
