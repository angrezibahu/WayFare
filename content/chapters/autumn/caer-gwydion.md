## Story

*An autumn chapter, a companion to the Milky Way and Pegasus chapters. The Welsh tradition reads the autumn sky as a stone country: forts of light against forts of dark, names that stand. Read on a clear autumn night with the Milky Way running overhead.*

**The Fourth Branch.** The *Mabinogi* — the four ancient branches of Welsh prose narrative, written down in the 13th and 14th centuries but considerably older in oral form — is the great medieval Welsh story-cycle. The Fourth Branch, *Math fab Mathonwy* (Math, son of Mathonwy), is the magician's branch. Its two main figures are **Gwydion** (GWID-yon), magician and storyteller and trickster, and his sister **Arianrhod** (ar-YAN-hrod), whose name means "silver wheel" or "silver circle." The branch tells of how Gwydion and his brother Gilfaethwy stole the magical pigs of Pryderi, started a war, and were turned by Math into deer, swine, and wolves as punishment, one shape per year. It tells of how Arianrhod refused to acknowledge a son she bore, and how Gwydion raised that son — **Lleu Llaw Gyffes**, "Lleu of the skilful hand," cognate with the Irish Lugh — through a series of deceptions and namings. The Fourth Branch is dense, strange, and beautiful, and it is the source for the two sky-names that this chapter is built around.

**Caer Gwydion — the Milky Way.** In medieval Welsh tradition, the Milky Way is **Caer Gwydion** — "Gwydion's fort." The fort is not a building; it is a *caer*, a circular stronghold of stone, a hilltop fastness, the kind of fort whose ruins still stud Snowdonia, Powys, and the Welsh Marches. Gwydion's fort is in the sky: a long, pale rampart of stars running from horizon to horizon. The name is preserved in the *Geiriadur Prifysgol Cymru* (the University of Wales Dictionary) with attestations from medieval Welsh poetry — Dafydd ap Gwilym, Iolo Goch, and others reference *Caer Gwydion* as a sky-name through the 14th and 15th centuries. Gwydion is the keeper of stories; the Milky Way is his keep. When the bards spoke of Gwydion's fort, they meant the river of light overhead — and a poet who knew the bard-craft was sometimes said to "have walked Caer Gwydion," to have the freedom of the sky.

**Caer Arianrhod — Corona Borealis.** Arianrhod's sky-fort is the small, neat, half-circle crown of **Corona Borealis** (the Northern Crown) — a quiet constellation east of Boötes, low in the autumn sky after dark. **Caer Arianrhod** means "the fortress of the silver wheel" — Arianrhod's name is itself a description of the constellation she rules, a circle of silver-white stars. In the Fourth Branch, when Arianrhod refused to acknowledge her son and refused him a name, Gwydion tricked her into naming him — and tricked her again into giving him weapons, and a third time into giving him a wife. After the third trick she retreated forever to her sky-fort, the silver-circled keep. The constellation is hers; she does not come down. There is a Welsh sea-mark of the same name — a half-submerged reef off Dinas Dinlle on the Caernarfon coast — and the sea-fort and the sky-fort share the name; the *idea* is one circular place, half-vanished.

**Other Welsh sky-fragments.** The Welsh sky-vocabulary is fragmentary but rich. Cygnus is sometimes referred to in late-medieval Welsh poetry as **Llwybr Llaethog**, "the Milky Path" (a calque on the Latin *Via Lactea*); but *Caer Gwydion* is the older, native Welsh name for the same band. The poet Iolo Goch (14th c.) compares his patron's hall to *Caer Gwydion ynt nodau Lleu* — "Caer Gwydion under the stars of Lleu" — meaning the Milky Way under the planets, a stone-grand simile. In Welsh, the night sky is built of stone forts more often than of myths; it is a country, not a story.

**A note on framing.** The Welsh literary tradition is alive. The *Mabinogion* is published, taught, performed, and studied in Wales and across the world. Welsh-language schools teach the Four Branches; the Eisteddfod hosts new bardic poetry every year; *Caer Gwydion* and *Caer Arianrhod* are still in active poetic use. Read Sioned Davies's modern translation. Better, if you can, learn enough Welsh to read the originals.

## Sky

The Welsh autumn sky has two clear architectures: the long fort and the small crown. Both are findable on a clear autumn evening from anywhere in Britain.

- **Caer Gwydion (the Milky Way).** A pale band of stars running from northeast (through Cassiopeia and Cygnus) overhead, and down to the southwest (through Aquila and Sagittarius). On a dark-sky night it is unmistakable; on a city night it is invisible. In autumn evenings the brightest stretch — through Cygnus — is high in the west after dinner. Caer Gwydion does not set; it rotates around the pole through the year. The bard's freedom: walk it.
- **Caer Arianrhod (Corona Borealis).** A small but distinctive half-circle of stars, looking like an open crown or a horseshoe, low in the western sky on autumn evenings (it is high overhead in summer; we name it here in autumn because it is on its way out, and because the autumn Mabinogion reading is when families settle to it). Seven stars make the crown clearly visible to the naked eye, the brightest being **Alphecca** (also known as **Gemma** — "the gem"), in Arabic *Al-Fakkah*, "the broken one" (because the crown is incomplete: the circle is interrupted on one side, like a stone fort whose wall has fallen).
- **How to find Caer Arianrhod from Caer Gwydion.** Find the Plough's handle. Arc to Arcturus (the orange star). From Arcturus, look east — the next clear-eyed pattern is the small half-crown of Corona Borealis. Six or seven stars; you cannot mistake the curve once you see it. The crown sits between Boötes and Hercules, in a part of the sky relatively short of bright stars, which makes the half-circle stand out.
- **A note on R Coronae Borealis.** Inside the crown sits a peculiar variable star: **R Coronae Borealis**. Most of the time it shines steadily at magnitude 6 — just visible to the naked eye on a dark night. Every few years, with no warning, it abruptly *fades* to invisibility (down to magnitude 14) and stays faint for months or years before recovering. Astronomers call stars of its kind *R CrB variables* — "things that go out for a while" — after this one. The mechanism is a dust shell that the star episodically blows off, blocking its own light. A star that retreats to her fortress, comes back, retreats again. The metaphor is cosmic.

## Science

- **The Milky Way is our own galaxy seen edge-on.** We covered this in the summer Milky Way chapter. Worth repeating here for context: Caer Gwydion is a hundred billion stars too distant to resolve as points, blurred into a glow by sheer distance. The "fortress" is two hundred thousand trillion miles long.
- **The Mabinogion as a manuscript record.** The *Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi* — the Four Branches — survives in two main medieval manuscripts: the *White Book of Rhydderch* (Llyfr Gwyn Rhydderch, c. 1350) and the *Red Book of Hergest* (Llyfr Coch Hergest, c. 1382–1410). Both are at the National Library of Wales (the White Book) and at Jesus College Oxford (the Red Book), and both have been digitised. The texts are in medieval Welsh; they are far older in the oral tradition that produced them — most scholars place the underlying narratives in the 11th century or earlier, with elements that may go back to pre-Christian Welsh tradition.
- **Corona Borealis in the deep field.** Beyond R Coronae Borealis, the constellation contains the **Corona Borealis Galaxy Cluster** — a true cluster of galaxies, more than 400 in number, about a billion light-years away. This is one of the densest large galaxy clusters in the local universe. With even a small backyard telescope at a dark site, you can see the brightest member galaxies as faint smudges. With Hubble, the cluster has been mapped in detail. From your back garden you cannot see the cluster, but it is there: another fortress, much further out.
- **Welsh in continuity.** Welsh is one of the oldest continuously spoken languages in Europe, in unbroken use as a community language for at least 1,400 years. Modern Welsh is mutually unintelligible with Old Welsh and partly intelligible with Middle Welsh, but the lineage is direct, with manuscript records all the way through. *Caer Gwydion* is a name still readable, and used, in modern Welsh poetry. The sky-vocabulary did not have to be reconstructed; it was kept.

## Skill — Naming What You See in Stone-Welsh

- **Two fortresses, one sky.** Step outside on an autumn evening and find both: Caer Gwydion overhead (the Milky Way through Cygnus); Caer Arianrhod low in the western sky (the half-circle of Corona Borealis). Sketch them both on one Fieldbook page with the Welsh names. You will have built the architecture of the medieval Welsh sky in five minutes.
- **A small Welsh sky-vocabulary.** Learn five Welsh sky-words: *seren* (star), *lleuad* (moon), *haul* (sun), *caer* (fort), *llwybr* (path). Then build them: *seren y bore* (the morning star), *lleuad llawn* (full moon), *Caer Gwydion* (the Milky Way), *Llwybr Llaethog* (the Milky Path). The pattern is generative; once you have the building blocks you can read the sky in Welsh from a tiny vocabulary.
- **Reading from the Mabinogion aloud.** The Sioned Davies translation reads beautifully aloud. Pick a passage from the Fourth Branch — Gwydion's first deception of Arianrhod is a good one — and read it as a winter-evening read-aloud after a Caer Gwydion sketch. The story and the sky together. This is what the Welsh bardic tradition was: read aloud, by firelight, with a hand pointing.
- **The R CrB augury.** R Coronae Borealis is currently in a long faded state (it dimmed in 2007 and has not fully recovered as of 2024–25). When it brightens — the star "returns to her fortress" — the press will note it; check the AAVSO website (aavso.org) for current magnitude. This is one of the few naked-eye variable stars whose changes you might catch in a human lifetime.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations under the autumn stone-Welsh sky. Most are slow; the slowness is deliberate.*

1. **Find Caer Arianrhod.** On a clear autumn evening, find the small half-crown of Corona Borealis low in the west. Sketch the seven stars and label Alphecca / Gemma. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Find Caer Gwydion.** On the darkest sky you can reach, find the Milky Way through Cygnus and trace it from horizon to horizon. Sketch the band on a Fieldbook page with both names: Caer Gwydion / Llwybr Llaethog. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Read a passage from the Fourth Branch.** Sioned Davies's *Mabinogion* (Oxford 2007) or Lady Charlotte Guest's open-access older translation. Read aloud the passage where Gwydion tricks Arianrhod into naming Lleu. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Five Welsh words.** Learn *seren*, *lleuad*, *haul*, *caer*, *llwybr*. Use one outside each evening for a week. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Caer Arianrhod the reef.** Look up the sea-mark off Dinas Dinlle on a tidal map (Caer Arianrhod, also called Llys Helig). Note that the sky-fort and the sea-fort share the name. Note in the Fieldbook the half-vanished geometry that joins them. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **R CrB watch.** Find R Coronae Borealis on a star chart; check its current AAVSO magnitude. If it is bright enough to be naked-eye, sketch the crown with R CrB visible. If it is faint, sketch the crown without it and note "Arianrhod in her fortress." Either way, a record. *Grade: Stretch.*
