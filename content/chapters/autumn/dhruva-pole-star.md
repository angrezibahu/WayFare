## Story

*An autumn chapter, paired with the Plough and Cassiopeia. The same star is at the heart of the chapter — Polaris in English, Dhruva in Sanskrit, scip-steorra in Anglo-Saxon — but the story is different, and that is the point. Read this with Polaris in view.*

**Dhruva — the steadfast one.** In the Hindustani sky, the pole star is **Dhruva** (DHROO-vuh), whose name means "the firm one," "the steadfast," "the unmoving." The Sanskrit story, told most fully in the **Bhāgavata Purāṇa** (around 800–1000 CE) and earlier in the **Viṣṇu Purāṇa** (around 400 CE):

**The boy who would not move.** **Uttānapāda**, an ancient king, had two wives. The first, Sunīti, was kind but unfavoured; her son was **Dhruva**, a five-year-old child. The second, Suruci, was the favourite; her son was Uttama. One day Dhruva, drawn by a small boy's wish to sit on his father's lap, climbed up beside the king as he sat on the throne. Suruci pulled him off, scornful: this seat is not for you; you were not born of *me*; if you wanted such a seat, you should have chosen a better mother. Dhruva, stung, ran to his own mother. Sunīti, hearing the story, did not deny what Suruci had said. She told the boy gently: *the only honour that no one can give you and no one can take from you is the honour you earn by your own steadfastness*. If you want a seat that no one can pull you off, she said, find what is unchanging, and hold to it.

The boy left the palace. He walked into the forest, sat down at the foot of a tree, and began to meditate on what was permanent — what could not be taken away. The story tells of the sage Nārada finding him there, trying to dissuade him, then teaching him a mantra of Viṣṇu when he would not be turned. Dhruva sat through cold and heat and rain. He sat through hunger and thirst. He sat for years. When at last Viṣṇu appeared to him — the great preserver, the keeper of what endures — and asked what he wished, Dhruva said: only to be near you. Viṣṇu placed him in the sky, at the one point around which the whole of the heavens turn, the one star that does not move. Every other star wheels through the night and through the year; Dhruva does not. He found what was permanent. He is sitting there still.

**A note on framing.** This is not "Hindu mythology" in a museum sense. The Dhruva story is a living bedtime story across South Asia, told to children for the same reason for two thousand years: *if you want what cannot be taken from you, find what is unchanging and hold to it*. It is also a navigational fact. The pole star that the parent points at is the same fact-of-nature that an astronomer measures, and the same fact-of-nature that a sailor — Hindustani, Anglo-Saxon, Polynesian, or Greek — uses to know where they are. The metaphor is not added on top of the astronomy; it follows from it. A star that does not move teaches both children and ships.

**Why Dhruva matters cosmologically in the Hindustani tradition.** In the Purāṇas, Dhruva is the centre of a great rotating cosmic structure: the seven sages (Saptarshi) circle him, the planets circle him, the sun and moon circle him. This is observationally true at human timescales and to a sky-watcher's eye: the pole *is* the still centre of nightly motion. The Purāṇas extend this to a moral cosmology — Dhruva is also the centre of *dharma*, the steadfast order around which everything else turns. The star and the principle share a name because they are read as two sides of one fact.

## Sky

Dhruva is **Polaris** — the same star we find in the Plough chapter, in the Cassiopeia chapter, and in every northern wayfinding chapter in this book. The Sanskrit name and the English name and the Old English *scip-steorra* and the Arabic *Al-Judayy* all point at the same star. What changes is what is around it.

- **Finding Dhruva from the Saptarshi.** Find the seven sages (the Plough). The two pointer stars at the front of the bowl — Pulaha (Merak) and Kratu (Dubhe) — point to Dhruva. Five times the gap between them, in a straight line outwards, lands you on Polaris.
- **Finding Dhruva from Cassiopeia.** Find the W (or M, depending on season). The deeper V of the W points roughly toward Polaris. The Plough's pointers are more accurate; Cassiopeia is the back-up route on a night when the Plough is hidden.
- **What sits with Dhruva.** Polaris is the brightest star of **Ursa Minor** (the Little Bear, *Ursa Minor*, in Sanskrit *Śiśumāra* or *the porpoise*). The seven main stars of Ursa Minor make a dimmer plough or dipper, with Polaris at its tip. From a moderately dark sky most of Ursa Minor is naked-eye visible; from a city all but Polaris and one or two faint companions vanish.
- **The Saptarshi circle Dhruva.** Stand outside long enough on any clear night and you will see the Plough rotate around Polaris counter-clockwise. In four hours it has turned a quarter-turn; in twelve hours, a half-turn (which you don't see, because the day is in the way); in twenty-four hours, a full turn. Dhruva, in the centre, does not move. The Saptarshi orbit him. The cosmology and the observation are the same picture.

## Science

- **Polaris is not exactly at the pole.** It sits about 0.7° off the true celestial pole — close enough to be a useful pole-star, but not perfectly fixed. In a long-exposure photograph you can see Polaris tracing a small circle around the true pole. Over the next 200 years, due to precession, Polaris will edge slightly closer to the true pole, then begin to drift away again.
- **Precession of the equinoxes — the wandering pole.** Earth's axis is not fixed in space; it traces a 26,000-year circle, like a wobbling spinning top. As a result, the celestial pole moves through different stars over millennia.
  - **Around 3000 BCE**, the pole star was **Thuban** in Draco — the star the Egyptians sighted down the airshaft of the Great Pyramid.
  - **Around 1100 BCE**, the pole was midway between Thuban and the modern Polaris.
  - **Today (2026)**, the pole sits very near Polaris.
  - **Around 14,000 CE**, the pole will be near **Vega** in Lyra.
  - **Around 27,000 CE**, the pole will return to where Polaris is now.

  Dhruva, the steadfast, is steadfast on a human timescale. On a cosmic timescale he is not.
- **The Hindustani astronomical tradition recognised precession.** The **Sūrya Siddhānta** (c. 4th–5th century CE) and the works of **Āryabhaṭa** (476–550 CE) include numerical treatments of the slow drift of the equinoxes. The term *ayanāṃśa* — "the precessional offset" — is built into modern panchāṅga calculations to keep the sidereal calendar aligned with the actual sky despite the wobble. The story of Dhruva is told in the Purāṇas as if the pole were fixed; the astronomers, working in the same tradition at the same time, knew it was not. Both are true: at any one moment Dhruva *is* the pole; over thousands of years, the *idea* of a pole moves. The tradition holds both.
- **Polaris is a Cepheid variable.** The star itself is a yellow supergiant about 433 light-years away, the closest Cepheid to Earth. Cepheids are pulsating stars whose pulsation period correlates precisely with their absolute brightness — so they are *standard candles*: by measuring the period and the apparent brightness, you can compute the distance. Polaris's pulsation has been weakening over the last century (which is itself a small mystery in stellar physics). Dhruva, the steadfast, is also pulsing and slowly evolving; he is just doing it on a timescale longer than ours.
- **Polaris is a triple system.** Polaris A is the supergiant; Polaris Ab is a tight close companion (resolved by Hubble in 2006); Polaris B is a distant companion visible in a small telescope. Three stars orbiting at the still point of the sky.

## Skill — One Star, Many Languages

- **Finding Dhruva.** From the Plough or from Cassiopeia. The technique is the same in any language; it is the most useful naked-eye fact in the Northern Hemisphere sky. Practise both routes; on a partly-clouded night you will need them.
- **Latitude from Dhruva.** The angle of Dhruva above your northern horizon equals your latitude. From London, about 51°. From the Hebrides, about 58°. From Madras — where the story was first written down in Sanskrit by people sitting on the roofs of stone houses — about 13°. As you travel, Dhruva moves up or down; latitude is altitude. The principle is the same as the Polynesian zenith-star method, just applied to the pole rather than to a passing star.
- **Watching the Saptarshi turn.** On a clear evening, find the Plough at, say, 9 pm. Sketch its position. At 11 pm, sketch again. The Plough has rotated counter-clockwise around Polaris by about 30°. You have just observed Earth's rotation — and confirmed the cosmology of the Purāṇas in real time. The Saptarshi turn around Dhruva. Always.
- **One star, four names, four sentences.** Learn to say, with the star in view, four sentences out loud:

  - **English:** "That is Polaris, the pole star."
  - **Sanskrit:** "*Dhruva*, the steadfast one."
  - **Old English:** "*Scip-steorra*, the ship-star."
  - **Arabic:** "*Al-Judayy*, the kid."

  One star, the same fact, four traditions. Doing this once changes how you see the sky.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations to the steadfast star.*

1. **Find Dhruva.** From the Plough's pointers or Cassiopeia's V, find Polaris. Say "Dhruva" aloud once with the star in view. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Read the boy's story.** Find the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, Skandha IV chapters 8–12 (Sanyal translation, sacred-texts.com, public domain). Read the passage where Dhruva sits down in the forest. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Latitude from Dhruva.** Measure the altitude of Polaris above your northern horizon with a clinometer or a homemade quadrant. Compare it to your map latitude. The match should be within a degree. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Watch the Saptarshi turn.** On one clear evening, sketch the Plough at 9 pm and again at 11 pm. Note that Dhruva has not moved; the Saptarshi have rotated around him. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Four names, one star.** Learn the four sentences above and say them outside, with Polaris in view, on one clear night. Note in the Fieldbook how it changed the look of the star. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **The wandering pole.** On the Year Map page of your Fieldbook, draw a small diagram showing the pole's 26,000-year circle through Draco, Polaris, Vega, and back. Mark the pyramids' Thuban around 3000 BCE; mark a future Vega-pole around 14,000 CE; mark today's Polaris. The steadfast is not eternal. *Grade: Stretch.*
