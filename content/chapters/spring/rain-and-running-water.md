## Story

*Three short pieces for a rainy-window morning.*

**A hundred words for rain.** *Smirr* is a fine drifting drizzle you barely feel. *Dreich* is the cold grey all-day grimness that damps everything. *Plowetery* is showery and unpredictable. *Goselet* is a hard soaking. *Uisge* is just water, pronounced *oosh-geh*, which is also the root of *whisky*, water-of-life. Languages that name their weather in detail are languages spoken by people who must go out in it.

**Y glaw mân (Welsh).** The small rain. Mist-fine, it rides the wind rather than falling. You can walk for an hour in *glaw mân* and only notice when you stop that your coat weighs twice what it did. Welsh hill-farmers know when the *glaw mân* arrives that the afternoon will be long and wet and the sheep will need counting twice.

**Barsāt and phuhār (Hindustani).** In North India the year has its own rainy season. *Barsāt* is the monsoon itself — months of it. *Phuhār* is a fine spray, a romance-of-films word. *Rimjhim* is the soft rhythmic patter of rain on leaves. When the first drops of *barsāt* hit dry earth the whole subcontinent smells of *mitti ki khushboo* — the fragrance of the soil. A culture that names its rains sees them.

## Land

**Rain changes the map.** A dry ditch becomes a stream. A stream becomes a river. A field becomes mud, a path becomes a slick of clay. What was a short-cut yesterday is a wade today. In spring the ground is already near-saturated from winter, so rain runs off instead of sinking in. Small catchments respond fast — a hill-brook can treble its flow within an hour of heavy rain up on the moor.

**Mud reads.** Soft ground keeps the day's news longer than snow. A single fresh print in mud tells you an animal passed this morning. A crumbled print with grass already springing back is yesterday or older. On a path, count deer prints against dog prints against boot prints — you will learn who uses the path and when.

**A rainbow is always opposite the Sun.** If you are looking at a rainbow, the Sun is exactly behind you. The higher the Sun, the lower the rainbow. At noon in summer there is barely a rainbow at all; at the start and end of a spring day, when the Sun is low, rainbows arch high. Double rainbows have the colours reversed in the outer bow. The sky between the two bows is darker — this is *Alexander's band*, named for Alexander of Aphrodisias, who noticed it around 200 AD.

**Petrichor — the smell of rain on dry ground.** The word was coined by two Australian scientists in 1964, from Greek *petra* (stone) and *ichor* (the fluid that runs in the veins of gods). It is an oil released by plants and held by rocks and soil through dry weather, then flung into the air by raindrops. Dogs smell it coming minutes before we do.

**Water shows you the land.** Wherever water is running, the land is tilting. A puddle shows you a low point. A gutter shows you a drain. A stream shows you a valley even when the valley is hidden by trees. If you are ever lost in hill country, downhill and downstream will always lead to people — eventually — because people settle where water gathers.

## Historical — three ways of knowing the rain

<div class="vibe vibe-egypt">

### Ancient Egypt — the Nilometer and the calendar of the flood

For three thousand years Egyptian life turned on a single measurement: the height of the Nile in July. Too little flood and the crop failed and people starved. Too much flood and the villages drowned. The difference between a good and a bad year was sometimes a single cubit — a forearm's length of water.

So the priests built **Nilometers** — stone staircases that dropped into the river, each step marked with a cubit scale. The most famous, at Elephantine Island near Aswan, dates from before the pyramids. When the river rose to sixteen cubits it was written on the temple wall as *"the flood of a joyful year"*. At twelve cubits, hunger. At eighteen, disaster.

The Egyptian year had three seasons, all named for water: **Akhet** (the flood, mid-July to mid-November), **Peret** (the emergence of the fields from the flood), **Shemu** (the harvest, when the water was gone). Their calendar was a rain-gauge given a name.

The flood itself came from rain none of them ever saw — the Ethiopian monsoon, two thousand kilometres upstream. A priest at Aswan measuring cubits in July was reading the weather of an Ethiopian July he would never live to know by any other means.

</div>

<div class="vibe vibe-ww2">

### MI9 — the silk map and the compass button (1939–45)

**MI9** was a branch of British military intelligence set up in 1939 to help servicemen evade capture and escape if caught. Its most beautiful invention was the **silk escape map** — a full topographic chart, printed on silk or mulberry-tissue, quiet when opened, waterproof, able to be sewn into a collar or hidden in a boot heel. Over 1.75 million were made.

The maps showed the **rain-water features** an evader needed: the small streams that flow all summer, the marshes that look like fields on a German 1:200,000 but would drown a man, the canals running east-west across the Low Countries that had to be crossed at night. MI9 paired each map with a **miniature compass** — often disguised as a uniform button that unscrewed, or hidden in a collar stud — because a man crossing wet country in cloud needs to know which way downstream is, and in Europe the big rivers run to the sea, which was home.

The MI9 rule was: **follow running water, at night, keep it on the same hand.** Rivers lead to towns, towns lead to bridges, bridges lead to the contacts of the escape line. Rain was an ally: it hid footprints and kept the dogs in.

</div>

<div class="vibe vibe-modern">

### Modern — radar, catchments and the hydrograph

Rain is measured today by two instruments working in tandem. A **tipping-bucket rain gauge** is a small see-saw that flips each time 0.2 mm of rain has collected — every flip is a click the computer counts. **Weather radar** fires pulses of microwave at the sky and reads the echo back from raindrops; droplet size shifts the echo's frequency, so the radar tells you not just *where* it's raining but *how hard*.

Join many rain gauges to a digital map of the ground, and you have a **hydrological model** of a catchment. The Environment Agency and SEPA run these around the clock. When rain falls on a hill, the model predicts how much reaches each stream, at what hour, at what depth. A flood warning for a village downstream is that calculation made visible.

The key modern word is **lag time** — the hours between rain falling on the fells and the flood peak arriving at the bridge. Urban catchments (concrete, tarmac, drains) have lag times of *minutes*. Upland peat catchments have lag times of several hours. A catchment you can't measure is a catchment that will surprise you.

</div>

## Science

- The water cycle is driven by the Sun. Sunlight evaporates water from oceans and lakes, the vapour rises and cools, it condenses into clouds, it falls again. A single water molecule may have been in a Roman bath, a North Atlantic wave, and a cloud over your roof.
- Rain clouds are grey because they are *thick*, not because they are made of different stuff. Enough white water droplets stacked on top of each other absorb the light that would otherwise come through, and what reaches your eye is the light that has bounced off the bottom — dim, grey.
- A *flash flood* does not need rain where you are. Rain twenty miles upstream can put a dry streambed under six feet of water in twenty minutes. Never pitch a tent in a dry gully, even on a sunny day.

## Skill — Reading rain and water

- **Check the catchment, not the forecast for your spot.** If you are walking by a stream in the Lake District, the weather that matters is the weather over the fells ten miles uphill, not the sun on your shoulders. Use a rainfall radar (Met Office app, or MetCheck) and look upstream.
- **Stepping-stones lie.** Stones that were safe last weekend may be fully submerged or just-below-surface-invisible this weekend. Poke with a stick before you trust your weight. If you can't see the bed of a stream, don't wade it.
- **Rising water is quick.** A ford that was ankle-deep at midday can be thigh-deep by teatime if it has rained on the moor. The UK drowning statistics are full of this.
- **Dry out at night, not in the morning.** Wet boots off, socks out to air, inside-out if you can. Wet clothes hung over a chair not in a pile. A wet kit put on a wet body in the morning is misery and cold. This is a small skill that pays every trip.

## Try It — wayfaring from the historical pages

<div class="vibe vibe-try">

**Build a garden Nilometer (after the Egyptians).** Push a long straight stake vertically into the ground of the nearest stream, pond or puddle that refills from weather. Mark the stake in centimetres with permanent pen — cubits, if you are feeling pharaonic (one cubit ≈ 52 cm). Read it at the same time every day for a month in spring. When the level jumps, look back 6, 12, 24 hours in the Met Office rain-radar archive: where did the rain actually fall to put that water on your stake? You have built the oldest weather instrument in the world and you are reading a catchment upstream by the height of a stick.

**Walk a river at night like an MI9 evader.** Pick a short, safe, legal stretch of towpath or lane beside a stream, with a grown-up, on a moonlit evening. Before you set off, note the direction of flow on a map. Now walk the stretch *without a torch on*, keeping the sound of the water on the same side of your body. Notice how much your ears tell you — the faster shallow riffles, the slower deep pools, the bridges you hear before you see. An evader on foot knew their river by sound alone. Log in the Fieldbook: what did the river *sound like* for each 100 paces?

</div>

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations for rainy days. Some of them want you out in the weather — that's the point.*

1. **Rain-Word Collection.** Collect fifteen rain-words across the five tongues above (Old English, Scots, Welsh, Irish, Hindustani) and any two more of your own choice. Write each with what it *really* means to someone who lives in it. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Rainbow Triangulation.** Next rainbow you catch, stand still. Find where the Sun is directly behind you, and see if you can work out the angle from the Sun-opposite point to the top of the bow. (It is always about 42° — but check, don't trust.) *Grade: Core.*
1. **Mud Reading.** After a wet spell, spend twenty minutes on a muddy path tallying animal prints. Aim for three species at least. Sketch the clearest. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Catchment Detective.** For your nearest stream, see if you can work out — on a map — the shape of ground that feeds it. Then walk the lowest 500m of it after rain and see the answer running past you. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Petrichor.** On the first warm rain after a dry week, step outside the moment it begins. Describe the smell in the Fieldbook in your own words — not someone else's. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Nilometer Month.** Keep your garden Nilometer (above) for a full month of spring. Note each day's rain on the radar and each day's reading on your stake. Draw the hydrograph. *Grade: Stretch.*
