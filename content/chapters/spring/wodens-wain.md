## Story

*A spring chapter, paired with the Plough. The sky over Britain a thousand years ago was the same sky we have tonight, but it was named in a different language — one that became ours. Read this on a clear spring evening with the Plough overhead.*

**Carles wægn — the churl's wagon (Old English).** When the Anglo-Saxons looked at the Plough, they did not see a plough — that name comes much later, from the Middle English *plough* / *plow*. They saw a **wagon**: *Carles wægn*, "Carl's wagon," or "the churl's wagon" (a *carl* in Old English was a free peasant farmer, the man who worked his own land). The handle of the Plough is the *þixle* — the wagon's tongue, the long pole the oxen were yoked to. The bowl is the cart bed. The wagon is being pulled across the northern sky by a team of stars too faint to give names to. By the 13th century the name had migrated to "Charles' wain" — Charlemagne's wagon — by a folk-etymology pun on *carl*. The Anglo-Saxon original is older and humbler: this is the cart that brings the harvest in.

**Wætlinga Stræt — Watling Street in the sky.** Watling Street is the great Roman road that runs from Dover through London to Wroxeter — one of the spines of Roman Britain. The Anglo-Saxons, walking it through dark forests, called it *Wætlinga Stræt*, "the Wætlings' street" (the Wætlings were a people of legend whose name attached to the road). When they looked up at the **Milky Way**, they saw the same: a long pale road across the sky, running from horizon to horizon. So they called it *Wætlinga Stræt* too — Watling Street stretched across the heavens. Layamon's *Brut*, around 1190, gives the earliest written reference; the name was almost certainly older in oral use. The road on the ground; the road in the sky; one name for both. There is no figurative leap — they are both *strǣt*, both walked.

**The Old English Rune Poem.** A 10th-century Anglo-Saxon poem — preserved in a single manuscript that was burned in the 1731 Cottonian fire and survives only in an earlier transcription — gives a stanza for each of the 29 runes of the late Anglo-Saxon futhorc. Several of the stanzas describe sky and weather:

> *Sigel semannum*    *symble bið on hihte,*
> *þonne hi hine feriaþ*  *ofer fisces beþ,*
> *oþ hi brimhengest*    *bringeþ to lande.*

> The sun is ever a joy in the hopes of seafarers,
> when they journey away over the fishes' bath,
> until the sea-stallion brings them to land.
> *— Old English Rune Poem, Sigel (the sun rune), trans. R.I. Page*

It is the only place the Anglo-Saxon word *sigel* — sun — survives in poetry. *Brimhengest*, "sea-stallion," is the ship; *fisces beþ*, "the fishes' bath," is the sea. The poem names the sun as a navigational fact: a thing that brings sailors home. Read it aloud once in Old English and once in modern translation, and you have the same sky in two times.

**Bede and the year.** The Venerable Bede, writing around 725 CE in Wearmouth-Jarrow, set down the **Old English month names** in his *De temporum ratione* ("The Reckoning of Time"). He is our chief source for what Britain called its months before Latin and French took over:

| Modern   | Anglo-Saxon          | Meaning                                         |
|----------|----------------------|-------------------------------------------------|
| January  | *Æfterra Gēola*      | "After-Yule" — month after midwinter           |
| February | *Solmōnaþ*           | "Mud-month" or "cake-month" (debated)           |
| March    | *Hrēþmōnaþ*          | Possibly "the month of the goddess Hrēþe"       |
| April    | *Ēosturmōnaþ*        | Month of the goddess Ēostre — gives us "Easter" |
| May      | *Þrimilcimōnaþ*      | "Three-milkings month" — cows milked thrice daily in the long days |
| June     | *Ærra Līþa*          | "Before-Litha" — before midsummer               |
| July     | *Æfterra Līþa*       | "After-Litha"                                   |
| August   | *Wēodmōnaþ*          | "Weed-month" — the time of weeding              |
| September| *Hāligmōnaþ*         | "Holy-month"                                    |
| October  | *Winterfylleþ*       | "Winter-fullness" (the first full moon of winter) |
| November | *Blōtmōnaþ*          | "Blood-month" — the slaughtering of cattle for winter |
| December | *Ærra Gēola*         | "Before-Yule"                                   |

This is a calendar of farming and weather. *Þrimilcimōnaþ* is May because cows can be milked three times a day on the long days of grass; *Wēodmōnaþ* is August because that is when the weeds need pulling; *Blōtmōnaþ* is November because that is when livestock that won't survive winter must be killed and salted. Bede was not romanticising; he was recording a working calendar. Some of these names — *Ēosturmōnaþ* and the goddess Ēostre — survive only in his text. We owe Bede the names of our months before they were renamed.

## Sky

The Anglo-Saxons named what they could see, plainly. Their sky-vocabulary survives in modern English place-names, in the names of the days of the week, and in fragments of poetry.

- **The Plough is *Carles wægn*.** Find the seven stars; trace the wagon. The two pointer stars (Merak and Dubhe) are the front of the cart bed. The handle is the long pole pulled by an invisible team.
- **The Pole Star is *scip-steorra*** — the "ship-star." The word is attested in late Old English / early Middle English texts; sailors used it for the same reason we still do. The Anglo-Saxons did not call it Polaris (a Latin name) but they knew which star was steadfast — the one the wagon swung around.
- **The Milky Way is *Wætlinga Stræt*** — Watling Street, the road across the sky. On a dark night away from town, look at the band of pale light running through Cygnus and across the summer sky. Then walk a length of the actual Roman road if you can — any *strǣt* in your county is named the same way. Two Watlings, one name.
- **The Sun is *sunne* (feminine in Old English).** The Moon is *mōna* (masculine). This is a Germanic peculiarity — the Romance languages have it the other way around. *Sunne* and *mōna* give us "Sunday" and "Monday" directly.
- **The other days of the week** are the Anglo-Saxon gods of the planets, Latinised: *Tiwesdæg* (Tiw, war-god, = Mars), *Wōdnesdæg* (Wōden = Mercury), *Þunresdæg* (Thunor = Jupiter), *Frīgedæg* (Frige = Venus), *Sæternesdæg* (the Roman Saturn, kept Latin). When you say "Wednesday" you are saying "Wōden's day," and when Wōden is in the sky he is the planet **Mercury** — visible in twilight near the horizon when the geometry favours it.
- **Stars are *steorran* and *tunglu***. Both words survive — *steorran* gives us "star"; *tunglu* (literally "lights") survives in the related Scots word *tongle* and in some dialect uses but is otherwise lost.

**Looking up with Anglo-Saxon names.** On a clear spring night, step outside and try this: name what you see in Old English. The Plough is *Carles wægn*. The Pole Star is *scip-steorra*. The Sun (gone) is *sunne*. The Moon is *mōna*. Each star is a *steorra*. The Milky Way (if you can see it) is *Wætlinga Stræt*. You are speaking the sky in the words that were used here a thousand years ago, and many of those words are still alive in modern English. The continuity is verbal as much as observational.

## Science

- **The Anglo-Saxon calendar was lunisolar.** Bede describes a year of twelve lunar months with an extra month inserted (*Þrilīþa*, "third Litha") in some years to keep the year aligned with the Sun. This is the same kind of system the Hebrew, Hindustani, and Chinese calendars use; the Anglo-Saxons were not unique in it. They abandoned it, more or less, when they took up the Roman Julian calendar. *Winterfylleþ* — October — was named for the *full moon* nearest the start of winter, showing the lunar reckoning under the Latin overlay.
- **Old English star nomenclature is sparse and observational.** Compared to the meticulous Arabic system, the Anglo-Saxons did not catalogue individual stars by name. They named asterisms (the wagon, the road) and they noted the sun and moon and the brightest planet (Wōden / Mercury, attested in some texts). For careful astronomy in early-medieval Britain you go to Latin texts (Bede) or to translations from the Arab tradition that came later. The Anglo-Saxon sky is a working farmer's and sailor's sky, not a court astronomer's sky.
- **Bede's astronomical work.** Bede's *De temporum ratione* is one of the most important astronomical texts of the early Middle Ages. He calculated the date of Easter using the Metonic cycle (19 years) — Greek mathematics inherited via Alexandria via Latin Church scholars — and explained the tides as Moon-driven, with reference to local observation in the Tyne (he could see the tide-races from his monastery). It is plausibly the first European text to describe lunar tides correctly. Bede was not "the Anglo-Saxons," but he was an Anglo-Saxon, and what he recorded is part of our record of how the sky was read in early Britain.
- **The Wōden's-day / Mercury identification** is *interpretatio romana* — Roman writers identifying foreign gods with their own. The Anglo-Saxon Wōden has functions in war, wisdom, ecstasy, and the dead that don't really line up with the Roman Mercury, but the day-name identification stuck. Tacitus, writing about the Germanic tribes in the *Germania* (98 CE), made the same equation; the day-names are seven hundred years older than the Old English manuscripts that record them.

## Skill — A Working Sky in Working English

- **Speak the sky in Old English once a season.** Step outside on a clear night and name what you see — *Carles wægn*, *scip-steorra*, *Wætlinga Stræt*, *sunne* (gone), *mōna* — using the words that were used here. The vocabulary is small and easy to memorise. It will change how you read place-names: every *strǣt* on the OS map (Watling, Akeman, Ermin, Stane) is named the same way; every village ending in *-ley* is a clearing; every *-wic* is a farm. The Anglo-Saxon sky and the Anglo-Saxon land share a language. Knowing one helps you read the other.
- **Day-names as a planet-finder.** The five visible planets are still hidden in our day-names. *Tiwesdæg* = Mars. *Wōdnesdæg* = Mercury. *Þunresdæg* = Jupiter. *Frīgedæg* = Venus. *Sæternesdæg* = Saturn. Knowing this once means that "Tuesday" and "Wednesday" are not just sounds but a sky-list. On a clear evening you can name "today's planet" and look for it: Mars in the south, Mercury in the twilight, Jupiter rising, Venus the bright morning star.
- **The wagon and the road.** The Plough as a wagon, the Milky Way as a road. The metaphor of the Anglo-Saxon sky is not heroic combat or royal myth; it is *getting somewhere*. The sky, like the country, is criss-crossed with routes — and the seven brightest stars of the north are pulling a cart along one of them. Hold this image once and the bourgeois English sky comes into focus.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations under the Anglo-Saxon sky. Half of them are vocabulary work, half are night work. Use both.*

1. **Name the wagon.** Find the Plough and trace it as *Carles wægn*: the bowl as the cart, the handle as the tongue. Sketch it in the Fieldbook with the Old English name beside the modern. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Wōden's-day Mercury.** On the next clear *Wōdnesdæg* (Wednesday) evening within a few weeks of Mercury's elongation (check Stellarium), step outside fifteen minutes after sunset and look low in the west. If you find a faint star where no other star is, you have found Wōden — the planet that gave the day its name. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Bede's months.** Write out the twelve Anglo-Saxon month names on the Year Map page of your Fieldbook, with their meanings. As each month rolls round, write a one-line note: was it really the mud month? The weed month? The blood month? *Grade: Core.*
1. **Read the Sigel rune aloud.** Find the Old English Rune Poem online (the Camden Rutgers edition is good). Read the *Sigel* (sun) and *Dæg* (day) stanzas aloud — once in Old English, once in modern English. Note which words you can almost guess. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Walking the Wætlings' road.** Walk a section of an actual Roman road if you live near one — Watling Street, Akeman Street, Ermin Street, Stane Street. On the next dark night you can reach a clear sky, find the Milky Way and trace it. The names are the same, deliberately. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **A small Old English vocabulary of the sky.** Learn ten Old English sky-words: *steorra* (star), *mōna* (moon), *sunne* (sun), *wægn* (wagon), *scip-steorra* (pole star), *strǣt* (road), *gēola* (Yule), *līþa* (midsummer), *fyrr* (fire), *heofon* (heaven). Use one each evening for a fortnight. *Grade: Stretch.*

## Further

- **Bede**, *The Reckoning of Time*, trans. Faith Wallis (Liverpool, 1999) — readable, scholarly, definitive. The source for the Anglo-Saxon month-names.
- **Ælfric**, *De temporibus anni*, ed. Heinrich Henel (1942) — Anglo-Saxon science writing, bilingual edition; on archive.org. Demonstrates what an Anglo-Saxon parish priest taught about sun, moon, and seasons.
- **The Old English Rune Poem** — Maureen Halsall's 1981 edition is the scholarly standard; the open-access Camden Rutgers project gives the text and a translation online.
- **Bosworth-Toller Anglo-Saxon Dictionary** — bosworthtoller.com — for the actual words. Searchable.
- **Layamon's Brut** — for *Wætlinga Stræt* as the Milky Way. The Cotton Caligula manuscript is the source; modern editions are widely available.
- **Sam Newton**, *The Origins of Beowulf* — context for the wider Old English literary culture in which the Rune Poem and Bede sit.
