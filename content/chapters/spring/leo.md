## Story

*A spring chapter. Read on a mild evening in March, April, or May, when Leo is high in the south after dark.*

**The Nemean Lion (Greece).** The first of Herakles’ twelve labours was to kill a lion whose hide no weapon could pierce. He tracked it to its cave in Nemea, stopped up one entrance, and went in through the other. Inside, in the dark, he wrestled it with his bare hands and throttled it. Afterwards he skinned it using one of its own claws — the only thing sharp enough — and wore the skin as armour for the rest of his labours. The gods put the lion in the sky as a warning and a memorial. It rises in spring; Herakles’ constellation follows it around the sky forever.

**Ur.Gu.La — the Great Lion (Babylon).** The Mesopotamians saw a lion here too, nearly four thousand years ago. Their name *Ur.Gu.La* survives on clay tablets. The Greek story almost certainly inherited the pattern from them; the myth travelled with the sky.

**The Royal Star (Persia, medieval Arab astronomy).** The bright star at Leo’s heart is **Regulus** — Latin for "little king." In Arabic it is *Qalb al-Asad*, "the heart of the lion." Persian astronomers listed it as one of the four royal stars, guardian of the north, watching the spring equinox. It sits almost exactly on the ecliptic — the Sun’s path through the sky — which is why the Moon and planets often slip past it and why ancient watchers paid it close attention.

## Sky

Leo is one of the few constellations that genuinely looks like the thing it’s named for. In spring evenings, look high in the south (from the Northern Hemisphere) for a shape like a backwards question mark with a triangle attached to the right.

- **The Sickle** — the head and mane. Six stars curving in a hook: the bright dot at the bottom of the hook is Regulus. Some families call this shape the Backwards Question Mark; it’s a useful name because it’s what it is.
- **The Triangle** — the hindquarters. Three stars making a right-ish triangle to the east of the Sickle. The bright star at the back corner is **Denebola**, from Arabic *Dhanab al-Asad*, "tail of the lion."
- **Regulus** is the heart. It’s bluish-white, steady, and sits right on the ecliptic, so if the Moon is also up, they’ll often be close.

**How to find it from the Plough.** Take the two stars at the *bottom* of the Plough’s bowl (the ones furthest from the handle). Draw a line through them *away* from Polaris. The line lands on Leo. This is the "poke a hole in the bottom of the bowl and the water falls on the lion" trick — the Plough as a dripping bucket.

**Leo as a seasonal clock.** Orion is the winter hero; Leo is the spring hero. When Leo is high in the south at 9pm, it is spring. When Leo is high in the south at 3am, it is winter and you are up too late.

## Science

- **Regulus** is not one star but four — a hot blue-white main star with a faint companion, plus a more distant pair. It spins so fast (once every sixteen hours or so) that it is squashed into an oval rather than a sphere; if it spun much faster it would fly apart.
- **Denebola** is younger than the Sun, surrounded by a disc of dust that is probably a solar system still forming. It is about 36 light-years away — the light you see tonight left it around the time an older sibling was born.
- **Wolf 359** is a tiny red dwarf in Leo, eighth-nearest star to the Sun (7.9 light-years). It is far too faint to see without a telescope, but it’s one of the closest stellar neighbours we have.
- **The Leo Triplet** — three galaxies (M65, M66, and NGC 3628) sitting close together under Leo’s belly. In a small telescope they look like three faint smudges; each is a city of hundreds of billions of stars, tens of millions of light-years away. A reminder: the stars of Leo are all inside our galaxy; the Triplet is three other galaxies peeking between them.
- Each November the sky produces the **Leonids**, a meteor shower that appears to radiate from the Sickle. Most years it’s a quiet trickle; every 33 years or so it produces an extraordinary storm.

## Skill — Reading Spring by Leo

- **Leo rising is the gate of spring.** In the Northern Hemisphere, when you first see Leo clearing the eastern horizon after dinner, winter’s grip is breaking. When Leo sits straight overhead at dusk, it is May.
- **Regulus marks the ecliptic.** If you watch the Moon travel across the sky for a week in spring, it will pass close to Regulus. Planets do the same. Learning one point on the ecliptic gives you a handhold on the whole zodiac.
- **Leo points to the Plough, and the Plough points to Polaris.** Reverse the Plough trick: from Regulus, trace back up the line and you hit the bottom of the Plough’s bowl; from there, the two pointer stars take you to Polaris. On a night when Leo is up and the Plough is hidden behind a roofline, you can still walk yourself to north.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations as the nights warm up. Leo is a patient hero — it returns every spring.*

1. **Find the Sickle.** On any clear spring evening between 9 and 11pm, step outside, face south, and see if you can pick out the backwards question mark. Note the date, the time, and the state of the sky. *Grade: Starter. Unlocks the rest of this chapter’s field triggers.*
1. **Plough-to-Leo.** Find the Plough, then try the bottom-of-the-bowl trick: drop a line from the two end stars of the bowl and see if it lands on Regulus. Sketch both shapes in the Fieldbook with the line between them. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Regulus and the Moon.** Pick a waxing month. Watch, over several evenings, as the Moon drifts eastwards past Regulus. Note where the Moon sits relative to Regulus on three consecutive nights — the drift is the ecliptic, showing itself. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Name the lion.** See if you can learn Regulus and Denebola — what each name means and which end of the lion they mark. Point them out to someone and see which one sticks first. *Grade: Core.*
1. **The Leo Triplet.** If you can lay hands on a small telescope or a pair of big binoculars, see if you can find M65 and M66 below the lion’s belly. Sketch what you see — three faint smudges is a perfect answer. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Spring clock.** On the first clear night of each spring month, sketch Leo’s position at the same time. Watch it drift from east to south to west as spring ripens into summer. *Grade: Stretch.*
