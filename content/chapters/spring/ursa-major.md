## Story

*A spring chapter, but in the UK the Plough is up every night of the year. Read on any clear evening when you can step outside and point at it.*

**Callisto the Bear (Greek).** Zeus loved the nymph Callisto. Hera, furious, turned her into a bear. Years later Callisto's son Arcas, now grown and a hunter, came across the bear in the forest and drew his bow — not knowing it was his mother. To stop a tragedy, Zeus caught them both up into the sky: Callisto as **Ursa Major**, the Great Bear, and Arcas as **Ursa Minor** or as **Boötes**, forever following behind her. One version says Hera had the last word and forbade the pair from ever dipping into the sea — which is why, from mid-northern latitudes, the two bears never set. They circle the pole all night, every night.

**Meskhetyu — the bull's foreleg (Egypt).** To the ancient Egyptians the seven bright stars were not a bear but a bull's thigh: *Meskhetyu*. It was carved on the ceilings of tombs as part of the northern sky. In Egyptian funerary ritual, a priest touched a ceremonial adze shaped like the thigh to the mouth of the mummy — the "opening of the mouth" — so the dead could speak, eat, and see in the afterlife. The tool and the constellation share a shape and a name.

**The celestial bear hunt (Mi'kmaq, Wabanaki Confederacy, northeastern North America — referenced).** Several Indigenous nations of northeastern North America tell a connected story: the four stars of the Plough's "bowl" are a bear, and the three stars of the handle are hunters chasing it around the pole through the seasons. In autumn, when the bear stands low on the horizon, it "bleeds," and the reds and browns of the autumn forest are its blood. This is a teaching story with its own keepers. Seek it out directly — Mi'kmaq and Abenaki writers have published it carefully; we do not retell it here.

**Arab star names.** The Plough's seven stars all carry Arabic names. From the end of the handle inwards: **Alkaid** (*al-Qā'id*, "the leader"), **Mizar** (*al-Mi'zar*, "the girdle"), **Alioth** (*al-Jawn*, "the black horse" or possibly "the fat tail of the sheep" — scholars disagree), **Megrez** (*al-Maghriz*, "the root of the tail"), **Phecda** (*Fakhdh*, "the thigh"), **Merak** (*al-Marāqq*, "the loins"), **Dubhe** (*Dubb*, "bear" — the one star name that preserved the bear). Next to Mizar, faint but visible to sharp eyes, is **Alcor** (*Suhā*, "the forgotten one"). Arabic tradition held that the pair were a medieval eye test.

## Sky

The Plough is the seven-star spoon or plough shape that dominates the northern sky. In Britain it is *circumpolar* — it never sets. In spring it swings high overhead in the evening. In autumn it is low on the northern horizon. Same seven stars, always the same shape, rotating slowly around Polaris as the year turns.

- **The Bowl** — four stars making a lopsided quadrilateral: Dubhe and Merak at the outer edge, Phecda and Megrez nearer the handle.
- **The Handle** — three stars curving out from the bowl: Alioth, then Mizar (with faint Alcor right beside it), then Alkaid at the tip.
- **Mizar and Alcor** — on a dark night, look carefully at the middle star of the handle. Beside it, very close, is a fainter star. Two stars where most eyes see one. With a telescope, Mizar splits again into a pair — and those are each doubles in turn. Mizar and Alcor together are six stars bound by gravity.

**The pointer stars — finding Polaris.** Take the two stars at the outer end of the bowl (Dubhe and Merak). Draw a straight line from Merak through Dubhe and keep going, about five times the distance between them. You land on **Polaris**, the pole star. That line is the single most useful trick in the Northern Hemisphere night sky. Learn it once, forever.

**The arc and the spike.** From the bend of the handle, trace the curve of the handle onward — past Alkaid — and keep going. The arc lands on a bright orange star: **Arcturus**, in Boötes. Keep spiking straight on past Arcturus and you reach **Spica**, in Virgo. The old rhyme: *arc to Arcturus, spike to Spica*.

**Dropping through the bowl.** Punch a hole in the bottom of the bowl (Phecda and Merak) and let the water fall south. It lands on **Leo**. Three tricks, three parts of the spring sky, from one asterism.

## Science

- **Mizar** was the first double star ever discovered with a telescope (Giovanni Battista Riccioli, around 1650). Its companion Alcor is visible to the naked eye. Modern observations show the Mizar–Alcor system is actually **six stars** orbiting each other in a gravitationally bound group.
- Most of the stars in the Plough are actually moving through space together. They were born in the same stellar nursery about 500 million years ago and are drifting together as the **Ursa Major Moving Group**. Dubhe and Alkaid — the two end stars — are *not* part of the group; they just happen to lie along the line of sight. In a hundred thousand years the shape of the Plough will be noticeably distorted as the five central stars drift on while the two ends go their own way.
- **Polaris itself is not quite at the pole.** It sits about 0.7° off true north — close enough to be useful, not so close that it doesn't move. In a long-exposure photograph you can see it tracing a small circle around the true pole.
- The Plough stars are all between 80 and 125 light-years away. Close, as stars go. The light on your face tonight left them within a human lifetime ago.

## Skill — Finding North, Telling the Season

- **The pointer stars find Polaris.** The single most important sky-skill in Britain. Practise it until you can do it in any weather, at any time of year, within five seconds of stepping outside.
- **The Plough is a yearly clock.** Its position at the same hour of night changes through the year. In spring evenings it stands high overhead, handle to the east. In summer it descends in the northwest. In autumn it is low in the north, scooped upright. In winter it rises in the northeast, upside-down. Learn these four positions and you can read the month from a glance.
- **Arc-to-Arcturus, spike-to-Spica.** Two stars, two constellations, found from the Plough alone. A full quarter of the spring sky opens up from this one signpost.
- **Through the bowl to Leo.** The third Plough trick — drop a line through the bottom of the bowl and you land on Regulus. Combined with the arc and the spike, the Plough gives you Leo, Boötes, and Virgo on a single spring night.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations under spring skies. The Plough is kind — it waits for you every clear evening.*

1. **Find Polaris.** On any clear night, step outside and see if you can find the Plough. Use Merak and Dubhe as pointers and follow the line up. Is the star you land on really north? Check with a compass afterwards and see how close you got. *Grade: Starter. Unlocks the rest of this chapter's field triggers. Counts towards the Spring Capstone.*
1. **Mizar and Alcor.** On the darkest night you can reach, look carefully at the middle star of the Plough's handle. Can you split the two stars with the naked eye? This was once a medieval eye test — see if you'd have passed. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Name the seven.** See if you can learn Alkaid, Mizar, Alioth, Megrez, Phecda, Merak, Dubhe — in order along the shape. Bonus round: what does each Arabic root actually mean? *Grade: Core.*
1. **Arc to Arcturus.** On a spring evening, trace the arc of the handle on and on until you land on Arcturus. Notice its colour against the Plough stars — it is visibly orange. Sketch the arc, and see what else sits along it. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Four-season Plough.** Over the course of a whole year, sketch the Plough at the same time (say 10pm) on the first clear night of each season. Four little sketches in all. You will have drawn the wheel of the year turning on the pole. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Compass-free north.** On a walk, try using only the Plough to set your bearing — no compass. Check at the end with a compass. How much did the sky get you? *Grade: Stretch. Counts towards the No-North Week challenge.*
