## Story

*A spring chapter, paired with the Plough. Read on a clear spring evening when the Plough is high and the arc of its handle lands squarely on a warm, orange star.*

**The guardian of the bear (Greek).** The Greeks saw Boötes as a herdsman or ploughman, driving the bear (Ursa Major) around the pole of the sky. The name *Arcturus* is Greek: *Arktouros*, "guardian of the bear." One story makes Boötes **Arcas**, the son of Callisto — the hunter who was about to shoot his mother before Zeus swept them both into the sky. In the sky he is still following her, forever, but no longer hunting. Another tradition made him Icarius, a farmer who gave wine to his neighbours, was killed by them when they thought he had poisoned them, and was placed in the sky in thanks — with his dog Procyon and his daughter Erigone nearby. Either way, Boötes is a man walking behind the bear with a tool in his hand.

**Al-Simāk al-Rāmih — the lofty lance-bearer (Arabic).** Medieval Arab astronomers saw a tall man holding a spear. Arcturus was **Al-Simāk al-Rāmih**, "the lofty one with a lance." Its counterpart, **Spica** over in Virgo, was *Al-Simāk al-A'zal*, "the unarmed lofty one." The two Simāks were visual landmarks, bright and alone in their quarter of the sky. When you arc from the Plough to Arcturus and spike on to Spica, you are walking from the armed lofty one to the unarmed one — old geography, same stars.

**Hōkūleʻa — star of gladness (Hawai‘i — referenced, not taught).** In the Hawaiian tradition, Arcturus is **Hōkūleʻa**, the "star of gladness." It is a **zenith star** for the Hawaiian Islands: at Hawai‘i's latitude it passes directly overhead. Polynesian navigators sailed for Hawai‘i by watching for Hōkūleʻa to climb to the zenith. In 1976, a double-hulled voyaging canoe named *Hōkūleʻa* sailed from Hawai‘i to Tahiti using only traditional wayfinding — no instruments — and reawakened a continent of voyaging knowledge that colonisers had declared dead. That canoe has since circled the Earth. We do not teach this wayfinding. We point you at the Polynesian Voyaging Society and at Nainoa Thompson to hear it from the people who kept it alive.

## Sky

Boötes (say *boh-OH-teez*) is a kite-shaped constellation that rides just east of the Plough. Its brightest star, **Arcturus**, is one of the warmest-coloured stars in the whole sky and one of the easiest to name once you know it.

- **The Kite.** Boötes is a narrow kite or ice-cream cone standing on its point. Arcturus is the point. Above Arcturus a diamond of fainter stars makes the body of the kite.
- **Arcturus** is orange, bright, and alone in its part of the sky. It is the fourth-brightest star visible from Earth (after Sirius, Canopus, and Alpha Centauri — and we can't see the last two from Britain, so in the UK it's third). It is distinctly coloured — not red like Betelgeuse, not pure white — a steady, warm amber.
- **The Y of Coma Berenices**, a faint scatter of stars, sits just west of the kite. On a dark night it looks like a little handful of stardust.

**How to find it — the arc from the Plough.** Find the Plough. Trace the curve of its handle. Keep the curve going off the end, in the same arc, and you will land on Arcturus. It is impossible to miss — the orange colour gives it away. Keep going along the same line, straight on from Arcturus, and you reach **Spica**, a hard blue-white star low in the south. The mnemonic every backyard astronomer learns: **arc to Arcturus, spike to Spica.**

**Boötes as a spring clock.** Arcturus climbs the eastern sky in April, passes high overhead in May and June, and drifts into the west through summer. If Arcturus is high overhead at 10pm, spring is tipping into summer. If you can only just find it in the west after sunset, autumn is coming.

## Science

- **Arcturus is a red giant**, an older, cooler, more swollen kind of star than the Sun. It is about 25 times the Sun's diameter and 170 times its luminosity. It is 37 light-years away — close in stellar terms — and it is burning helium in its core, a late phase of stellar life. Our Sun will go through something similar in about five billion years.
- **Arcturus is a sprinter.** It is not moving with the rest of the local stars — it's cutting across them at nearly 150 km/s relative to the Sun, part of a population called the *thick disc*, thought to be older stars from an ancient merger with a smaller galaxy. When you look at Arcturus, you are looking at a visitor passing through.
- **Arcturus opened the 1933 Chicago World's Fair.** Arcturus's light was focused by a photocell onto a switch that turned on the fair's floodlights. The light had (astronomers then thought) taken 40 years to reach Earth — meaning it had left Arcturus at the time of the *previous* Chicago fair, in 1893. (The modern distance is 37 light-years, not 40, but the symbolism was beautiful.)
- **Coma Berenices** (the hair of Queen Berenice) is a true open cluster, about 280 light-years away — one of the closest to us — and its faint stars are drifting together as a real group.

## Skill — Latitudes, Horsemanship, Spring Signposts

- **Arc to Arcturus, spike to Spica.** One curve through three stars: Alkaid (end of the Plough's handle), Arcturus, Spica. Three constellations in one gesture. Practise until you can do it with a finger in three seconds.
- **Arcturus is a zenith star for Hawai‘i (~19°N).** If you ever find yourself sailing south through the Pacific, when Arcturus passes directly overhead at your darkest hour, you are at Hawai‘i's latitude. We will not sail there — but the principle is universal: any star's declination is the latitude at which it passes overhead. The sky gives you latitude for free if you know how to read it.
- **Boötes marks the late-spring sky.** On a mild May evening, Arcturus straight up means the Plough is in the northwest, Leo is sliding down the western sky, and the Summer Triangle is just clearing the eastern horizon. The handle of the Plough points at the year.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations to follow the arc. Warm evenings are coming — take them outside with you.*

1. **Arc to Arcturus.** On any clear spring evening, find the Plough, trace the arc of the handle, and see where you land. That's Arcturus. Really look at the colour. Note the date, the time, and what colour you'd call it. *Grade: Starter. Unlocks the rest of this chapter's field triggers.*
1. **Spike to Spica.** On the same evening, keep the arc going past Arcturus and carry on until you reach Spica, low in the south. Sketch the arc through all three stars. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Name the kite.** See if you can learn Arcturus, Izar, Seginus, Nekkar, Muphrid — the five stars of the kite — and where each one sits. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Izar as a double.** With a small telescope on a steady night, find Izar (the upper-left corner of the kite). It splits into a yellow-and-blue double, sometimes called *Pulcherrima*, "the most beautiful." See if you agree with the name, and sketch what you see. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Read a latitude.** From Arcturus's declination (+19° 10'), see if you can work out the latitude at which Arcturus would pass directly overhead. Compare the answer to Hawai‘i. Write the number down. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Spring clock.** On the first clear night of each spring month, sketch Arcturus's position at 10pm. Watch it climb from east to overhead as the season ripens. *Grade: Stretch.*
