## Story

*A spring chapter, a companion to the Plough. The seven stars are the same; the language is different. Read both chapters together once and the sky will hold two lives at the same time.*

**The Seven Sages.** In the Hindustani sky the seven bright stars of what the British call the Plough are the **Saptarṣi** — the **seven sages** (*sapta* = seven, *ṛṣi* = sage). They were the seven *mind-born* sons of Brahmā: **Marīci, Vasiṣṭha, Atri, Pulastya, Pulaha, Kratu**, and **Aṅgiras**. They are the rishis of the *Ṛigveda* — the visionary poets to whom the oldest hymns were revealed — and they were placed in the northern sky to keep watch over the world from above. The constellation is named for them in the *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* (c. 800 BCE), where it is also called *Ṛkṣa* — "the bears" — a pre-existing name that the Greek word *arctos* and the English "Ursa" both inherit, distantly. The Plough is not a translation of the Greek bear; the Hindustani reading and the Greek reading both inherit a much older cognate name from a shared linguistic root.

**Vasiṣṭha and Arundhatī — the wedding stars.** Walk along the handle of the Plough to the middle star: in the Greek sky this is **Mizar**, with faint **Alcor** beside it. In the Hindustani sky this is **Vasiṣṭha** — one of the seven sages — with his wife **Arundhatī**, the only woman among the rishis, walking beside him. They are the closest visible pair of "married" stars in the sky, and they appear in a Hindu wedding ceremony in a small but unbroken way: at the **saptapadī**, the seven steps the bride and groom take around the sacred fire, the priest may invite the couple to step outside (or face the direction of the Saptarshi if it is daytime) and look up at Vasiṣṭha and Arundhatī. The two stars stand for the constancy of the marriage — two lights bound, walking together. A first-century Sanskrit text, the *Gṛhya Sūtras*, names this gesture; a wedding next month somewhere in India will repeat it.

**Dhruva — the steadfast one.** Above the Saptarshi, off the end of the pointer stars, is **Dhruva** — the unmoving star. We meet him in his own chapter (autumn). The Saptarshi circle Dhruva all night, every night; the sky turns; Dhruva does not. The story of how the boy Dhruva became the pole star is told in the **Bhāgavata Purāṇa** and in the **Viṣṇu Purāṇa**, and it is one of the most-loved bedtime stories in South Asia. We will tell it then. For now: the line from the pointer stars (the front of the Plough's bowl) extended out finds Dhruva — exactly as it finds Polaris, exactly the same star, one sky.

**Krittikā — the foster-mothers.** Below and to the south, in winter and early spring, are the **Krittikā** — the Pleiades. In the older form of the story, the Krittikā are the six wives of six of the Saptarshi, who fostered the war-god *Kārttikeya* when the gods could not entrust him to anyone else; the seventh wife, **Arundhatī**, refused and remained with her husband Vasiṣṭha. The Krittikā became the Pleiades; the seventh wife stayed at her husband's side. Look up: the six Pleiades visible to most eyes, and the seventh "lost"; the six Saptarshi sages with their wives gone, and Vasiṣṭha alone with Arundhatī. The geometry of the sky and the geometry of the story interlock.

**A note on framing.** The Saptarshi are not a literary figure or a dead myth. They are named in marriage rites, in genealogies, in Sanskrit grammars, in classical music nomenclature, and in calendrical eras: **Saptarṣi Kāla** is a Hindu cyclical era based on the precession of the seven sages, used in Kashmir and parts of Punjab into the 19th century and revived in academic publications today. This is a live system. We name what we see, in the words used to name it, and we point you at the *pañcāṅga* and at the texts.

## Sky

The seven bright stars of the Saptarshi are exactly the seven bright stars of the Plough. The shape is the same; the figure is different. Here is the Hindustani assignment of names along the shape, walking from the bowl down the handle, sage by sage:

- **Kratu** — the leading star of the bowl (Greek **Dubhe**; the outer pointer). The sage of perception and intelligence.
- **Pulaha** — opposite Kratu, at the inner front of the bowl (Greek **Merak**; the second pointer).
- **Pulastya** — the back-bottom of the bowl (Greek **Phecda**).
- **Atri** — the back-top of the bowl, where the handle joins (Greek **Megrez**).
- **Aṅgiras** — the first star of the handle (Greek **Alioth**).
- **Vasiṣṭha** — the middle star of the handle, with his wife **Arundhatī** beside him (Greek **Mizar** with **Alcor**).
- **Marīci** — the tip of the handle (Greek **Alkaid**).

Different scholarly sources disagree on a few of these assignments — some swap Kratu and Pulaha, some put Marīci at the bowl rather than the tip. The seven names are constant; the order along the shape has minor variants. The traditional rule is: the seven sages walk; Vasiṣṭha is always in the middle of the handle so that Arundhatī can be pointed out at weddings.

**The pointer to Dhruva works in both traditions.** A line from Pulaha through Kratu (= Merak through Dubhe) extended five times the gap finds Dhruva (= Polaris). The trick is the same; the names along the line are translations of each other.

**Vasiṣṭha and Arundhatī.** On a dark night, look hard at Mizar (the middle star of the handle). Arundhatī sits right beside it — a fainter star, very close. If you can split them with your naked eye, you have keen sight; in many South Asian wedding traditions, the priest will say so. (Cosmically: Mizar and Alcor are about a fifth of a light-year apart, both within a much larger gravitationally bound multiple system. Six stars, walking together. The metaphor is observational fact.)

## Science

- **The Saptarshi Era / Saptarṣi Kāla** is a cyclical Hindu era in which each of the seven sages "dwells" in a nakshatra (lunar mansion) for 100 years before moving to the next. The full cycle is 2,700 years. It is a calendrical reflection of the slow precession of the equinoxes — the same astronomical phenomenon that the Greek astronomer Hipparchus described around 130 BCE — and is attested in Indian sources from a comparable period.
- **The Saptarshi as bears, and a deep linguistic link.** The Sanskrit word *ṛkṣa* (bear) — used as a name for these stars — and the Greek *arktos* (bear) and the English "Arctic" all descend from the same Proto-Indo-European root *h₂ŕ̥tḱos*. Several cultures across the Indo-European family seem to have inherited the same "the seven bright stars near the pole are a bear" association from a common source perhaps 5,000 years ago. The same association turns up *independently* in Mi'kmaq tradition in northeastern North America and in Siberian traditions — which makes scholars wonder whether the bear-and-pole-stars connection is older still and human-deep, or whether it is independent reinvention. The question is open.
- **Vasiṣṭha and Arundhatī, observationally.** Mizar is a binary star system of two pairs: A1, A2 around B1, B2 — four stars in a tight gravitational dance. Alcor is itself a pair (Alcor A and a faint red dwarf). All six are gravitationally bound to one another at a distance of about 83 light-years from us. The "wedding stars" are *literally* two close partners, surrounded by family.
- **Precession and the wandering pole.** Dhruva is currently within 0.7° of the true pole, but the pole is not fixed: it traces a 26,000-year circle through the sky. Five thousand years ago the pole was near **Thuban** in Draco — the star the Egyptians sighted down the airshaft of the Great Pyramid. In about 13,000 years it will be near **Vega** in Lyra. Dhruva is not eternal as a pole star. The *idea* of a pole star is. The Hindustani astronomical tradition was aware of precession by the time of the *Sūrya Siddhānta* and recorded it; the texts know that Dhruva, the steadfast, is steadfast on a human timescale, not a cosmic one.

## Skill — Reading One Sky in Two Languages, and Finding North in Both

- **The pointer trick is universal.** From Merak (= Pulaha) through Dubhe (= Kratu), five times the gap, you land on Polaris (= Dhruva). The geometry transcends the language. Practise it once in each set of names and you have memorised the most useful navigational fact in the Northern Hemisphere sky in two registers.
- **The Saptarshi as a clock.** Like the Plough — same stars, same motion — the Saptarshi turn around Dhruva once per night and once per year. In spring evenings they ride high overhead; in autumn evenings they sit low in the north. Either reading gives you the season.
- **Spotting Arundhatī.** On a dark night, find Mizar in the handle. Look for a fainter star *very close* beside it. If you see two, you have split Vasiṣṭha and Arundhatī by eye. In the Hindu wedding tradition, this is a small private blessing — one couple looking up at another. Astronomically, it is also a six-star system seen as two points of light. Both readings are correct.
- **Walking with the sages.** The Saptarshi was used as a literal wayfinding aid across the Indian subcontinent before maps became common — for night travel by foot, by camel, by river. Knowing the four cardinal directions from the Plough was as essential to a Punjabi caravaneer as it was to a Hebridean fisherman. The same stars; two languages of the same skill.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations under spring skies. The Saptarshi are circumpolar — they never set from Britain. Take them slowly.*

1. **Find the seven sages.** On any clear evening, find the seven bright stars and read them in order: Kratu, Pulaha, Pulastya, Atri, Aṅgiras, Vasiṣṭha, Marīci. Sketch the shape with each Sanskrit name beside the English (or Greek) one. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Vasiṣṭha and Arundhatī.** On a dark night, see if you can split Mizar and Alcor with the naked eye. If you can, you have just performed a small piece of a Hindu wedding rite, in solidarity. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Pointer to Dhruva.** Extend the line from Pulaha (Merak) through Kratu (Dubhe) and find Dhruva (Polaris). Verify with a compass tomorrow. Note in the Fieldbook how close you got. *Grade: Core. Counts toward the Spring Capstone.*
1. **One star, three names.** Take Mizar / Vasiṣṭha and learn its three names — Greek (*Mizar*), Sanskrit (*Vasiṣṭha*), and Arabic (*al-Miʾzar*, "the girdle"). Say each aloud once outside, with the star in view. *Grade: Core.*
1. **The wheel of the seven.** Over a year, sketch the Saptarshi at the same time of night (say 10pm) on the first clear night of each season. Four sketches. The wheel turns; Dhruva does not. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **The Saptarshi era.** Look up your year (2026 in the Gregorian calendar) in the Saptarshi Kāla. Note which nakshatra each of the seven sages is "dwelling in" right now. Write it on the Year Map of your Fieldbook. *Grade: Stretch.*
