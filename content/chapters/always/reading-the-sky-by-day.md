## Story

*A short piece for a cloud-watching afternoon.*

**Luke Howard, the chemist who named the clouds.** In 1802, in a lecture theatre in London, a Quaker pharmacist named Luke Howard stood up and did something no one had ever done before: he gave the clouds names. *Cumulus* — heap. *Stratus* — layer. *Cirrus* — curl of hair. And combinations: *cumulonimbus*, heap-rain. *Stratocumulus*, layered-heap. Before Howard, clouds had no classification — they were just *the sky*, different every day, a thing you could not speak of precisely. After Howard, a sailor in Liverpool and a farmer in Aberdeenshire and a student in Calcutta could all mean the same thing when they said *cirrus*. Goethe wrote him a poem. Constable painted every cloud in his paintings *by Howard's names*. It is one of those small, immense moments when a person hands the world a new vocabulary.

## Sky

**The ten cloud genera, from highest to lowest.**

*High — above 6 km. Thin, cold, made of ice crystals.*
- **Cirrus** — the "mare's tails." White streaks, wispy, fibrous. Often a sign of weather changing within 24 hours.
- **Cirrostratus** — a thin sheet high up, through which the Sun or Moon makes a *halo* (a 22° ring). A halo often means rain or snow within a day.
- **Cirrocumulus** — tiny white ripples, "mackerel sky."

*Medium — 2 to 6 km. Grey or blue-grey, may hide the Sun as a dim disc.*
- **Altostratus** — grey sheet, Sun behind a ground-glass window. Often precedes steady rain.
- **Altocumulus** — white or grey patches and rolls, shadowed underneath. Common on a changeable day.

*Low — below 2 km. The clouds we know best.*
- **Stratus** — low grey blanket. On hills this is just *fog you happen to be inside*.
- **Stratocumulus** — lumpy low grey sheet, patches of blue between. Britain's default sky.
- **Nimbostratus** — the rain-sheet. Dark, thick, featureless. The sky when it "rains all day."
- **Cumulus** — the cotton-wool cloud of a fair summer day. Flat bottom, cauliflower top.
- **Cumulonimbus** — the storm cloud. A cumulus that has grown into a towering anvil. Thunder, hail, heavy rain, lightning. If you see one up-wind of you, take note.

**Haloes, rainbows, sundogs.**
- *22° halo* — a ring around the Sun or Moon when cirrostratus ice crystals bend the light. "Halo tonight, rain tomorrow" is often true.
- *Sundogs (parhelia)* — bright patches 22° to the left and right of the Sun, caused by the same ice crystals in flatter orientation. Most common in winter on low sun.
- *Rainbows* — opposite the Sun, 42° from your shadow, inside a raindrop shower.
- *Crepuscular rays* — the shafts of sunlight that fan down through gaps in cloud. Parallel in reality, made to fan by perspective.

**Birds as weather instruments.**
- Swifts and swallows flying *low* are chasing insects that have been flattened low by humid, falling pressure. Bad weather coming.
- Gulls going *inland* in numbers often mean a storm over the sea they know about before you do.
- Crows settling early — wind rising.
- Dawn chorus unusually quiet — a front moving through.

**Smoke as a weather instrument.**
- Smoke that *rises straight* — calm, high pressure, often stable weather.
- Smoke that *drifts flat* — a temperature inversion, often meaning fog or still, trapped air.
- Smoke that *tumbles and twists* — gusty wind, unsettled weather.
- Smoke that suddenly *changes direction* — a front has arrived.

## Science

- Clouds form when moist air rises and cools until the water vapour condenses onto tiny particles (*condensation nuclei* — dust, salt, pollen). Cool warm-moist air enough and it always makes cloud.
- A cloud is *heavy*. A small cumulus might weigh 500 tonnes. It floats because that weight is spread through a huge volume of air, and the droplets are continually replenished by updrafts even as they fall.
- The *dew point* is the temperature at which the air's water vapour starts to condense. Forecasters watch the gap between temperature and dew point: if they close, fog forms.
- *Sunset is red* because at low angles, sunlight travels through more atmosphere and the shorter (blue) wavelengths are scattered out, leaving red. Dust and smoke deepen the effect. After a volcanic eruption, sunsets can be spectacular worldwide for months.

## Skill — Reading a day's weather from the sky

- **Morning cloud census.** Look up on waking. What clouds are there? Which way are they moving? That is your first guess at the day.
- **Build or break.** If cumulus clouds are *building* through the morning — getting taller, flatter bottoms darker — expect showers or thunderstorms by afternoon, especially in summer. If they're *breaking up* as the day goes on, fair.
- **Watch the high before the low.** Cirrus appearing in a previously clear sky often means a warm front is coming — 12 to 24 hours out. Cirrus → cirrostratus → altostratus → nimbostratus is the classic sequence before steady rain. Spot it early and you know to pack the waterproof.
- **Wind backing means worsening.** In the Northern Hemisphere, a wind that shifts anticlockwise (e.g. SW → S → SE) usually means weather is deteriorating. A wind that veers clockwise (SW → W → NW) usually means a front has passed and things are improving.
- **Listen to the birds.** The swift is a more honest forecaster than the evening news. Trust a low swallow over the app.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Five invitations to keep an eye on the sky. Pick one you fancy and see what the weather is about to tell you.*

1. **Cloud Census.** For one week, at breakfast and at teatime, see how many of Luke Howard's cloud names you can put to the sky. Jot down what weather turned up next. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Halo Hunt.** Next time the sky goes pale-hazy-white with a dim Sun or Moon, keep an eye out for a 22° halo. (Handy trick: thumb-tip to little-finger at arm's length is roughly 20°.) Make a prediction for the next day's weather, then check how you did. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Swift Barometer.** In summer, notice how high the swifts are flying each evening, and see what the weather does over the next 24 hours. Is there a pattern hiding in there? *Grade: Core.*
1. **Smoke Signals.** Over a week, watch the smoke from a chimney or a bonfire each morning. Sort the behaviours — straight, flat, tumbling — against what the weather actually did. Any rules emerge? *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Name a Sky.** On one beautiful day, sketch the whole sky above your head, label every cloud type you can name, and give the sky a name of your own. *Grade: Starter.*
