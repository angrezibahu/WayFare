## Story

*Read aloud on a windy afternoon, ideally on a ridge with hawthorns leaning at you.*

In the 1950s a hill-farmer in Cumbria was asked, by a young Ordnance Survey cartographer with a clipboard, how he knew the prevailing wind direction in his fields without an anemometer. He pointed without speaking at a thorn tree — bent like an old hand — at a line of stone-walled scrub running east-of-north-east, at a single rowan with all its branches on the lee side, and at a flag of grey wool caught on a barbed-wire fence pointing the same way. *Fifty years of weather*, he said, *written all over the field*. The cartographer wrote it down word for word in his notebook; the notebook is in the Cumbria archives still.

The trees on a British hillside are the longest-running weather diary in the country. Read them, and you can name the prevailing wind at any place in Britain or Ireland without lifting a finger. This chapter is how to read them.

## Land

**The four readable wind-clues, in order of reliability.**

1. **Flag-trees.** A *flag-tree* is an exposed tree whose canopy has grown into a one-sided, flag-like shape — branches reaching out only on the lee side, the windward side bare or stunted. Look at any ridge, sea-cliff, or hilltop in Britain. Most exposed mature trees are flag-trees. **The flag points downwind.** In the UK, that means most flag-trees flag towards the **north-east**, because the prevailing wind is from the south-west.
1. **Tree lean.** A mature, exposed tree growing in wind will lean *away* from the wind. The lean is usually subtle — five to ten degrees — but on hawthorns, rowans, and exposed beeches on coasts and ridges it is unmistakable. **The tree leans downwind.**
1. **Wind-pruned scrub.** Hedges and scrub patches in exposed places have a clipped, wedge-shaped profile, low on the windward side and rising on the lee. The shape is a fossil of the wind.
1. **Cloud movement.** Pick a single cloud and watch it for thirty seconds. Note where it tracked across the sky relative to landmarks (a chimney, a treetop). That is the **wind at cloud height** — usually one to three kilometres up. It is *not* the same as the wind at ground level: it can be 30° to 60° different, and is often steadier.

**The British prevailing wind, in one sentence.** Across most of the British Isles, the prevailing wind is from the **south-west** — a maritime air-stream off the Atlantic, warm and damp. In a few places it is more westerly (Hebrides) or southerly (parts of the south coast), and on the east coast a north-easterly is much more common in spring and early summer than the average suggests. **Look up your own region's prevailing wind in a Met Office climate page; do not trust the average if you live somewhere with a strong local effect.**

**Smoke and the immediate wind.**
- Smoke that **rises straight** — calm, often high pressure, often stable.
- Smoke that **drifts flat** — a temperature inversion, often fog or trapped air.
- Smoke that **tumbles and twists** — gusty, unsettled.
- Smoke that *suddenly changes direction* — a front has arrived.

## Science

- The British Isles sit in the **mid-latitude westerly wind belt** of the Northern Hemisphere — the global circulation drives air from west to east at our latitude. The Atlantic Ocean to the west is the source of warm, moist air; cold continental air arrives from the north-east.
- A **flag-tree's** asymmetry comes from years of wind-pruning of the windward shoots — buds and tips on the windward side die back from desiccation and salt (on coasts), and the tree concentrates growth on the lee side where the air is calmer and damper.
- **Wind shear** — the wind at 1 km altitude is usually stronger than the wind at the surface, and rotated **clockwise** in the Northern Hemisphere (anti-clockwise in the Southern) by the **Ekman spiral**. So if surface wind is from the south-west, the cloud-level wind is often more westerly. Pilots, sailors, and gliders know this; walkers should too.
- A *prevailing* wind is a **statistical** thing — the most common direction, not the only one. On any given day the wind can be from any quarter; the trees record the *long-run* preference, and the long run is what shaped them.

## Skill — Putting it together in the field

- **Stand at the edge of a wood**, in an exposed place, and read the trees first: which way are they flagging? Lock that direction in. That is your *prevailing wind heading*.
- **Now look up at the clouds.** Are they moving the same way? Likely *roughly* — but slightly clockwise of the surface wind, in the UK.
- **Now feel the wind on your face.** Is it consistent with the trees? On most days yes; on some days the surface wind has turned sharply against the prevailing — that *itself* is a strong weather signal (often, deteriorating weather).
- **Cross-check with a known landmark.** The trees lean north-east, you know your prevailing wind is south-westerly, therefore the trees are looking *away from south-west* — which means south-west is *behind them*. From there the cardinal directions follow.

In the British Isles this method gives you a rough compass without any instrument. In the Hebrides on a winter morning, with the trees flagged hard north-east and the cloud-bank racing the same way, you can know your bearing within 20° from the moment you step outside.

## Try It — The Wind-Flag and Cloud Survey

<div class="vibe vibe-try">

**A walk that reads the wind in two layers.** On a breezy afternoon, take a 30-minute walk that includes at least one exposed place — a ridge, a coastal path, the edge of a high field. With a compass and the Fieldbook in hand:

1. **Find five flag-trees.** For each, note its *flag direction* (which way the branches reach) with a compass. Sketch the canopy if you have time.
1. **Pick three single clouds**, one at a time, and watch each for at least 30 seconds. Note its *track* across the sky against a fixed landmark (a chimney, a hilltop). Convert each track into a compass bearing.
1. **Log the surface wind** with a wet finger, a tuft of grass thrown into the air, or by feel.

Lay all three readings out on one sheet. **Do the trees agree with each other?** Usually yes. **Do the clouds agree?** Usually yes — but rotated *clockwise* of the trees by 20°–40° (the Ekman spiral). **Does the surface wind agree with the trees?** Most days, yes. **If anything strongly disagrees, why?** Local turbulence? A shift of weather? A bend in the valley?

The single sheet of paper is now a small wind-survey of the place where you live, complete with cloud-level wind. Date and sign it; in twenty years it will be a record.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Four invitations to read the air.*

1. **Tree-Lean Survey.** *Grade: Stretch.* Do the survey above with ten flag-trees. *(Pairs with the deck card "The Tree-Lean Survey".)*
1. **Surface vs Sky.** *Grade: Core.* For a week, every morning, log the surface wind direction (by smoke, flags, tree-tops) and the cloud-level wind (by watching one cloud). Were they always offset? In which sense?
1. **Hometown Prevailing.** *Grade: Starter.* Find your nearest Met Office weather station online and look up the prevailing wind direction for your home town. Compare with the trees. Were they honest?
1. **A Wind Map of the Garden.** *Grade: Core.* On a windy day, walk slowly through the garden with a long ribbon on a stick. Note where the wind is fast, where it is slow, where it is turbulent. Sketch a small *wind-map*. The next time you sit outside, sit where the ribbon went still.
