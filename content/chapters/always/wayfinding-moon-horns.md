## Story

*Read aloud on a clear crescent evening, ideally with the Moon already in view.*

A sailor waiting for the tide on a Cornish quay in 1830 leans on the rail, watches the new Moon rise over the headland, and traces with his finger an imaginary line down through the two horns of the slim crescent. It strikes the water out beyond the harbour mouth. *South*, he thinks, *or near enough.* He has not heard of latitude or declination; he has not done a sum. He has a habit, learned from his father, who learned it from his.

The same gesture turns up in **Baden-Powell's** *Scouting for Boys* in 1908: draw a line through the horns, follow it to the horizon, you will find south "near enough for a Scout to walk by". And in the Arabic poems of the Bedouin, where the crescent of *Hilāl* is followed and named, the same geometry sits beneath the metaphor.

It is a humble trick, and a good one — but it deserves its caveats, which the postcard versions tend to forget. This chapter gives the trick its full hearing.

## Sky

**The basic horns-to-horizon line (Northern Hemisphere).**
1. Wait for a clear evening with a **crescent Moon** — first or last quarter is best, but anything from a thumbnail-thin sliver to half-Moon will do.
1. Imagine a straight line drawn through the two horn-tips of the crescent.
1. Carry that line straight down to the **horizon**.
1. The point where the line meets the horizon is *roughly* **south**.

It is best done with the Moon high in the sky, well clear of the horizon. A Moon that is itself near the horizon makes the geometry shallow and unreliable.

**The Southern Hemisphere version.** The same line points to *roughly north*. The Moon, like the Sun, swings the wrong way round when you cross the equator.

**Why this works at all.** The lit side of the Moon **always faces the Sun**. The crescent's two horn-tips sit on the rim of the lit hemisphere, which is perpendicular to the Sun-Moon line. So a line through the two horns is a chord across the *terminator* (the day-night boundary on the Moon). Carry that chord to the horizon and you are pointing at the part of the sky most opposite the Sun. Around the time the Moon is up in the evening, that opposition direction is roughly south in the UK — the Sun has set in the west, the Moon is in the south-west or south, and "down through the horns" lands at south.

## Honest caveats — when the horns lie

Like every solar trick, this one has its quiet falsehoods. Tell a child the truth.

- **It is *roughly* south, not *exactly*.** The bearing can be off by **15° to 25°**, especially when the Moon is low or far north or south of the celestial equator (which it can be, by up to 28°).
- **The trick is best when the Moon is high.** A Moon a hand's-breadth above the horizon makes the line very shallow; a small error in your eye is a big error on the ground.
- **It works best around the quarter Moons.** A nearly-full Moon has very small horns and the line is hard to draw. A very thin crescent gives a clear line but is usually low in the sky.
- **It is no use in the tropics.** The Moon's path can put the lit side anywhere on the sky, and the south-or-north convention breaks down.
- **A compass is more accurate.** As ever, this is a check and a starter, not a navigational fix.

When the trick is at its best — a high half-Moon at mid-latitudes, on a clear evening — it can give south to within about 10°. That is good enough to know which way the path goes through a wood, not good enough to plot a course on the sea.

## Science

- The Moon orbits Earth at about **5° off** the plane of Earth's orbit (the **ecliptic**). Its declination — how far north or south of the celestial equator it sits — wanders between about **+28°** and **−28°** over an 18.6-year cycle. That is why the horns-trick scatter changes from year to year.
- The geometry: the *lit hemisphere* of the Moon faces the Sun directly. The terminator is a great circle perpendicular to the Earth-Moon-Sun angle. The two horns lie on that great circle on either side of the disc. A chord through them, projected to the local horizon, gives the *azimuth* of the bottom of the lit hemisphere, which is broadly the direction *opposite the Sun*.
- A *waxing* crescent has its horns pointing left (NH) — the chord is "horns up, lit side down-right". A *waning* crescent the mirror image. Either way, the *line through the horns* is the chord that you drop to the horizon.
- The line through the horns is also called the **Moon's terminator chord** or, on old voyaging notes, the **Moon's belly-line**.

## Skill — Using the Moon as a compass through the night

- **Always know which way the lit side points** — that direction is the Sun, even by day, even through cloud-thinned blue. The Moon-by-day lets a child find the Sun without looking at it.
- **Pair the horns trick with Polaris** if both are visible: the horns give south, Polaris gives north. They should agree (within ~10°).
- **Draw the Moon every night for a month**, the way the Moon Diary asks. After a fortnight, your eye will read the horn-angle as fluently as it reads a clock face.
- **Trust the trick at quarter-Moon, distrust it at full-Moon and at the very-thin crescent low on the horizon.** Honest gear knows its limits.

## Try It — Horns to Horizon, with Honesty

<div class="vibe vibe-try">

**The half-Moon test.** On the next first-quarter or last-quarter evening, go to a place with a clear, low horizon — a sea wall, a hilltop, a school playing-field with no trees on the south side. Find the Moon. Hold a length of string taut between the two horn-tips and let it sag straight to the ground. The point where the string-line meets the horizon is *your* south, by the Moon. Take a compass reading of that same point. Write down both bearings in the Fieldbook with a single sentence on what the weather and the horizon were doing.

Repeat once a fortnight for a season. The errors are not random — they are signed and seasonal, and a child who keeps the diary will discover, without being told, that the Moon's trick lies more in winter than in summer, and more low on the horizon than high. That discovery is the point of the diary.

</div>

## Stretch — Challenges

*Four invitations. Take them on whatever clear evenings the weather hands you.*

1. **Horns to South.** *Grade: Core.* Do the half-Moon test once. Note the bearing-difference between the Moon and the compass. *(Pairs with the deck card "Horns to Horizon".)*
1. **Horns Diary.** *Grade: Stretch.* Repeat the test on five different evenings across a season. Plot the errors. Is there a pattern by date? By Moon altitude?
1. **Moon-by-Day.** *Grade: Starter.* Find the daytime Moon in a blue sky and use the lit edge to point at where the Sun is. Confirm by glancing (carefully!) at the Sun afterwards. How close were you?
1. **Read the Belly-Line.** *Grade: Core.* Sketch the crescent Moon and *draw on the sketch* the chord through the horns and the line dropped to the horizon. Hand the sketch to someone who has never tried the trick — can they explain why it gives south?
