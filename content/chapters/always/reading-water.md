## Story

*Two short pieces, for any evening.*

**Hōkūleʻa and the swells.** The *Hōkūleʻa* is a double-hulled Polynesian voyaging canoe launched in 1976, sailed from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti without any instruments, to prove that the old wayfinding had worked. Her navigator, Mau Piailug of the Caroline Islands, read the swells with his body. He lay in the hull and felt them: a fast, short swell from the trade winds layered on a deeper, longer swell from a storm a thousand miles away. Islands deflect swells for hundreds of miles. Even unseen, an island rearranges the water around it. Mau could feel an island beyond the horizon in the pattern of the waves against the hull. This knowledge is not ours. It is alive and taught, carefully, to apprentices in the Pacific. Seek out the Polynesian Voyaging Society directly.

**The Thames was once a wetland.** Before the Victorians embanked it, the Thames sprawled wide and shallow. Streets in central London keep the names of streams that still run beneath them in pipes — *Fleet Street, Walbrook, Tyburn*. London was built at a tidal reach where ships could come in and a river could be crossed on foot at low tide. Every old city was first a place where you could cross the water or where the water stopped you. Rivers are the reason for roads.

## Land

**Water always goes downhill, but not always the quickest way.** A stream chooses resistance — whatever is softest, lowest, weakest. It finds the weakness in the land and exposes it. Follow a small stream across a field and you are following the gentle low of that field, the place a cow would walk, the place a fox does walk.

**A stream widens where it slows.** Where a stream slows (flat ground, a lake, an estuary), it drops its load — sand, gravel, silt — and spreads. Where it speeds up (narrow valley, steep drop), it cuts down. That is why mountain streams are stony and lowland rivers are muddy. Same river, different places.

**Meanders and oxbows.** A lowland river bends because a tiny kink in the course becomes self-amplifying: water on the outside of the bend goes faster, erodes more, bends more. Eventually a bend becomes so tight that the river cuts across its own neck and leaves a curved pond behind — an *oxbow lake*. You can see a whole life of a river on one OS map if you learn to look.

**Coasts talk.**
- *Sheltered bays* — sand, gentle, slow-moving water.
- *Exposed headlands* — cliffs, crash, salt spray inland for hundreds of metres.
- *Longshore drift* — the wind-driven current along a coast — piles sand at the downwind end of a beach. You can tell which way the prevailing wind blows by which end the sand is piled at.
- *Spits* (sand bars reaching across a bay) and *tombolos* (sand bars tying an island to shore) grow in the direction of drift.

**Tides.**
- Two high tides and two low tides every 24 hours and 50 minutes (that extra 50 minutes is why the Moon rises 50 minutes later each day — it's the same number).
- **Spring tides** are the biggest — at new Moon and full Moon, when Sun and Moon pull together. **Neap tides** are the smallest — at the quarter moons, when they pull at right angles. "Spring" has nothing to do with the season; it comes from *to spring up*.
- The tidal *range* — the height difference between high and low — varies wildly around Britain: 12m in the Severn (second largest in the world), 1m off Portland Bill. Always check the local tide table, never guess.
- **Rule of Twelfths.** Tide doesn't rise evenly. Between low and high it rises roughly: 1/12 of the total in the first hour, 2/12 in the second, 3/12 in the third, 3/12 in the fourth, 2/12 in the fifth, 1/12 in the sixth. So it rises fastest around the middle of the cycle. This is the rule that catches people on causeways.

## Science

- A *drainage basin* (or catchment) is all the land from which water flows into a single stream or river. Ridge lines separate basins. If a raindrop falls on the east side of the ridge it ends up in one river; a metre west it ends up in another. On OS maps you can trace catchments by following the contours.
- Tides are caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon (mostly) and the Sun (about half as much). The water on the Moon-side of Earth bulges towards it; the water on the opposite side bulges away, because *that* water is being pulled less than the solid Earth is. Hence two high tides, not one.
- Salt water and fresh water don't mix instantly. In an estuary, a layer of salt water slides in along the bottom under the fresh water going out on top. Pilots used to ride these layers deliberately — drop a weighted chain into the inbound salt wedge and you move upriver against the surface flow.

## Skill — Using water to navigate and stay safe

- **Downhill, downstream, down to people.** Lost in hill country without a compass? Follow water. It goes to bigger water, which goes to settled land. It is slow. It works.
- **Never wade what you can't see the bottom of.** Clear water you can judge. Peaty or muddy water, don't risk it.
- **Never cross a river above your knees alone.** A river above knee-height has more force than you imagine. If you must cross, face upstream, feet apart, stick as third leg, shuffle sideways.
- **Tides on causeways.** Before walking a tidal causeway (Holy Island, St Michael's Mount, Cramond Island), check: what time is low tide, what time is safe to cross by, and by what time must you be back. Build in an hour of slack. Rescue services are called out for tidal strandings more than almost anything else.
- **Rip currents at the beach.** A rip is a narrow current flowing *out* to sea through the surf. Signs: darker water, a gap in the waves, foam streaming out. If caught in one, do not swim against it. Swim sideways (parallel to shore) until you're out of it, then back in. Teach this in your family before a beach trip. It saves lives.

## Stretch — Challenges

1. **Source to Sea (Map).** On an OS map, trace a local stream from its source to where it joins a bigger river. How many fields, roads and villages does it cross? *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Tide Diary.** For a month, record the daily high-tide times for your nearest coast. Notice the pattern — how they shift roughly 50 minutes a day, how spring tides cluster at new and full Moon. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Estuary Walk.** Walk an estuary at low tide and again at high tide on the same day. Sketch the same view twice. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Rule of Twelfths in Practice.** On a slow-moving beach with a gentle gradient, mark the waterline every hour through a rising tide. Compare your six marks to the Rule of Twelfths. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Waterway Home.** Pretend you've no phone, no compass, no map. From a hilltop you don't know, find your way home using only the tilt of the ground and the streams. (Actually walk it — staying safe.) *Grade: Stretch.*
