## Story

*Read aloud at the back door at dusk, while the first stars come.*

The Polynesian wayfinders of the great voyaging canoes — the *Hokule'a* sails today in their tradition — held a **32-point star compass** entirely in memory. They knew, for every season and every direction, **which stars rise at which point on the horizon** and **which set at which point**. As one star climbed too high to use, another rose to take its place. A wayfinder could keep a true course across an empty Pacific by *the rising and setting of stars at the rim of the world*, hour after hour, night after night.

This chapter is not the Polynesian system — that is theirs to teach, and the *Polynesian Star Compass* chapter on this site points you to their own voices. But it is the same simple idea, at the smallest, family-sized scale: at **dusk**, the first stars appear while the horizon is still bright enough to see; at **dawn**, the last stars hang above a horizon already lighting. That short window — when sky and land are both legible — is when a single star, fixed against a fence post or a ridge-line, can give you a bearing as honest as any compass.

## Sky

**Why dusk and dawn are the navigator's hour.**

In full night, you can see the stars but the horizon is invisible — you cannot tell *where* on the rim a star stands. In full day, the horizon is clear but the stars are gone. The brief overlap — about **20–40 minutes** after sunset and again before sunrise (longer further from the equator) — is the **navigational twilight**. In that window:
- Bright stars and planets are visible,
- The horizon line is still drawn,
- Distant landmarks stand black against a glowing sky.

A bearing taken at twilight is a piece of pure natural navigation. Sailors took them for centuries.

**The simplest twilight bearing — Polaris.** In the Northern Hemisphere, on any clear night, **Polaris** sits within **0.7°** of the true celestial north pole. *Stand facing Polaris and you face true north.* At dusk, Polaris is one of the very first stars to appear; at dawn, it is one of the last to fade. Across most of Britain (lat 50°–58° N), Polaris is also high enough above the horizon (about 50°–58° altitude) to be visible above local hills and rooftops.

To set true north for a walk by Polaris at dusk:
1. Find the **Plough** (Ursa Major) — easy in the northern UK sky almost any evening.
1. The two end stars of its bowl point straight to **Polaris** (about five fist-widths along the line). See the Ursa Major chapter for full detail.
1. Drop a vertical line from Polaris to the horizon. **That point on the horizon is true north.**
1. Pick a landmark on that point (a tree, a chimney, a hilltop notch).
1. From now until full daylight, that landmark is your *true north peg*.

**Other twilight stars worth knowing.**
- **Venus** ("the morning star" / "the evening star") is the brightest dot in the dusk or dawn sky and is often visible for an hour either side of the Sun. Its bearing changes night to night, so use it as a *local* peg, not a fixed compass.
- **Sirius** — the brightest fixed star — rises about 16° south of due east in mid-winter UK skies and sets about 16° south of due west. Its rising or setting *azimuth* on any date can be looked up in Stellarium.
- **Orion's Belt** sits on the *celestial equator*. From anywhere on Earth, the Belt **rises near due east and sets near due west** — within a few degrees on a clear horizon. That alone makes Orion the single most useful seasonal compass in the winter sky.
- **Arcturus** — the brightest star in the British spring evening — has a known azimuth at any date and is high enough to read off most horizons.

**Honest caveats.**
- A star's *bearing on the horizon* depends on its **declination** (its fixed position in the sky) **and** the observer's **latitude**. The bearing of a rising star is the same every night for one observer; but a star rising due east at one latitude can rise far north of east at another.
- The Polynesian system, the Arab *kamāl*, and the European maritime almanac all encode the same fact: **a star compass is local**. Learn the rising bearings for your own latitude — the chapter on Polynesian wayfinding has a careful pointer here, but the system itself is theirs and you should read directly from Hawaiian and Maori sources.
- Twilight bearings require a *clear horizon*. In a wood, in a city, in fog, the trick fails.

## Science

- **Polaris** is currently within 0.7° of the celestial north pole because Earth's axis happens to point near it now. That changes — over **26,000 years**, the axis traces a great circle around the sky (precession), and other stars take Polaris's role at intervals. **Thuban** in Draco was the pole star four thousand years ago; **Vega** will be in twelve thousand years' time.
- **Civil twilight** is when the Sun is 0–6° below the horizon: roughly when the brightest planets and stars first appear. **Nautical twilight** is 6–12° below: the horizon is still discernible, most navigation stars are visible — *this is the twilight window for star-bearings.* **Astronomical twilight** is 12–18° below: the sky is almost dark.
- A star's **azimuth** at rising is determined by its **declination** δ and the observer's **latitude** φ:  cos(azimuth) = sin(δ) / cos(φ). For Sirius (δ ≈ −16.7°) at lat 52° N, the rising azimuth is about 105° — that is, 15° south of due east. The maths is straightforward; you can do it on the back of an envelope.

## Skill — A working twilight bearing

- **At dusk**, just as the first stars come out, *look around for Polaris first*. Once Polaris is fixed, choose a landmark on the *horizon below* it. That landmark is *true north* for the rest of the night, even when the horizon dissolves into dark.
- **At dawn**, find Polaris, Venus, or a known bright star *before* the sky lightens enough to drown them. Mark a landmark below each. The landmarks survive sunrise.
- **Pair with the Sun**. Once the Sun is up, the shadow stick or watch-as-compass takes over. The hand-off from twilight stars to the day-Sun is the navigator's continuous record.
- **Practise with Stellarium**. Set the date and time, set your home location, and watch the modelled sky show you exactly *where* a star will rise tomorrow. The model is honest; check yourself against it.

## Try It — The Twilight Bearing

<div class="vibe vibe-try">

**A clear-horizon evening.** Pick a clear evening with a good low northern horizon (a sea wall, a hilltop, the top of a school playing field). About **30 minutes after sunset**, stand outside and look north. Find the Plough; trace the pointer-stars to **Polaris**. **Mark, with your eye and a sketch, the landmark on the horizon directly below Polaris.** Sketch the landmark in the Fieldbook with the words *TRUE NORTH* beside it.

For the rest of the evening, that landmark is your peg. Walk anywhere, look back at the peg, and you know where north is. At full dark, even when the rest of the horizon is invisible, you will still find Polaris in the sky and the peg will still be on the horizon — except now the horizon you choose is *the silhouette of the trees* or *the rooflines* against the dim sky. Set north once, hold it for hours.

**For a dawn version**, do the same with Venus or Polaris before the eastern sky brightens. The peg you set will outlast the stars themselves.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Four invitations to read the rim of the world.*

1. **Polaris Peg.** *Grade: Core.* Set a Polaris peg at dusk and check it with a compass next morning. How close was your eye?
1. **Belt-Bearing.** *Grade: Stretch.* On a clear winter evening at twilight, find Orion's Belt and use it to set due east (rising) or due west (setting). Compare with a compass; what error?
1. **Venus Watch.** *Grade: Starter.* For a week of evenings, log Venus's bearing at first sighting (with a hand-stretched compass-bearing or by landmark). Watch how its bearing wanders.
1. **Star-Compass Sketch.** *Grade: Stretch.* For your home latitude, sketch a small *rising-and-setting compass*: where does Sirius rise? Where does Vega set? Use Stellarium to check your numbers. *(Refer to the Polynesian Star Compass chapter on this site for the deeper system — read from Polynesian voices directly, do not retell.)*
