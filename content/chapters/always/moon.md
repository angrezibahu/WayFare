## Story

*From several traditions — read one aloud tonight.*

**Chang’e (China).** Chang’e was once a woman who swallowed an elixir of immortality to keep it from thieves. She floated up and up until she came to rest on the Moon, where she still lives with a jade rabbit who pounds medicine in a mortar. On the Mid-Autumn Festival, families eat mooncakes and look up, and the rabbit is sometimes visible in the dark patches on the Moon’s face.

**Máni (Norse).** Máni is the Moon, a man who drives a chariot across the night sky, chased forever by the wolf Hati. When Hati catches him — during an eclipse — people once made noise to scare the wolf off and save the Moon.

**Tsukuyomi (Japan).** Tsukuyomi-no-Mikoto was born from the right eye of the great god Izanagi. He rules the night. He is cold and proper; his sister Amaterasu, the Sun, refuses to share the sky with him after a quarrel, which is why day and night never meet.

## Sky

The Moon is the easiest thing in the sky. It rises in the east and sets in the west, like the Sun — but roughly 50 minutes later each day, which is why sometimes it’s up at night and sometimes it’s out in the afternoon.

**The phases, simply:**

- **New Moon** — between us and the Sun; invisible.
- **Waxing Crescent** — a thin sliver in the evening sky, horns pointing away from the Sun.
- **First Quarter** — half-lit, up at sunset, sets at midnight.
- **Waxing Gibbous** — more than half, growing.
- **Full Moon** — opposite the Sun; rises as the Sun sets.
- **Waning Gibbous, Last Quarter, Waning Crescent** — the same in reverse, visible more in the morning.

The lit side always faces the Sun. So if you can see a crescent, you know where the Sun is, even at night.

## Science

- The Moon is a quarter of Earth’s diameter — unusually huge for a moon. Most moons are tiny compared to their planets.
- It’s about 4.5 billion years old, formed (most likely) when a Mars-sized object called Theia slammed into early Earth and the debris coalesced.
- It’s **tidally locked** — the same face always points at us. The “dark side” isn’t always dark; it’s just the far side, which got photographed for the first time in 1959 by a Soviet probe.
- Moonlight is just reflected sunlight. It’s roughly 400,000 times fainter than direct sunlight, which is why you can look at it without hurting your eyes.
- The Moon is drifting away from Earth at about 3.8 cm per year.

## Skill — Navigating by the Moon

- **Rises east, sets west** — same as the Sun. A crescent low in the west after sunset means you’re looking west.
- **Horns-to-horizon trick (Northern Hemisphere):** draw an imaginary line through the two horns of a crescent Moon and extend it down to the horizon. That point on the horizon is *roughly* south. It’s a rough trick, not a compass, but good enough to confirm you’re not wildly turned around.
- **Moon clock:** a full Moon rises at sunset, a first-quarter at noon, a last-quarter at midnight. Knowing the phase tells you roughly when the Moon rose and will set.
- **Lunar calendars** — many cultures (Islamic, Hebrew, Chinese) built their whole year around the Moon because it’s more obvious than the Sun’s slow seasonal drift. One lunar cycle is ~29.5 days, which is why “month” and “Moon” share a root.

## Stretch — Challenges

*A handful of invitations. Try one, try all, try them in your own order. They are here to send you outside.*

1. **Moon Diary.** See if you can draw the Moon every night (or every clear night) for one full cycle — 29 or 30 days. One little square a day in the Fieldbook. Note the time. By the end, you will have drawn a shape no photograph quite catches. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Predict Tomorrow.** Each night, have a go at predicting what the Moon will look like tomorrow. See how you did the following evening. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Daytime Moon.** The Moon is up for about half the daylight hours — have a wander and see if you can spot it in a blue sky. First and last quarter are the easiest phases to catch. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Horns to Horizon.** On a crescent evening, try the horns-to-horizon trick to guess where south is, then see how close a compass says you got. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Moonrise Math.** Using only the phase and the time, see if you can predict when the Moon will rise tonight. Check against an almanac afterwards — how did you do? *Grade: Stretch.*
