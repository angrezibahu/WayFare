## Story

*Read aloud on a sunny afternoon, ideally with a stick already in the lawn.*

Four thousand years ago, in the city of Heliopolis on the Nile, a priest of Ra would walk out at dawn carrying a length of palm-rib and a string with a small weight on the end. He would stand the rib upright, drop the string beside it, and watch the shadow swing from west to east through the morning. At its shortest, when the shadow had nearly disappeared into the foot of the rib, the priest would chalk a line on the temple pavement. *Noon*, said the line. *True north,* said the line. *Begin.*

The same trick — a stick, a shadow, a patient hour — was known to Polynesian wayfinders setting voyaging canoes at dawn, to Roman surveyors laying out a road, and to British shepherds checking their watches against the Sun. It belongs to no one and to everyone. The Greeks called the upright stick a *gnomon*, "the one that knows".

Pick up a stick. You are now holding the oldest navigational instrument on Earth.

## Land

**The basic shadow stick — west and east.**
1. Push a straight stick (about a metre long, but any length works) vertically into level ground in full Sun.
1. Mark the *tip* of its shadow with a small stone or a peg. Call this stone **W**.
1. Wait 15–20 minutes.
1. Mark the new tip of the shadow with another stone. Call this stone **E**.
1. The straight line between **W** and **E** runs **west to east**. **W** is west; **E** is east.
1. Stand with **W** to your left and **E** to your right. You are now facing **north** (Northern Hemisphere). Behind you is **south**.

That works anywhere on Earth that the Sun is shining and the ground is flat-ish. It does not need a watch, a compass, a clock, or batteries. It is the most reliable rough-direction trick that has ever existed.

**Refinement — true north from the shortest shadow.**

The Sun's path across the sky is an arc, and at one moment in the day the Sun stands at its highest. That moment is **local solar noon**. At local solar noon, a stick's shadow is at its **shortest** and points due **north** (NH) or due **south** (SH).

To find solar noon by stick:
- Through the late morning, mark the tip of the shadow every fifteen minutes.
- The marks will form a curve.
- The shortest mark on the curve is the moment of local solar noon.
- A line from the foot of the stick through that mark is true north (or south, in the Southern Hemisphere).

This method is more accurate than the quick *W-E* trick — within a degree if you are patient — and it costs only a stick and a morning.

**Approximate time from the same stick.**

Once you have the *true north* line marked on the ground, the stick becomes a sundial:
- Solar noon = the shortest shadow, on the north line.
- Each hour either side, the shadow swings about **15° east** (morning) or **15° west** (afternoon).
- Mark out hour-lines fanning at roughly 15° increments. The clock you have just drawn is good to about ten minutes.

A caveat: the shadow rotates *uniformly* only at the equator, on the equinoxes. At higher latitudes and other times of year the angle compresses, especially in winter — the lines bunch up near noon and spread in the morning and evening. For family use, ten-minute accuracy is plenty.

## Science

- The Sun appears to move across the sky at **15° per hour** (360° ÷ 24 h). The Earth turns; the Sun stands still.
- *Local solar noon* is defined by the Sun crossing your local **meridian** — the imaginary north–south line overhead. It can be up to **16 minutes** off your watch's clock-noon. Two reasons: your **longitude** (every degree east or west of your time-zone meridian shifts solar noon by four minutes), and the *equation of time* (Earth's orbit is not a perfect circle).
- The shadow-stick is, geometrically, an **analemmatic sundial without numbers**. The numbers are what civilisation eventually added; the geometry is older.
- The Egyptians used a **merkhet** — a plumb-line plus a slotted sighting bar — paired with a shadow-stick to set the meridian to a fraction of a degree. The Great Pyramid is aligned to within 1/15th of a degree of true north, which is essentially the limit of what a careful shadow-stick can do.

## Skill — A working sun-compass in five minutes

If you are stopped in a strange place on a sunny day:
1. Find a stick or any vertical object (a fence-post, a walking-pole stuck in earth).
1. Mark the shadow tip; wait fifteen minutes; mark again.
1. Stand with the first mark to your **left**, the second to your **right**.
1. You are facing roughly **north**. Behind you, south.

Total time: 15 minutes. Total gear: a stick and two pebbles. Total accuracy: within about 10°, which is the difference between *I am wandering* and *I am walking towards somewhere*.

## Try It — Shadow Stick Bearings and Time

<div class="vibe vibe-try">

**A whole-morning sundial.** On the next clear morning, push a 1 m garden cane vertically into a flat patch of lawn. Every fifteen minutes from 10:00 a.m. until 1:30 p.m., place a small stone at the tip of its shadow. Each stone needs the time written on it (a bit of masking tape works). At the end, the stones make a curve. The **shortest** stone is local solar noon — that line, from cane to stone, is **true north**. The other stones are an *hour-clock*: 15° per hour either side of the noon line. Lay a stick along the noon line and stand on it. You are now standing on your own meridian, with a clock and a compass made entirely of pebbles.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Five small invitations. Take them in any order, on any sunny day.*

1. **First Shadow.** *Grade: Starter.* Do the basic 15-minute West-East trick on a Saturday morning. Sketch the two stones and the line in the Fieldbook. *(Pairs with the deck card "Shadow Stick at Noon".)*
1. **Find True North.** *Grade: Core.* Do the shortest-shadow method through a single morning. Confirm the noon line afterwards with a compass. How close did you get?
1. **Pebble Sundial.** *Grade: Core.* Lay out a complete pebble sundial in the garden. Come back the next sunny day and read the time from it. How accurate to the hour? To the half-hour?
1. **Solar-vs-Clock-Noon.** *Grade: Stretch.* Look up your longitude. Calculate the predicted offset between solar noon and clock noon for the date. Then measure it with the stick. Were you right?
1. **A Phone-Free Hour.** *Grade: Starter.* For one hour outside, tell the time only by glancing at a sundial you have built. Note when you were tempted to check your phone, and what the sky was doing.
