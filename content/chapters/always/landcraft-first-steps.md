## Story

*A small story, true, told by many families in many places.*

Once, before phones and before GPS, before maps were cheap and printed, people walked out of their front doors and knew where they were. They knew because they had been taught. The Sun was a clock; a stick was a compass; a stream ran downhill; a leaning tree pointed to the wind that had been blowing for fifty years.

This chapter is the first handful of those small, ordinary skills. None of them are secret. All of them need practice.

In Sanskrit the word for direction is *dishā*, and the eight cardinal directions are not abstractions but persons — each has its *Dikpāla*, a guardian. *Indra* keeps the east, *Yama* the south, *Varuṇa* the west, *Kubera* the north, and four more stand at the corners. To know your direction, in that older grammar, is to know whose country you are walking through. The skills below are the small, secular version of the same instinct: a way of asking the land where you are.

## Land

**The Sun rises in the east and sets in the west.** Close enough. (It drifts north and south through the year — see *Dawn Compass* in the deck.) At local solar noon, the Sun is due south (Northern Hemisphere) and due north (Southern Hemisphere). The Sun is a rough clock accurate to maybe ten minutes.

**A shadow moves.** Push a stick in the ground; mark the tip of its shadow; wait fifteen minutes; mark it again. The two marks give you a west–east line. The first mark is west. This works anywhere on Earth that the Sun is shining.

**Water runs downhill.** Even in a city, gutters and drains tell you which way the ground tilts. In open country, follow a stream downstream and you will reach more people, more bridges, and eventually a larger stream.

**Moss is a liar.** "Moss grows on the north side" is the single most often repeated wrong fact in outdoor lore. Moss grows on whichever side is damp and shaded — which might be north, might be under an overhang, might be just where the wind doesn't reach. Use your eyes; don't use the rule.

**Trees lean with the wind.** In exposed places, mature trees grow leaning away from the prevailing wind. In the UK that is roughly south-westerly, so most exposed trees lean north-east. A ridge of leaning hawthorns is a fifty-year-old compass.

Notice how the Welsh *dwyrain* and *gorllewin* embed the Sun in their bones — *dwyrain* from the rising, *gorllewin* from the setting. The Irish *oirthear* and *iarthar* do the same. East and west are not arbitrary tokens; they are the Sun's own verbs.

## Science

- Local solar noon is when the Sun is at its highest — not 12:00 on the clock. Clock noon is a political convention; solar noon is physics.
- The Sun's path across the sky is an arc whose angle changes with latitude and with the time of year.
- Wind prevails from one direction in most places on Earth because of how the atmosphere circulates — trade winds, westerlies, polar easterlies.

## Skill — Putting it together

If you can:

1. Tell roughly where south is (shadow stick, or watch-as-compass, or the Sun at noon).
1. Tell which way water flows where you are.
1. Read one clear wind signal (a flag, a chimney, a tree).

…then you are not lost, even without a phone. You have a direction and a drainage.

## Historical — three ways of finding direction

Three civilisations, three instruments, one question — *which way is north*. The Egyptian priest, the wartime airman, and the satellite engineer were all asking the same thing. Their answers, set side by side, are a small history of human ingenuity.

<div class="vibe vibe-egypt">

**Ancient Egypt — the gnomon and the obelisk-shadow**

The first wayfinders were astronomers. In Heliopolis — the City of the Sun, just north-east of modern Cairo — the priests of Ra raised obelisks of red granite that were, among other sacred things, the largest shadow-sticks ever built. *Cleopatra's Needle*, now on the Thames embankment in London, once stood in front of the Temple of the Sun at Heliopolis and threw its shadow across a paved court so that the noon mark could be read from the steps.

To set such a stone *true*, the Egyptians used an instrument called the *merkhet* — literally "instrument of knowing". It was nothing more than a plumb-line (a string with a small weight) and a slotted sighting bar made from a palm-rib. By holding the plumb-line vertical and sighting through the slot at a circumpolar star — one of the stars that wheels nightly around the celestial pole and never sets — the priest-astronomer could fix the meridian, the true north–south line, with extraordinary precision. Two merkhets used together let temples be aligned to the cardinal directions to within a fraction of a degree.

The shadow-stick you push into your garden is the same idea, scaled down by four thousand years and by a great deal of granite. The stick is a portable obelisk; its moving shadow is the same west-to-east line that the Heliopolitan priests read off their pavement.

</div>

<div class="vibe vibe-ww2">

**MI9 — the button compass and the silk map**

In a small office in London during the Second World War, a stage magician turned intelligence officer named *Christopher Clayton Hutton* — known to everyone as *Clutty* — invented the escape kit. Hutton worked for MI9, the branch of British military intelligence that helped downed airmen and prisoners of war find their way home. His brief was simple and almost impossible: hide a compass and a map on a man so that a German guard searching him would not find them.

His answer to the compass was the *brass-button compass*. The top button of an RAF tunic unscrewed, and inside lay a tiny magnetised needle on a pivot. To make sure the needle was not deflected by the airman's own buttons, the rest of the tunic's brass was specified as *non-magnetic* by design — a quiet, deliberate piece of metallurgy that no enemy ever noticed. The map was printed on silk, which folds smaller than paper, makes no rustle, and survives a soaking; silk maps were sewn into linings, hidden in the heels of flying boots, or rolled inside fountain pens.

The technique you may already know — *watch as compass*, holding the watch flat with the hour hand pointing at the Sun — is a cousin of Clutty's button. Both turn an everyday object into an instrument by knowing one thing the object can do that its owner had forgotten.

</div>

<div class="vibe vibe-modern">

**Modern — GPS, and what is actually happening**

The Global Positioning System is a constellation of around twenty-four satellites — there are usually more, with spares — orbiting at about *20,200 km* above the Earth, arranged so that from any point on the surface at least four are above the horizon at any moment. Each satellite carries an *atomic clock*, accurate to a few nanoseconds a day, and broadcasts a continuous time-stamped signal.

Your receiver listens to at least four of these signals at once. By comparing the tiny differences in arrival time — light from each satellite is travelling at almost exactly 300,000 km per second — it solves a four-way trilateration: three for position, one for the receiver's own clock error. The arithmetic depends on the *SI second*, redefined in 1967 as 9,192,631,770 oscillations of an atom of *caesium-133*. Without that redefinition the system could not work.

Civilian GPS is accurate to about *three metres* under a clear sky. Under trees, between buildings, or indoors it is much worse — and the helpful "blue dot" on a phone is not pure GPS at all but a blend of GPS, Wi-Fi network fingerprints, and cell-tower triangulation. The satellites do not see you; you listen to them. If the sky above you is blocked, the system is silent, and the older skills — sun, shadow, water, wind — become the only ones you have.

</div>

## Try It — wayfaring from the historical pages

<div class="vibe vibe-try">

**The Egyptian merkhet — Polaris by plumb-line.** On a clear night, make a plumb-line from a length of string and any small weight (a key, a washer, a pebble in a sock). Suspend it from a stick pushed into the ground or from a low branch so that it hangs free and still. Cut a sighting card — a postcard with a vertical slot — and hold it a pace behind the line. Move your eye until the card's slot, the plumb-string, and *Polaris* (the North Star) line up as one. Drive a small peg into the ground directly under your eye. Walk twenty paces in any direction, set the plumb-line up again, and re-sight Polaris through the slot — drive a second peg. The line between your two pegs is *true north*, found by exactly the method used by the priests of Heliopolis four thousand years ago.

**Make and use a button compass — after MI9.** Take an ordinary sewing needle and a magnet (a fridge magnet will do). Stroke the needle along the magnet *thirty times in the same direction* — always the same end of the needle, always the same direction of stroke — lifting the needle clear between strokes. The needle is now magnetised. Lay it on a slice of cork, a leaf, or a small square of paper, and float it in a saucer of still water. Within a few seconds it will swing and settle on the magnetic north–south line. To carry it the way an MI9 evader did, sew the needle and a folded paper-square into the lining of a coat — and forget it is there until the day you need it.

</div>
