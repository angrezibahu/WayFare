## Story

*A short piece for a map-and-tea evening.*

Before there were OS maps, there was the shepherd who knew every hollow where a sheep might hide in snow. There was the gamekeeper who knew every badger path through his covert. There was the old woman who lived next to a hill and could tell you, without looking, that the south-facing slope would have primroses three weeks before the north-facing slope. This knowledge is *terrain-reading* — the way a landscape *tilts*, *faces*, *drains*, and *grows*. A map is a beautiful thing. A map and a pair of eyes is better.

## Land

**Slope has a direction and a steepness.** The direction it faces is called the *aspect*. In the Northern Hemisphere, a south-facing slope gets more Sun — it is warmer, drier, earlier into spring, first to flower, first to lose snow. A north-facing slope is cold, damp, mossy, and keeps its winter late. Before you look for anything on a hillside — a wildflower, a viable campsite, a dry path, a shady rest — ask: which way does it face?

**Contour lines, plainly.**
- Contours are lines of equal height. On a British 1:25,000 Explorer map they are drawn every 5 metres (every 10 metres in mountain areas).
- **Close together = steep. Far apart = gentle.** That's the rule.
- A ring of closed contours is a hill. Several inside each other is a steeper hill.
- Contours bending *uphill* (towards higher ground) show a *valley* or a stream. Contours bending *downhill* show a *spur* or *ridge*.
- A *col* or *saddle* is the low point between two summits. Old drovers' paths take cols, because cols are the lowest way over a ridge.

**Shape reveals process.**
- *Rounded hills* — slow erosion, old landscape (Scottish Highlands, Welsh Brecon).
- *Sharp peaks and cirques* — glacial scouring (Lake District, Cairngorms).
- *Chalk downs* — softly rolling, thin soil, dry valleys with no streams because the chalk swallows the rain (South Downs, Chilterns).
- *Flat river plains* — old flooding, lush, often with straight-edged fields and drainage ditches (the Fens, Somerset Levels).
- *Moors* — high, acidic, peaty, wind-shaped trees.

**Vegetation reads the ground.**
- *Nettles and docks* together often mark old human habitation — livestock dung, nitrogen in the soil, an old yard.
- *Reeds and rushes* mark standing water or a seasonally wet patch even in summer.
- *Gorse and bracken* love thin, acid soil.
- *Bluebells in woodland* almost always mean an *ancient* wood — more than 400 years old in the UK.
- *Ivy climbing a tree* is a sign of a long-undisturbed place; it takes decades to grow to height.

**Human marks read too.**
- *Drystone walls* follow old boundaries, often older than any road, often marking parish or estate edges.
- *Holloways* — paths worn down by centuries of feet and carts — tell you which way was travelled most.
- *Ridge-and-furrow* fields — long wave patterns in the grass — show medieval ploughing preserved under later pasture.
- *Church towers* are usually the highest human-made thing for miles in old villages, and are often sighted on from each other. In some English counties you can triangulate your position from two towers.

## Science

- Slopes face the way they do because of how the land was folded or eroded — an accident of geology and climate that now shapes everything that lives on them.
- A north-facing slope in Britain may receive only 70% of the sunlight of a south-facing slope at the same latitude. Over a year that is a real difference in soil temperature, which is why flowering dates differ by weeks.
- *Glacial U-shaped valleys* have flat bottoms and steep sides because ice grinds wide. *River V-shaped valleys* have narrow bottoms because water cuts down. A glance at valley shape often tells you the last thing that carved it.
- A *rain shadow* forms downwind of a range of hills: the windward side gets the rain, the leeward side is dry. The English Lake District has this — the western fells drench; the Eden Valley behind them is one of the drier parts of the north.

## Skill — Reading terrain in the field

- **Ridge-to-valley thinking.** When you are lost or tired, zoom out. Which *ridge* am I on? Which *valley* drains below me? Every location in hill country is on a ridge or in a valley or on the slope between. Name your ridge and your valley before you do anything else.
- **Handrails.** A *handrail* is a linear feature you can follow — a wall, a stream, a road, a ridge crest. Planning a walk, identify the handrail for each leg. It is almost impossible to lose a long handrail even in bad visibility.
- **Catching features.** A *catching feature* is something across your line of travel that will stop you if you've gone too far — a ridge, a road, a lake shore. In mist, aim off (deliberately to one side), walk until your catching feature stops you, then turn to the handrail that leads to your target. This is orienteering-grade navigation with no fancy equipment.
- **Aspect check on a dull day.** Even with no Sun, you can often tell which way a slope faces: thicker mosses and brighter greens on north-facing, drier grasses and more heather on south-facing. Snow lies longest on the north-facing slopes of UK hills.
- **Time by height.** Naismith's Rule: 1 hour per 5 km on the flat, plus 1 hour per 600 m of ascent. For children and tired adults, add half again. Knowing this saves daylight miscalculations.

## Stretch — Challenges

1. **Contour Walk.** On a 1:25,000 OS map of a local area, find a hill. Trace the highest contour ring. Then, on the ground, walk around the hill at roughly that height. Does it feel level? *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Aspect Diary.** On a hill near home, on a spring morning, compare three plants on the south-facing slope with three plants on the north-facing slope. How different are they in development? *Grade: Core.*
1. **Read a Wall.** Find a drystone wall or old hedge near you. Follow it. Where does it go? What does it separate? Is it straight or does it kink around an older feature? Write its life story. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Handrail & Catching Feature.** On a 1 km walk, plan your route in terms of a handrail (to follow) and a catching feature (to stop you). Walk it. Did they work? *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Name Your Valley.** Find out the name of the valley and the ridge or hill where you live. If no one has named the ridge, name it yourselves and put the name on your family Year Map. *Grade: Starter.*
