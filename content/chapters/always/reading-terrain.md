## Story

*A short piece for a map-and-tea evening.*

Before there were OS maps, there was the shepherd who knew every hollow where a sheep might hide in snow. There was the gamekeeper who knew every badger path through his covert. There was the old woman who lived next to a hill and could tell you, without looking, that the south-facing slope would have primroses three weeks before the north-facing slope. This knowledge is *terrain-reading* — the way a landscape *tilts*, *faces*, *drains*, and *grows*. A map is a beautiful thing. A map and a pair of eyes is better.

## Land

**Slope has a direction and a steepness.** The direction it faces is called the *aspect*. In the Northern Hemisphere, a south-facing slope gets more Sun — it is warmer, drier, earlier into spring, first to flower, first to lose snow. A north-facing slope is cold, damp, mossy, and keeps its winter late. Before you look for anything on a hillside — a wildflower, a viable campsite, a dry path, a shady rest — ask: which way does it face?

**Contour lines, plainly.**
- Contours are lines of equal height. On a British 1:25,000 Explorer map they are drawn every 5 metres (every 10 metres in mountain areas).
- **Close together = steep. Far apart = gentle.** That's the rule.
- A ring of closed contours is a hill. Several inside each other is a steeper hill.
- Contours bending *uphill* (towards higher ground) show a *valley* or a stream. Contours bending *downhill* show a *spur* or *ridge*.
- A *col* or *saddle* is the low point between two summits. Old drovers' paths take cols, because cols are the lowest way over a ridge.

**Shape reveals process.**
- *Rounded hills* — slow erosion, old landscape (Scottish Highlands, Welsh Brecon).
- *Sharp peaks and cirques* — glacial scouring (Lake District, Cairngorms).
- *Chalk downs* — softly rolling, thin soil, dry valleys with no streams because the chalk swallows the rain (South Downs, Chilterns).
- *Flat river plains* — old flooding, lush, often with straight-edged fields and drainage ditches (the Fens, Somerset Levels).
- *Moors* — high, acidic, peaty, wind-shaped trees.

**Vegetation reads the ground.**
- *Nettles and docks* together often mark old human habitation — livestock dung, nitrogen in the soil, an old yard.
- *Reeds and rushes* mark standing water or a seasonally wet patch even in summer.
- *Gorse and bracken* love thin, acid soil.
- *Bluebells in woodland* almost always mean an *ancient* wood — more than 400 years old in the UK.
- *Ivy climbing a tree* is a sign of a long-undisturbed place; it takes decades to grow to height.

**Human marks read too.**
- *Drystone walls* follow old boundaries, often older than any road, often marking parish or estate edges.
- *Holloways* — paths worn down by centuries of feet and carts — tell you which way was travelled most.
- *Ridge-and-furrow* fields — long wave patterns in the grass — show medieval ploughing preserved under later pasture.
- *Church towers* are usually the highest human-made thing for miles in old villages, and are often sighted on from each other. In some English counties you can triangulate your position from two towers.

## Historical — three ways of knowing the ground

<div class="vibe vibe-greece">

### Ancient Greece — Eratosthenes measures the Earth

Around 240 BC, **Eratosthenes of Cyrene**, head librarian of the Great Library at Alexandria, did one of the cleverest pieces of terrain-reading in history. He had heard that in the city of Syene (now Aswan), at noon on the summer solstice, the Sun shone directly down a deep well — that is, it was exactly overhead. In Alexandria, on the same day and hour, a vertical gnomon (a stick) cast a short shadow.

Eratosthenes measured the shadow's angle: 7.2 degrees — one fiftieth of a full circle. He knew the distance from Alexandria to Syene (about 5,000 *stadia*, measured by professional pacers called **bematists**). If 5,000 stadia corresponded to one fiftieth of the Earth's circumference, the whole Earth was 250,000 stadia round. His answer, in modern units, was within **1–2%** of the true figure of 40,075 km.

He had no satellite, no ruler longer than his arm, and only a library and a stick. What he had instead was the realisation that **two shadows in two places on one day are a measuring tape wrapped around the planet.** Every contour line on every modern OS map is a descendant of that insight.

(Eratosthenes also drew the first known map of the known world with a grid of parallels and meridians — the ancestor of every latitude-longitude coordinate system ever drawn since.)

</div>

<div class="vibe vibe-ww2">

### MI9 and the birth of British orienteering (1939–60)

The MI9 silk maps (see *Rain and Running Water*) taught a generation of British servicemen to read ground they had never walked. When they came home, many went on to found the British orienteering movement: Major **John Disley** (Olympic steeplechase bronze, 1952) and **Chris Brasher** (4-minute-mile pacer for Bannister) organised the first British championships in 1967, using the same "handrail / catching-feature / attack-point" vocabulary MI9 had used for evaders.

The MI9 technique had four parts, which are still taught in every Mountain Leader course:

1. **Handrail.** A linear feature you can follow with your eyes shut — a wall, a fence, a stream, a ridge crest. Plan every leg of a route along a handrail.
1. **Catching feature.** A feature *across* your line of travel that will stop you if you have gone too far — a ridge, a road, a river, a lake shore.
1. **Attack point.** A specific, unmissable place near your target from which you commit to the final bearing — an isolated building, a lone tree, a path junction.
1. **Aiming off.** Deliberately aim to one side of your target, so that when you hit the handrail you *know* which way to turn.

A servicemen's joke in 1944 ran: *"The map is a plan, the ground is the truth; when they disagree, believe the ground."* It is still true.

</div>

<div class="vibe vibe-modern">

### Modern — LiDAR and the laser-read landscape

In the 2010s the UK Environment Agency began surveying the country with **LiDAR** — Light Detection And Ranging. An aircraft flies at 1,000 metres above the ground and fires a pulsed laser downwards a hundred thousand times a second. Each pulse reflects from whatever it hits first — a leaf, a roof, a sheep — and a proportion reaches the ground beneath. A computer separates the ground-returns from the canopy-returns and draws a map of the Earth's *bare surface*, accurate to about 15 cm vertically.

The result is a **Digital Terrain Model** (DTM), now freely available for almost all of England and much of Wales and Scotland. Archaeologists love it: Roman roads, medieval ridge-and-furrow, and Iron Age hill-forts that are invisible under today's hedgerows show up on LiDAR as clearly as if the trees had been removed. Hydrologists love it: the exact height of every river bank, every flood embankment, every drain gulley.

A LiDAR hill-shade map beside an OS Explorer map is the closest thing to terrain-reading god-mode that has ever existed. But — a warning — LiDAR is a photograph, not a conversation. It cannot tell you *why* the wall kinks there (the old badger sett), or *why* the path chose this col rather than the lower one (the wind in winter). The shepherd still knows things the laser does not.

## Science

- Slopes face the way they do because of how the land was folded or eroded — an accident of geology and climate that now shapes everything that lives on them.
- A north-facing slope in Britain may receive only 70% of the sunlight of a south-facing slope at the same latitude. Over a year that is a real difference in soil temperature, which is why flowering dates differ by weeks.
- *Glacial U-shaped valleys* have flat bottoms and steep sides because ice grinds wide. *River V-shaped valleys* have narrow bottoms because water cuts down. A glance at valley shape often tells you the last thing that carved it.
- A *rain shadow* forms downwind of a range of hills: the windward side gets the rain, the leeward side is dry. The English Lake District has this — the western fells drench; the Eden Valley behind them is one of the drier parts of the north.

## Skill — Reading terrain in the field

- **Ridge-to-valley thinking.** When you are lost or tired, zoom out. Which *ridge* am I on? Which *valley* drains below me? Every location in hill country is on a ridge or in a valley or on the slope between. Name your ridge and your valley before you do anything else.
- **Handrails.** A *handrail* is a linear feature you can follow — a wall, a stream, a road, a ridge crest. Planning a walk, identify the handrail for each leg. It is almost impossible to lose a long handrail even in bad visibility.
- **Catching features.** A *catching feature* is something across your line of travel that will stop you if you've gone too far — a ridge, a road, a lake shore. In mist, aim off (deliberately to one side), walk until your catching feature stops you, then turn to the handrail that leads to your target. This is orienteering-grade navigation with no fancy equipment.
- **Aspect check on a dull day.** Even with no Sun, you can often tell which way a slope faces: thicker mosses and brighter greens on north-facing, drier grasses and more heather on south-facing. Snow lies longest on the north-facing slopes of UK hills.
- **Time by height.** Naismith's Rule: 1 hour per 5 km on the flat, plus 1 hour per 600 m of ascent. For children and tired adults, add half again. Knowing this saves daylight miscalculations.

## Try It — wayfaring from the historical pages

<div class="vibe vibe-try">

**Measure a hill the Eratosthenes way.** On any sunny noon, find a hill or a long slope near home. At its *top*, push a straight stick vertically into the ground and measure the length of its shadow. Walk to the *bottom* of the same slope on the same bearing, same time, same stick-height — do this as quickly as you can, so the Sun has hardly moved — and measure the shadow again. The difference in shadow-length tells you how much the slope has tilted the ground *relative to the Sun*. A bit of trigonometry (grown-up's job, or a phone calculator) converts that into the slope's angle. You have used a stick, a ruler, and the Sun to measure a gradient with no sat-nav and no OS map. This is what Eratosthenes did at the scale of a planet.

**Plan a route the MI9 way.** Pick a local 2 km walk — a loop round a park or a wood. Before leaving home, at the map, break it into legs. For each leg, write three things in the Fieldbook: (1) my **handrail** (what am I following?), (2) my **catching feature** (what will stop me if I go too far?), (3) my **attack point** (where will I switch from handrail to final bearing?). Walk it. At each leg, tick the three. This is exactly how a Special Operations Executive agent dropped into Occupied France was trained to think about their first 10 km.

</div>

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations to wonder at the land's own shape. Take them in any order — the land is patient.*

1. **Contour Walk.** On a 1:25,000 OS map of a local area, pick a hill and trace its highest contour ring. Then, on the ground, see if you can walk all the way around the hill at roughly that height. Does it feel level? What gives the illusion away? *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Aspect Diary.** On a hill near home, on a spring morning, compare three plants on the south-facing slope with three on the north-facing slope. How far ahead is the sunny side? *Grade: Core.*
1. **Read a Wall.** Find a drystone wall or an old hedge near you and follow it. Where does it go? What does it separate? Does it kink around an older feature you can still see? Write out its life story. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Handrail & Catching Feature.** Plan a 1 km walk in terms of a handrail (to follow) and a catching feature (to stop you). Walk it. Did either of them save you? *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Name Your Valley.** See if you can find out the name of the valley and the ridge or hill where you live. If nobody has named the ridge, name it yourselves and put the name on your family Year Map. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **LiDAR Hunt.** Find your home on the Environment Agency's LiDAR DTM viewer. Look for an earthwork, hollow, or ridge that isn't on the OS map. Go out and see if it's there on the ground. *Grade: Stretch.*

## Go deeper

**The science.** Environment Agency National LiDAR Programme (environment.data.gov.uk — search "LiDAR Composite DTM", free to download) — bare-earth digital elevation data that reveals ridge lines, earthworks, and ancient features invisible on the surface or on a standard map.

**The cultural context.** Oliver Rackham, *The History of the Countryside* (Dent, 1986) — how to read a British landscape as a historical document; the standard reference for understanding what holloways, wood-banks, and hedgerow trees mean.

**App or tool.** British Orienteering (britishorienteering.org.uk) — club events welcome families; orienteering maps are the most detailed terrain-reading resource available and every session is a practical lesson.

**Book or video.** Oliver Rackham, *The History of the Countryside* (Dent, 1986); also the *Rackham's Wood* lecture series on YouTube — free, and Rackham himself explaining ancient-woodland signs.
