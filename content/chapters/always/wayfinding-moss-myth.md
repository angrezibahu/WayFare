## Story

*Read aloud on a slow walk through a damp wood, ideally with a hand on a mossy trunk.*

This is the most repeated wrong fact in outdoor lore. In half the camp manuals on half the bookshelves of the world, in films and novels and children's adventure series, a lost hero pats the side of a tree, finds moss, and declares: *That side is north. We go this way.* The hero is then admired, and the family follows, and the writer cuts to the next scene. It is a story so well-loved that it cannot quite be killed.

But the real wood is not so kind. **Moss grows on whichever side is damp and shaded** — which might be the north of the trunk, which might be the under-side of an over-hang, which might just be the side that doesn't catch the wind. A line of trees beside a stream may all be mossiest on the side facing the water, regardless of compass direction. A tree at the lee of a hill may carry moss on its leeward face — perhaps west, perhaps east. A wall in a London courtyard may grow moss on its *south* side, where a building shadows it from morning to evening.

This chapter is here to bust the rule, kindly, and replace it with what the wood is actually trying to tell you.

## Land

**What moss actually likes.** Mosses are tiny, simple plants that take their water *directly from the air and surface* (they have no proper roots). They thrive on:
- **Constant moisture** — air moisture, rainfall, dew, runoff.
- **Shade** — direct strong sun dries them out and kills them back.
- **Stable, slow-drying surfaces** — bark, stone, brick, soil that doesn't bake.

**What moss does not particularly care about.** Compass direction, in itself. Moss is not a magnet.

**Why the rule sometimes seems to work.** In a *very open* clearing or field, on an *isolated* mature tree, in *northern* mid-latitudes (the UK is at 50–60° N), the **north** side of the trunk *may* indeed be the side most shaded from direct sun — because the Sun never quite reaches the north face. In that one *clean* situation, with no other tree casting shade, no wall, no overhang, no slope, no prevailing wind, the moss might prefer the north. The rule is the *first-order solution* in a textbook with no other features.

In any **real** wood, the trees are crowded, the wind is from one direction, the slope tilts, the soil is damp on one side. *Every* one of those factors will outweigh the simple solar shading. The "north" rule is overwhelmed by reality almost everywhere it is repeated.

**What plant patterns *do* tell you.**
- **South-facing trunks** in the UK are often *drier* and may carry **paler, crustose lichens** that like dry bright bark. The colour shift up and down a single trunk is real.
- **North-facing trunks** are often *cooler and damper* — they may carry **richer, deeper green** mosses. Again, this is a tendency on isolated trees, not a law in a wood.
- **Wind-side bark** is typically *more eroded*, with looser scales and less lichen, on exposed trees. The lee side carries more growth. The *prevailing wind direction* is often a more reliable land-clue than moss.
- **Wet-side trunks** near a stream or wet flush carry rich moss regardless of compass direction.

The honest version of the rule is: **moss-and-lichen distribution is a clue, not a compass; read the whole tree, the whole place, and the prevailing wind.**

## Science

- A *moss* is a non-vascular plant that absorbs water from rain and damp air directly through its leaves. It cannot move; it cannot survive long drying. The Northern Hemisphere has roughly **800 species** of moss, each with its own preferences.
- A *lichen* is a partnership between a fungus and a photosynthetic alga or cyanobacterium. Different lichen species have very different sun- and damp-tolerances; the colour and crustiness of lichen on bark is a more usable clue than moss alone.
- Mosses orient by water and shade. They do not respond to compass direction. There is no biological mechanism that would make them prefer "north" as a direction; they prefer "wet, shaded".
- *Microclimate* — the conditions on a single trunk — is set by **direct sun (above), reflected light, ground-up moisture, prevailing wind, and adjacent objects** (other trees, walls, slopes). Compass direction is one input among five.

## Skill — Reading what trees are actually telling you

- **Look for *patterns across many trees*, not single trees.** If twenty trees all carry richer moss on the same side, that side has something the others lack. Often the prevailing wind, often slope, sometimes shade. *Then* — and only then — work out what compass direction that side corresponds to.
- **Combine moss with the *flag-tree* and *wind-pruned* signs** (see the prevailing-wind chapter). Moss + tree-lean + scrub-direction together are a reasonable triangulation. Moss alone is not.
- **Be especially suspicious of moss in deep wood**, near streams, near walls, on stone. The moisture there overrides everything.
- **Use moss as a *story-teller*, not a compass.** "There has been water here." "Wind has been from there." "Sun has not reached this corner." The wood will tell you these things if you ask.

## Try It — The Five-Tree Moss Survey

<div class="vibe vibe-try">

**Five trees, five truths.** On the next damp morning, walk to five mature trees in five different settings:
1. An **isolated** tree in an open field.
1. A tree in the **middle of a wood**.
1. A tree by a **stream or pond**.
1. A tree on a **slope** (note which way the slope tilts).
1. A tree near a **wall, barn, or large stone**.

For each tree:
- Find the *mossiest* side. Note its **compass bearing** with a compass.
- Note **what else** is happening on that side — is it the lee of the prevailing wind? Is it facing a wet flush? Is it shaded by a wall?

Tally the five bearings in a table. **Were they all the same direction?** Almost certainly not. **Was *any* a clean "north"?** Maybe one. **What single feature explains the most trees?** It will usually be moisture or shade, not compass direction.

Sketch the five trees in the Fieldbook with the moss side marked. The page becomes a small *error-curriculum* artifact: an old folk rule held up against the real wood, and the real wood winning.

</div>

## Stretch — Challenges

*Four invitations to befriend a wood and distrust a saying.*

1. **The Moss Survey.** *Grade: Starter.* Do the five-tree survey above. *(Pairs with the deck card "Moss Is a Liar".)*
1. **A Wall, Two Sides.** *Grade: Starter.* Find a stone or brick wall running roughly east–west. Compare the moss on its north and its south face. Was the rule honest here?
1. **Lichen Up and Down.** *Grade: Core.* On one mature tree, photograph the trunk from waist-height to head-height on north *and* south sides. Are the lichens different colours? What does this tell you about microclimate?
1. **Tell the Saying Back.** *Grade: Story.* Find an old book in the house that repeats the moss-as-compass rule. Write a short paragraph in the margin explaining when it works and when it doesn't, in your own words. The next reader of the book will inherit a more careful piece of folklore.
