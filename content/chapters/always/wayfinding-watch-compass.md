## Story

*A small story, true. Read aloud while looking at any wristwatch.*

In a Black-Country office in 1940, a stage magician turned intelligence officer named **Christopher Clayton Hutton** — *Clutty* to everyone — was inventing the *escape kit*. The brief was almost impossible: hide a compass and a map on an airman so that a German guard searching him would not find them. Clutty's answer was the *brass-button compass* — the top button of an RAF tunic unscrewed, and inside lay a tiny magnetised needle on a pivot.

But back home, in Scout halls and on Guide weekends, an older trick had been taught for decades: hold a watch flat, point the hour hand at the Sun, and the line halfway between the hour hand and 12 was *roughly south*. **Baden-Powell** wrote it into *Scouting for Boys* in 1908. Generations of boys and girls did it on hot afternoons with stiff brass watches, and it was taught with a wink — useful for a rough orientation, never to be trusted as a compass.

This chapter is about that watch-trick. It is one of the most-quoted bits of outdoor craft, and it is honest only when its caveats are honest with it.

## Land

**The method (Northern Hemisphere).**
1. Hold an *analogue* watch flat in the palm of your hand, dial up.
1. Turn yourself (not the watch) until the **hour hand** points directly at the Sun.
1. Imagine an angle between the hour hand and the **12 o'clock mark**. **Halfway** through that angle is the line that points **south**.
1. Behind it, north.

A worked example. It is **2 p.m.**, the Sun is shining. Point the hour hand at the Sun. The angle between the hour hand (at 2) and 12 is 60°. Halfway through that angle is at the 1 o'clock position. So **1 o'clock on your dial points roughly south.** North is at the 7-o'clock side.

**The method (Southern Hemisphere).**

Reverse the geometry: point **12** at the Sun. Halfway between 12 and the hour hand points roughly **north**.

**British Summer Time and clock-skew.**

The trick assumes your watch shows **solar time**. If the clock has been jumped forward an hour for daylight saving (BST in the UK, summer time elsewhere), the answer drifts by half an hour of dial-rotation, i.e. about 15° of bearing. So in BST, *use 1 o'clock as your "12"* — point the hour hand at the Sun, and bisect to **1**, not to 12. Some Scout manuals teach this as "in BST, use the 1".

## Honest caveats — when this trick lies

This is the bit most websites leave out.

- **Latitude matters.** The trick assumes the Sun is on a vertical north-south arc through noon. That is only true at high mid-latitudes. **Within the tropics the method fails badly** — the Sun can be due north of you at noon (Southern Hemisphere summer in Australia, Northern Hemisphere summer in southern India). For everywhere between roughly 23.5° N and 23.5° S, this trick is no better than guessing.
- **Time of year matters.** Even in the UK, in deep winter, the Sun is so low and so far south that the watch-trick can be off by **10–15°** in the morning and evening. Around solar noon, on a level horizon, in May, it can be within a few degrees. Around 8 a.m. in December it can be 20° wrong.
- **Local solar time is not clock time.** Clock noon is a political convention; solar noon depends on your **longitude** within your time zone. In Cornwall, solar noon can be twenty minutes after clock noon; in Norfolk, ten minutes before. That difference is *also* worth bearing in mind when bisecting the angle.
- **A small magnetised compass is more accurate than this trick will ever be.**

The right way to teach this to children: it is a **rough orientation device**, not a compass. It tells you which side of the field is south to within roughly 10°. That is enough to know which direction you are walking. It is not enough to fix a position on a map.

## Science

- The Sun appears to move at **15° per hour** in the sky. The hour hand of an analogue watch also moves at **30° per hour** on the dial — *twice* as fast. The bisection trick exists because halving the dial angle (30°/h → 15°/h) matches the Sun's apparent motion.
- The trick is at its most accurate at the equinoxes, away from the tropics, on a level horizon, in solar time.
- The "watch" can be *drawn*. A clock face on the back of an envelope, with a pin for the hour hand and the Sun's direction noted, gives the same answer as a real watch. MI9 escape-kit users were sometimes taught to scratch a clock face on a tin lid for exactly this reason.

## Skill — Honest use in the field

- **Use it as a sanity check, not a primary reading.** If your map says the path goes south-west and the watch trick says you're heading south-east, *something is wrong* — but don't yet decide what.
- **Bracket it with another method.** Combine watch-as-compass with the shadow-stick. They should agree to within about 5°. If they disagree by more than 15°, suspect one of them — usually you've forgotten BST, or the Sun is too low, or you're in summer in the tropics.
- **Better still — use it to *check* a real compass.** Hand a child a wristwatch and ask them to find south. Hand them a compass and ask them to find south. Compare. Discuss the difference. That is the lesson.

## Try It — A Watch, the Sun, and a Compass

<div class="vibe vibe-try">

**Three readings, one bearing.** Pick a sunny mid-morning. Stand in a familiar place where you already know which way is south (the front of a house, an end of a football pitch, a known landmark). Take three readings of "where is south?":

1. With an **analogue watch** (or a clock face drawn on paper). Point the hour hand at the Sun, bisect to 12 (or to 1 in BST), and note the bearing.
1. With the **shadow stick** (15-minute method): mark west and east stones, infer south.
1. With a **real magnetic compass**.

Now lay all three on the same sketch. How wide is the spread? In the Fieldbook, write down which method got nearest, and *why* you think the others were off — was it BST? Time of day? The local landscape blocking the horizon? A magnetic anomaly under your feet?

Repeat the experiment in *winter* and again in *summer*, and you will have a small honest study of when each method earns its keep.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Four invitations. Bring a watch, a stick, and a sense of humour.*

1. **Three Souths.** *Grade: Core.* Do the experiment above. Write down the spread between the three methods. *(Pairs with the deck card "Watch as Compass".)*
1. **Drawn Watch.** *Grade: Starter.* On a piece of card, draw a clock face. Use it (no real watch on your wrist) to find south at midday. Was the answer the same as the wristwatch?
1. **BST Trip-Up.** *Grade: Stretch.* On the day the clocks change, do the watch trick at the same time of day before and after. By how much did the apparent south shift? Why?
1. **The Tropic Lie.** *Grade: Stretch.* Look up the rule for the Sun's noon position at latitude 10° N in June. Decide on paper: would the watch trick get south right or wrong, and by how much? The point is to know when *not* to trust the rule.
