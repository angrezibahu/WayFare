## Story

*Read aloud at the top of any local hill, with a view down both sides.*

A shepherd in the Yorkshire Dales in 1948, asked which side of his hill the sheep would be on at lunchtime in March, said: *south side, of course — the sun's been on it all morning, the grass is up, and they're not stupid.* He was right by an instinct he could not have written down. The south-facing slope of any British hill, in March, is **three weeks ahead** of the north-facing slope of the *same* hill. The primroses know it. The sheep know it. The shepherd knew it before he knew the word *aspect*.

This chapter is the small, ordinary craft of *reading two sides of a hill*: knowing which way a slope faces, what that does to the soil and the plants and the snow, and how to use that knowledge to find your way and your warm spot. It is the wayfinding-strand companion to the longer *Reading Terrain* chapter.

## Land

**Aspect — which way a slope faces.** Stand on a slope and look *downhill*. The compass direction you are looking is the slope's **aspect**. A slope you are looking south down has a *south aspect*; a slope you are looking north down has a *north aspect*.

**The two sides of a British hill.**

*South-facing slopes* (NH) — the sun side:
- Receive **direct, longer-lasting sunlight** all year, especially morning to mid-afternoon at noon.
- **Warm faster** in spring; **lose snow first** in winter.
- Soil is **drier**; plants are **earlier** by 2–3 weeks compared to the north side; primroses and bluebells flower first.
- Tend to be **grassier** and lighter-vegetated; on uplands, sheep prefer them.
- Skin and lichens on bark and rock often **paler and crustier** (sun-tolerant species).

*North-facing slopes* (NH) — the shade side:
- Receive **little direct sun**, especially in winter when the Sun stays low in the south.
- **Cool, damp**; **snow lies longer**, often well into late spring on Highland north faces.
- Soil **damper and acidic**; plants are **later**; mosses, ferns, and woodland plants thrive.
- Tend to be **darker green** and richer in shade-loving species (bryophytes, *Asplenium*, woodland flowers).
- Old British woodland (e.g. ancient ash-and-hazel coppices) often *survives best* on north slopes — the south slopes were cleared for pasture, the north slopes were left to wood.

The Southern Hemisphere reverses everything: *north-facing* is the sun side, *south-facing* the shade side.

**Ridge-and-valley thinking.** Every place in hill country is *on a ridge*, *in a valley*, or *on the slope between*. When you are tired or lost, name your ridge and your valley first. The naming is half the navigation.

- **A ridge** is the spine of high ground between two valleys. You can usually walk a ridge a long way without losing height.
- **A valley** drains downhill to a stream or river. Following the valley downstream leads toward people.
- **A col or saddle** is a low point on a ridge — the natural way over.

**Reading terrain on a still day.** Even with no Sun visible:
- Look at *which side of the hill carries snow* (the shaded side, late into spring).
- Look at *which side carries old dark moss and bracken* (the damp side).
- Look at *which side has been cleared for fields* and *which side is wood* (often south = field, north = wood, in old British landscapes).
- Smell the air: a damp leafy smell on one side and a drier grassy smell on the other often gives the aspect away with closed eyes.

## Science

- A **north-facing slope** in Britain may receive only **70%** of the direct sunlight of a *south-facing* slope at the same latitude, integrated over a year. The difference is largest in winter (when the Sun's elevation is lowest) and smallest in midsummer (when the Sun passes near vertical at midday on the longest day).
- The temperature difference between north and south slopes can run to **2–4 °C** in soil temperature on the same hill in spring, with knock-on effects on flowering dates.
- *Snow patches* on the **north-east** facing corries of the Cairngorms occasionally last all year — the **Garbh Choire** snow patch has, in some recent years, persisted into the next winter.
- **Frost hollows** form where cold air drains downhill on still nights and pools at the bottom of a valley. Plants there flower *later* than the slope above them — the valley floor can be *colder than the ridge top*. Aspect is not the only thing; *cold-air drainage* is the second thing.
- **Aspect ecology** is the study of how slope direction shapes biological communities — a field with a long British tradition in upland sheep-farming and oak-woodland research.

## Skill — Walking with aspect in mind

- **Plan your warmth.** On a cold winter day, plan your lunch break on a *south-facing slope*; you will be ten minutes' worth of warmer, with the same thermos.
- **Plan your damp.** In summer, plan your hot-day rest on a *north-facing slope* under a north-facing wood — five degrees cooler and shaded.
- **Use aspect to set direction on a dull day.** On a still, overcast day with no Sun, look at *which slope carries the most snow* (shade-side), or *which slope is wooded versus grassy* (often north = wood, south = field). It is rough — within 30° — but it costs nothing.
- **Look for *holloways* and old paths** on the *south-facing* slope; people walked the warmer side for warmth and dryness through the centuries. Many old footpaths in Britain *bias south-facing*; this is why a holloway is a clue to the south side of a valley.
- **Combine aspect with the prevailing wind chapter.** A *south-facing slope* sheltered from a *south-westerly wind* — i.e. a slope tucked behind a south-east-facing ridge — is a *thermal sweet-spot*. Old farms often sit there for a reason.

## Try It — The Two-Sides Traverse

<div class="vibe vibe-try">

**One hill, two slopes, two readings.** Pick a hill near home with a clear summit and clear north and south flanks. On a *spring* morning, walk to the south-facing slope first. Without rushing:

- Sit for **five minutes** facing downhill. Note what flowers or grasses you see, the colour of the grass, the smell of the air, the temperature on your face. Sketch one square metre of ground in the Fieldbook.

Then cross over the ridge to the *north-facing* slope. Sit for five minutes again, facing downhill. Note all the same things. Sketch a different square metre of ground.

Now compare the two sketches.

- **Are the plants the same species?** Almost never. North slopes carry more woodland and shade-loving species; south slopes more grassland and sun-loving.
- **Are the flowers the same time?** In April, the south side is usually two to three weeks ahead.
- **Is the ground the same dampness?** North side will be damper, often with darker soil.
- **What does this tell you about the *whole hill*?** It tells you the south side is the human side: drier, warmer, walkable in winter — which is why the path you took up has a holloway on the south flank and a stone wall on the north.

A two-sides traverse repeated on the same hill across a year will turn into a small natural-history record of one place. Do it once a season for four years and your child will own the hill.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Five invitations to know two sides of one hill.*

1. **Aspect Diary.** *Grade: Core.* Do the two-sides traverse on a local hill in spring. Compare three plants on each side. *(Pairs with the deck card "Aspect Diary" in the Reading Terrain chapter.)*
1. **Snow Patch Watch.** *Grade: Stretch.* In late spring, find a snow patch surviving on a north-facing slope near home. Photograph it; note the date. The next year, photograph it again on the same date. Are the patches the same size?
1. **Name Your Ridge.** *Grade: Starter.* If your local ridge has no name on the OS map, name it yourselves and put the name on the family Year Map. *(Pairs with the deck card "Name Your Valley" in the Reading Terrain chapter.)*
1. **Holloway Hunt.** *Grade: Core.* Walk a 1 km stretch of old footpath near home. Does it run along the south side of a valley, the north side, or the bottom? Why might that be?
1. **Aspect on a Dull Day.** *Grade: Stretch.* On an overcast day with no Sun, walk a hill *guessing* which slope is which by snow, plants, and damp. Then check with a compass at the top. How close were your guesses?
