## Story

*Read aloud on the morning of a long walk.*

When **Alexander the Great** marched east in the 320s BC, he took with him a small, quiet detachment of professional pacers called **bematists**. Their job was to count their footsteps every step of the way — through Babylon, through the Iranian plateau, across the Hindu Kush, into the Punjab. Their counts, written down each evening, became the master-distances on the maps the Greeks made of Asia for the next thousand years. Modern measurements show the bematists' figures, paced out on dust and rock, were within **a fraction of a percent** of the truth over thousands of kilometres.

A child's stride, used carefully, is the same instrument that measured the breadth of an empire. This chapter is how to teach a stride to count.

## Land

**The pace-count.** A *pace* is the stride from one foot-strike to the next of the *same* foot. (A *step* — left, right — is half a pace; we count paces, not steps, because they are more even.)

**Find your 100-metre pace-count.**
1. Find a measured 100 m stretch. A standard football pitch goal-line to halfway is roughly 50 m, so end-to-end is about 100 m. A 400 m running track has 100 m markers. An athletics field is best.
1. Stand at one end. Walk at your *normal* relaxed pace to the other end.
1. Each time your **right foot** strikes the ground, count: *one, two, three…*
1. The number you have at the far end is your **100 m pace-count**.

For a tall adult, this is around **60–65** paces. For a 10-year-old, perhaps **70–75**. For an 8-year-old, around **80–90**. The number is your own; learn it.

**From pace-count to distance.** From now on, on any flat path, you can measure distance:
- 100 m = your count.
- 1 km = ten times your count.
- A "tactical kilometre" — the figure used by infantry navigators — is therefore something you can measure with no equipment.

**Adjustments.** Your 100 m count is *not constant*. It changes with:
- **Slope.** Going *uphill*, your stride shortens — pace-count goes *up*. Downhill, the opposite (with care for safety). On a moderate uphill, expect pace-count to rise by 10–20%.
- **Terrain.** Bog, scree, soft sand — pace-count rises sharply.
- **Tiredness.** A tired walker takes shorter strides.
- **Pack weight.** A heavy rucksack shortens stride; pace-count rises by 5–10%.
- **Children.** Pace-count is *very* sensitive to whether the child is excited (long strides) or grumpy (short ones). Practice settles this; do not expect adult-level steadiness from a child's first attempts.

**Ranger beads.** A **set of nine beads on a thong** is the classic counting tool. (Often there's a second strand of four beads above, for kilometres.)
- Slide one bead down per 100 m.
- When all nine are down, that's 900 m. The next 100 m: slide one *up* on the kilometre strand and reset the 100 m strand to the top. You have just counted **1 km**.
- A beginner with ranger beads and a measured pace-count can pace a 5 km walk to within **±5%** in fair conditions.

**Naismith's Rule.** A complementary tool: **1 hour per 5 km on the flat, plus 1 hour per 600 m of ascent.** For children and tired adults, add half again. Pace-count *plus* Naismith gives both **distance** and **time** — the full pair a navigator needs.

## Dead reckoning, in plain English

**Dead reckoning** is what sailors and navigators do when there is no GPS: from a known starting point, keep a careful record of *direction*, *speed* (or pace-count), and *time*. With those three numbers you can calculate where you must be, even with no landmarks. Every voyage Christopher Columbus made and every flight a Battle-of-Britain pilot made started with dead reckoning, and most of them ended exactly where the navigator's pencil said they would.

For a child outdoors:
1. **Direction** — a compass bearing or "the path I'm following".
1. **Speed** — your pace-count gives this.
1. **Time** — a watch.

A leg planned in advance: *from this gate, walk 230 paces (≈ 350 m) on a bearing of 040°, then turn left and follow the wall*. With those three numbers, even in mist, even with no signposts, even with a phone in the pocket on flight-mode, the child cannot get lost. *That* is what dead reckoning gives.

## Science

- A walking *cadence* — steps per minute — is roughly **100–120** for a brisk adult, **120–140** for a child trying to keep up. Cadence varies with leg length and gait.
- The Roman **mile** was *mille passus*, "a thousand paces" — and a *passus* was a *pace* in the bematist sense (left-right-left, the distance between successive strikes of the same foot). A Roman mile is about 1,480 m. A modern mile (1,609 m) drifted longer over the centuries.
- *Pace consistency* is a trained skill: with practice over a season, a careful walker can hold their 100 m pace-count to within ±2 paces on flat ground. With *no* practice, expect ±5–10 paces variability.
- **Stride length** scales (for adults) at roughly **0.40 × height** in metres, but is highly individual. A child's stride scales differently — short legs, faster cadence, lower momentum.

## Skill — Pace-count + bearing in the field

- **Plan the leg before you walk it.** Each leg of a route gets *one bearing, one pace-count*. Write them down. Then walk.
- **Count in fives or tens.** Most people lose the count if they try to hold a four-digit number in their head. Slide a ranger-bead every 100 m and reset the count.
- **Use a *catching feature*** beyond your target (see Reading Terrain). If your pace-count says you've arrived but the catching feature isn't in sight, you've under-paced; if you hit the catching feature first, you've overshot. Either way you know what to do.
- **Aim off** — deliberately walk to one side of your target so that when you hit the catching feature you know which way to turn.

## Try It — The 100-Metre Pace and a Mystery Walk

<div class="vibe vibe-try">

**Step one — find your number.** On a calm afternoon, find a 100 m stretch (a football pitch end-to-end works; a measured stretch on a quiet pavement works too). Walk it three times at your normal pace. Each time, count each right-foot strike. Take the **average** of the three counts. That is your 100 m pace-count. Write it inside the front cover of the Fieldbook in pencil — your number will change as you grow.

**Step two — the mystery walk.** Now have a grown-up plan, in secret, a short three-leg route in a park or quiet area: *N paces on bearing X, then M paces on bearing Y, then P paces on bearing Z*. The grown-up writes only the bearings and pace-counts on a slip of paper, *not* the destination. The child gets the slip, a compass, and a wristwatch. Walk it. Where did you arrive? Was it where the grown-up expected? If not, by how much, and why?

This is dead reckoning, child-shaped. After three or four mystery walks, the child has a working sense of distance-by-foot that no map app can give.

</div>

## Stretch — Challenges

*Five invitations to make the feet a measuring tool.*

1. **Pace-Count 100 m.** *Grade: Core.* Find your number. *(Pairs with the deck card "Pace-Count 100m".)*
1. **Hill vs Flat.** *Grade: Core.* Take your pace-count on flat ground, then on a moderate uphill. Subtract. By how many paces did the slope cost you per 100 m?
1. **Make a Set of Ranger Beads.** *Grade: Starter.* From paracord and beads (or buttons!), make a 9-and-4 bead pace-counter. Test it on a 1 km walk.
1. **A Mystery Mile.** *Grade: Stretch.* Plan a 1 km walk for a sibling using only bearings and pace-counts. Walk it together; see how close you get.
1. **Pace-Diary.** *Grade: Stretch.* Keep a column in the Fieldbook for *paces walked today* on every walk for a season. By the end you will have walked, in counted footsteps, a measurable number of kilometres of your own life.
