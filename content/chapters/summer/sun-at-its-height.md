## Story

*Read aloud on the longest day, if you can.*

**Stonehenge at midsummer.** On the morning of the summer solstice — around 21 June, give or take a day — the Sun rises over the Heel Stone and shines straight down the avenue into the centre of Stonehenge. The people who raised those stones four and a half thousand years ago did it so that one morning a year, the Sun itself would come home. They did not leave us any writing, so we do not know what they *thought* about it. But we know, from the stones alone, that they watched the Sun carefully enough to know exactly where it would rise on the longest day.

**The same morning, a thousand miles north, at Newgrange in Ireland**, it is the opposite — the *midwinter* Sun that slides down a long stone passage and lights the back wall for seventeen minutes. One people, watching from opposite ends of the year. Stones as instruments.

**Eratosthenes (Greek, c. 240 BC).** In the Egyptian city of Syene, on the day of the summer solstice at noon, the Sun shone straight down a deep well with no shadow. At the same moment in Alexandria, 800 km north, an upright stick cast a shadow at 7.2°. Eratosthenes reasoned that if the Earth were flat, the Sun would hit both places the same way. Since the shadows differed by 7.2° — which is 1/50 of a circle — the distance between the two cities must be 1/50 of the Earth's circumference. Multiply by 50, and the Earth is about 40,000 km around. He was right within a few per cent. With a stick.

## Land

**The Sun at solstice noon is at its highest all year.** In southern Britain that's roughly 62° above the southern horizon. Shadows are at their shortest. A six-foot adult at midsummer noon casts a shadow only about three feet long.

**Shadows at dawn and dusk are enormous.** At sunrise and sunset in midsummer, your shadow can stretch forty metres across a meadow. Long shadows reveal ridges, furrows, ant-hills, and the ghosts of old ploughed fields. If you want to see the shape of the land, go out when the Sun is low. Aerial archaeologists do all their best work in the first and last hour of light.

**The Sun rises and sets far north of east and west.** On the solstice in the south of England, the Sun rises about 50° north of east and sets about 50° north of west. You can watch this all summer if you have a flat eastern horizon — a field, a beach, a rooftop. Pick a landmark on the horizon each week. The Sun will slide back south as summer ages.

**Heat rises in waves.** On a hot afternoon over tarmac or dry rock, the air shimmers — you are seeing light bent by columns of warmer, thinner air rising from the ground. This is the same shimmer that becomes a mirage over a long road: the sky reflected in the hot air just above the surface, looking like water.

**Dry ground tells.** After a week of summer heat, the first plants to wilt are the ones on south-facing slopes and thin soil. Trees on thin soil lose their leaves earliest in a drought. In old maps, the line between lush and parched follows the geology exactly. Chalk browns first. Clay holds on. Peat sets on fire.

## Science

- The Sun's height at noon changes through the year because the Earth's axis is tilted at about 23.5°. At the solstice, your hemisphere is tilted as far *towards* the Sun as it ever gets. Six months later, as far away. In between, the equinoxes — equal day and night everywhere on Earth.
- On the solstice, the Sun is directly overhead at the Tropic of Cancer (23.5° N). At the winter solstice, overhead at the Tropic of Capricorn. "Tropic" comes from Greek *tropē*, "turning": the latitudes where the Sun *turns back*.
- Heat stroke begins when the body cannot cool itself — usually because it cannot sweat fast enough, or because the air is so humid that sweat cannot evaporate. Signs: headache, confusion, nausea, stopped sweating. Treatment: shade, water, cool wet cloth, and rest. Do not ignore it, especially in children.
- The hottest time of day is not noon but about 2–4pm. The ground keeps heating up for hours after the Sun's peak, and releases heat into the air. Clear nights, the reverse — the coldest time is just before dawn.

## Skill — Wayfaring in heat

- **Walk at the edges of the day.** Dawn and dusk are cool, beautiful, full of wildlife, and miles easier on your body. Plan a summer day-walk to be done by noon, or to start after tea.
- **Shade is water.** A tree, a hedge, a wall in shadow — in hot weather these are survival resources. Map them on the way out so you know where to rest on the way back.
- **One litre per hour in real heat.** Carry more water than you think. A child in direct sun on a chalk hill in August needs to drink regularly whether they say they're thirsty or not — by the time you feel thirsty, you are already behind.
- **Hats are not optional.** The top of the head is the single fastest route to heat stroke, especially for children. A wide-brim hat is better than a cap; a cap is better than nothing.
- **Feet matter more in summer.** Hot ground, dry socks, blisters that bloom in the first mile. Swap socks at the halfway point on a hot long walk; some people carry talc. Boots laced a touch looser than in winter, because feet swell.

## Stretch — Challenges

1. **Solstice Shadow.** On a sunny day near the summer solstice, measure your shadow at noon (local solar noon — check a sunrise/sunset website). Multiply by roughly ten to estimate your height from the Sun angle. How close? *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Dawn Sun Landmarks.** For three mornings in June or July, note where the Sun rises relative to a landmark on your horizon. Sketch the horizon three times. Has it moved? *Grade: Core.*
1. **Eratosthenes.** Pair up with a family in another county or country (by phone or post). On the same sunny day, each of you measures the shadow of a one-metre stick at local solar noon. Compare. You have just measured the Earth. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Low-Sun Walk.** Walk a familiar field or park in the last hour before sunset. What do the long shadows reveal that midday hides? Sketch one hidden feature. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Shade Map.** For one hot day, map every shade-spot on a 1-mile route (trees, walls, bus shelters). This is a survival map in a heatwave. *Grade: Starter.*
