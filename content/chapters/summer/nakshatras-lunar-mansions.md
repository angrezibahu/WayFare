## Story

*A summer chapter, paired with the Moon. The lunar mansions are visible all year, but summer's long warm nights are the best time to walk all 27 of them across a month. Read on a clear evening with the Moon up.*

**The Moon's twenty-seven houses.** The **Nakshatra** system is a way of dividing the path the Moon walks against the stars into 27 equal segments — *lunar mansions* — each named for the bright star or asterism that marks it. The Moon takes about 27.3 days to circle the sky once against the background stars (its **sidereal month**), so it spends roughly one full day in each nakshatra. Nakshatra means "that which never decays" — a bright unchanging star — and by extension, the segment of sky around it. The system is the spine of the Hindu calendar, of much classical Indian astrology, of marriage dates, naming ceremonies, festival timings, and farming almanacs. It has been in continuous use for at least three thousand years.

**The twenty-seven daughters of Daksha.** The mythological frame, told in the *Mahābhārata* and the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa*: the patriarch **Daksha** had twenty-seven beautiful daughters and gave them all in marriage to **Chandra** (the Moon). Chandra was meant to spend equal time with each — one night per wife — but he became infatuated with **Rohiṇī** (Aldebaran, the red one) and would not leave her side. The other twenty-six wives complained to their father. Daksha cursed Chandra to wither and die. The other gods, alarmed (the Moon disappearing would unbalance the world), pleaded the curse down to a milder form: Chandra would wax and wane every month rather than die outright. The waxing and waning Moon is Chandra recovering and re-darkening, month after month — and his nightly travel through the sky, one nakshatra after another, is him fulfilling, at last, his promise to visit each wife in turn. The Moon's monthly cycle has a story baked into it that explains both its wandering and its phases.

**Walking the wives.** The 27 nakshatras are listed in the *Atharvaveda* (around 1100 BCE) in an order that begins with **Krittikā** (the Pleiades) — placing the system at a time when the spring equinox sat in Krittikā, around 2300 BCE. Later texts begin the list with **Aśvinī**, reflecting the slow precession of the equinoxes. The system has been recalibrated repeatedly over three millennia; the names of the wives are constant, the order they are visited has been adjusted to match the sky as it actually is. Modern panchāṅga calendars correct for *ayanāṃśa*, the precessional offset, so the nakshatra you read off the panchāṅga *is* where the Moon is tonight.

**A note on framing.** This is not an academic curiosity. A wedding date in Mumbai next month will be chosen by which nakshatra the Moon sits in; a baby's name is often based on the syllables associated with the nakshatra of birth; the *Karva Chauth* fast and *Karthik Pūrṇimā* festival are timed to the Moon's position relative to specific nakshatras. The 27-fold sky lives in millions of decisions a year. We name what is alive.

## Sky

Walking the 27 nakshatras is something you can do in a single lunar month, on the nights the Moon is up, by following it from one bright star to the next. **Junction stars are anchors.** Nine of the 27 junction stars are top-tier bright stars (Aldebaran, Betelgeuse, Regulus, Spica, Arcturus, Antares, Altair, Pollux, Markab). Once you know these nine, you have nine of the 27 nakshatras pinned down by direct sight. The others are fainter and need a star chart for the first few times, and become familiar by the third lunar month of looking.

**The Moon's monthly walk.** Each night, the Moon shifts about 13° eastwards against the stars — roughly the width of two and a half nakshatras every two days. By the end of a lunar month, it has been in all 27 (with one nakshatra visited twice in some months because of the half-day fractional fit). Watching this directly — Moon near Aldebaran tonight, near Betelgeuse in two days, near Pollux in four — is the simplest, slowest, most rewarding sky-skill you can take up.

## Science

- **Three thousand years of calendrical use.** The earliest extant nakshatra list is in the **Atharvaveda** (XIX.7), conventionally dated around 1100 BCE. The *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa*, the earliest standalone Indian astronomical text (c. 1400–1200 BCE), uses the system to compute lunar months and the timings of Vedic ritual. The classical text **Sūrya Siddhānta** (c. 4th–5th century CE) gives precise positions of junction stars and methods for predicting eclipses, occultations, and conjunctions in nakshatra terms. The Hindustani astronomical tradition is one of the longest unbroken observational records on Earth.
- **A sidereal, not tropical, system.** The nakshatras are measured against the actual stars, not against the Sun's position relative to the equinoxes. This means they don't drift with the seasons — Krittikā (the Pleiades) is always Krittikā, regardless of where the equinox falls. The Western tropical zodiac, by contrast, is anchored to the equinox and so the "constellations" of the zodiac no longer match the sky. Tropical Aries is currently a long way from the actual constellation Aries. The nakshatras have no such drift.
- **The 27.3-day fit.** The Moon's sidereal period is 27.32 days, which doesn't fit a whole number of nakshatras of 13°20' each. The system therefore uses a 28th *abhijit* (Vega) inserted to absorb the extra time in some calculations — and modern panchāṅgas use precise astronomical computation to give nakshatra durations of varying length on different nights. The system is not a 27-day flat division; it is a precise sidereal lunar position, named.
- **The nakshatras and Chinese xiu, and Arabic manāzil.** The Chinese **lunar mansion** system (*xiù*) has 28 mansions and is independently attested in oracle-bone inscriptions from the Shang dynasty (c. 1300 BCE). The Arabic **manzil** system also has 28 mansions and is described in classical Arabic astronomy. The three systems share some boundaries and some bright-star anchors but are clearly independent traditions; the question of whether they share an even older common source is debated by scholars without resolution. What is undisputed: three of the four major literate civilisations on Earth invented similar lunar-mansion systems independently within a few centuries of each other, suggesting that subdividing the Moon's path against the stars is a near-universal observational instinct.
- **Why precession matters here.** The Atharvaveda list begins with Krittikā; later lists begin with Aśvinī. The shift reflects the **precession of the equinoxes** — the same 26,000-year wobble that moves the Pole star slowly through the sky. The Hindustani astronomers were aware of this from at least the *Sūrya Siddhānta* and recorded the offset (*ayanāṃśa*) numerically. Modern panchāṅgas use ayanāṃśa values around 24° to convert tropical positions to sidereal. The sky has turned 24° in the last two thousand years; the calendar still works.

## Skill — Walking the Sky with the Moon

- **The Moon as guide.** Once you know the 27 names, you can use the Moon as a tour guide: find the Moon, find which nakshatra it sits in tonight, learn the junction star, and you have learned one new bright star. Repeat for one lunar month; you have walked all 27.
- **Nine bright anchors first.** Start with the nine top-tier junction stars (the bold ones in the table above). These are stars you almost certainly know, or will know, from the other chapters in this book: Aldebaran (winter), Betelgeuse (winter), Pollux (winter), Regulus (spring), Spica (spring), Arcturus (spring), Antares (summer), Altair (summer), Markab (autumn). Once you have these nine pinned to their Sanskrit names, the Hindustani sky is two-thirds in your hand.
- **A pañcāṅga in your pocket.** Bookmark a panchāṅga site (drikpanchang.com is the most reliable). Look up tonight's nakshatra before you go outside. Note the deity, the auspicious times, the symbol — these are not idle ornament; they are how Hindustani families decide when to sign contracts, hold ceremonies, plant crops. You will be reading the same calendar millions of households read tonight.
- **A waxing-Moon walk.** From new moon, follow the waxing crescent through the evening sky — Moon in the west on day 1, slightly higher on day 2, more westward and fuller on day 3. As you do, note which bright star the Moon is near each evening. By full moon, two weeks in, you will have a Moon-and-junction-star diary that traces the ecliptic across half the sky.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations to walk the Moon's twenty-seven houses. Several of these are multi-week — patience is the chapter.*

1. **Tonight's nakshatra.** Look up tonight's nakshatra on a panchāṅga site. Note the name, the deity, the symbol. Find the junction star in the sky. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Nine anchors.** Over a season, learn the nine bright junction stars (Aldebaran, Betelgeuse, Pollux, Regulus, Spica, Arcturus, Antares, Altair, Markab) and the Sanskrit name of the nakshatra each anchors (Rohiṇī, Ārdrā, Punarvasu, Maghā, Citrā, Svātī, Jyeṣṭhā, Śravaṇa, Pūrva-Bhādrapadā). Speak each pair aloud once with the star in view. *Grade: Core.*
1. **A lunar-month diary.** Over one full lunar month (27.3 days), note each evening which nakshatra the Moon sits in and which junction star is closest. Twenty-seven entries. By the end, the Moon will have walked the whole sky in front of you. *Grade: Core.*
1. **A wedding-date sky.** Find out the nakshatra of a wedding in your family or community (ask a relative who knows; or look up a friend's wedding date in retrospect). Note which junction star marked the sky on that day. Trace the Moon's relationship to that star. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Read the Atharvaveda nakshatra hymn.** Atharvaveda XIX.7 lists the nakshatras with epithets. Whitney's English translation is on archive.org. Read it aloud once, slowly. The hymn is short — about a page. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Three lunar mansion systems on one chart.** Make a single Fieldbook page that lists, for any one night, the Hindustani nakshatra, the Chinese *xiu*, and the Arabic *manzil* the Moon sits in. Three systems, one Moon. (The Wikipedia page "Lunar mansion" is a passable cross-reference index for this; the underlying astronomy is the same.) *Grade: Stretch.*
