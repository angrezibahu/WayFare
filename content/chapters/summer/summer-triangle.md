## Story

*A summer chapter. Read outside on a warm evening between June and September, when the Triangle is overhead.*

**The Weaver Girl and the Cowherd (China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam).** The seventh day of the seventh lunar month is **Qixi**. The story: a celestial weaver, Zhinü — the star **Vega** — fell in love with a mortal cowherd, Niulang — the star **Altair**. Heaven disapproved and placed a river between them, the **Milky Way**. Once a year, on Qixi, a bridge of magpies forms across the river and the lovers are allowed to meet for one night. Families look up on that night to see if Vega and Altair are "bright" — if they are, the lovers are crossing. The star between them, **Deneb**, is sometimes the bridge; sometimes it is simply another star keeping watch. This story has been told for more than two thousand years and is the reason Qixi is sometimes called the Chinese Valentine’s Day — though it is much older and stranger than that phrasing suggests.

**The falling eagle and the swan (Greek tradition, via Arab astronomy).** The Greeks named each corner separately: **Lyra**, the lyre of Orpheus, whose music could soften stones; **Cygnus**, a swan in flight down the Milky Way; **Aquila**, the eagle that carried Zeus’s thunderbolts. The Arabs kept the Greek patterns but renamed the stars in careful detail. **Vega** is *al-Nasr al-Wāqi‘*, "the falling eagle" (folded wings). **Altair** is *al-Nasr al-Ṭā’ir*, "the flying eagle" (open wings). **Deneb** is *Dhanab al-Dajāja*, "the tail of the hen." The two eagles face each other across the Milky Way; between them, a swan flies south along the river. When you look up on a July night, you are reading that same sky.

**Keoe (Hawai‘i — referenced).** Vega is the **zenith star** of Hawai‘i: at Hawai‘i’s latitude, it passes directly overhead. Polynesian navigators used zenith stars as latitude markers on long ocean crossings. We mention this and do not teach it. Seek out Nainoa Thompson and the Polynesian Voyaging Society to hear this knowledge from its people.

## Sky

The Summer Triangle is the three brightest stars of summer nights in the Northern Hemisphere, forming a huge, lopsided triangle that rides almost directly overhead from July through September. Once you have it, half the summer sky falls into place around it.

- **Vega** — brightest of the three. High in the east in June, almost overhead in August. A hard, white, steady light. The head of the lyre.
- **Deneb** — northeast of Vega. The tail of the swan Cygnus, whose wings stretch down the Milky Way. Cygnus is also called the **Northern Cross** — a clean cross-shape with Deneb at the top.
- **Altair** — southeast of Vega, lower than the other two. The middle star of a short row of three: two fainter stars flank Altair, like eyes either side of a nose. That little row is the head of Aquila the eagle.

**The Milky Way runs through it.** On any dark-sky night away from town, look up at the Triangle and trace the pale river of stars that passes between Vega and Altair. That is the disc of our own galaxy, seen edge-on. Cygnus flies along it. Deneb is inside it. If you have never seen the Milky Way, a July night on a dark hill is the right time to fix that.

**How to find each corner.**

- **From Vega** — the brightest. It is almost impossible to miss overhead in August.
- **From the Plough** — extend the handle’s curve *away* from Arcturus and on past; you’ll pass through Vega on the way towards the south.
- **Cygnus the cross** — trace the line from Deneb down through the heart of the cross (Sadr) and on to Albireo at its foot. Albireo, in a small telescope, splits into a gold-and-blue double star, one of the prettiest sights in the sky.

## Science

- **Vega** is only 25 light-years away, twice the Sun’s mass, and twice its temperature. Its spectrum was the zero-point reference for the whole magnitude system — every other star’s brightness is measured against it. In about 13,000 years, Earth’s axial precession will point at Vega, and it will be our pole star instead of Polaris. That’s happened before and will happen again; the sky above us turns on a slow wheel of its own.
- **Deneb** is a hundred times further than Vega — about 2,600 light-years — and yet still one of the brightest stars in the sky, which means it is enormously luminous: some 200,000 Suns. It is a blue supergiant. It will go supernova within a few million years.
- **Altair** is 17 light-years away. It spins so fast — once every nine hours — that it, like Regulus, is squashed into an oval. Photographs made with interferometers actually show this.
- **Cygnus X-1**, hidden in the neck of the swan, was the first object confirmed to be a **black hole**. It cannot be seen by eye, but when you look at Cygnus you are looking towards it.
- The **Cygnus Rift** is a dark lane running through the Milky Way inside the swan — not a hole in the galaxy, but vast clouds of interstellar dust blocking the light of stars behind. The "missing" band of sky is, in a sense, the sky *fuller* — full of cold matter.

## Skill — Navigating by the Triangle

- **Vega is a summer zenith star for mid-northern latitudes.** For Britain, Vega passes almost overhead around midnight in early July. If a star passes overhead from your location, you are at that star’s declination in latitude. Polynesian navigators used this trick to hit Hawai‘i on thousand-mile crossings.
- **The Northern Cross points south.** The long axis of Cygnus (Deneb to Albireo) points roughly south on summer evenings. Not a precision tool — but if you’re in a wood with no horizon, the sky can still tell you which way is which.
- **The Milky Way is a rough east-west line in summer.** In July the band of the Milky Way runs from the northeast (through Cygnus) down to the southwest (into Sagittarius and the galactic centre). Learning to read the Milky Way as a band, rather than a smear, is a dark-sky skill worth a whole summer.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations under summer skies. Warm nights reward the patient looker — take a blanket.*

1. **Find the Triangle.** On a clear summer evening between 10 and midnight, step outside, look straight up and a little south, and see if you can pick out the three brightest stars. Cross-check Vega against Stellarium to be sure. Note the date, the time, and the sky. *Grade: Starter. Unlocks the rest of this chapter’s field triggers.*
1. **The Northern Cross.** See if you can trace out all six stars of Cygnus. Sketch what you see in the Fieldbook. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Name the three.** See if you can learn *al-Nasr al-Wāqi‘* (Vega), *al-Nasr al-Ṭā’ir* (Altair), and *Dhanab al-Dajāja* (Deneb) — and what each Arabic name is actually saying about the bird it belongs to. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Milky Way spotting.** On the darkest night you can reach, lie on your back with the Triangle above you and trace the Milky Way with your finger from one horizon to the other. If you can't see it at all, that is itself a finding worth noting — the light pollution and where you were. *Grade: Core. Counts towards the Summer Capstone.*
1. **Albireo.** With binoculars or a small telescope, find Albireo (at the foot of the cross). Describe the two colours in the Fieldbook in your own words. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Zenith test.** On a clear July midnight, lie on your back and see which star is closest to straight overhead. Is it Vega? If so, you have just measured your own latitude, roughly — Vega's declination is about +38°. *Grade: Stretch.*

**The science.** NASA Kepler mission — the Kepler telescope observed a patch of sky in Cygnus for four years; most of its exoplanet discoveries lie within the Summer Triangle; search "Kepler mission Cygnus exoplanets" for the summary.

**The cultural context.** Anne Birrell, *Chinese Mythology: An Introduction* (Johns Hopkins University Press, 1993) — careful scholarly source for the Qixi / Cowherd and Weaver Girl story; read the primary tradition rather than a retelling.
