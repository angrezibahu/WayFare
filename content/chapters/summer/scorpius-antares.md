## Story

*A summer chapter. Read on a warm July or August evening, facing south, low to the horizon. In Britain, Scorpius never climbs high — but its heart, Antares, is unmistakable.*

**The scorpion that killed Orion (Greek, via Mesopotamian).** When Orion boasted he would kill every animal on Earth, Gaia — the Earth herself — sent up a scorpion from a crack in the ground to sting him. The scorpion won. Both were placed in the sky, but on opposite sides, so that they would never meet. When Orion rises in winter, Scorpius sets; when Scorpius rises in summer, Orion is nowhere to be seen. Watch this for a year and you will prove it for yourself. It is one of the oldest and most satisfying sky-stories on Earth — and the pattern of "a scorpion in the south in summer" goes back at least to the Babylonians, more than three thousand years ago. The Greeks inherited the story along with the stars.

**Qalb al-ʿAqrab — the heart of the scorpion (Arabic).** The bright red star at the middle of Scorpius is **Antares**. The name is Greek: *anti-Ares*, meaning "rival of Mars" — because it is red like Mars, and when the planet passes near it in the sky, the two reds compete. The Arabic name is plainer and older: *Qalb al-ʿAqrab*, "the heart of the scorpion." In the medieval Arab tradition, Antares was one of the four **royal stars**, guardians of the four cardinal points through the year: Regulus (north, spring), Antares (south, summer), Fomalhaut (west, autumn), Aldebaran (east, winter). These four anchored the calendar of the sky.

**Te Matau a Māui — the fishhook of Māui (Aotearoa New Zealand — referenced).** Māori tradition sees not a scorpion but a **great fishhook**: the hook with which the demigod **Māui** fished up the North Island of New Zealand (Te Ika-a-Māui, "the fish of Māui") from the sea. The curl of Scorpius's tail is the barb of the hook. This is a story with its own keepers. We do not retell it. The Society for Māori Astronomy Research and Traditions (SMART) has published Māori tellings — read them directly.

**Ka Makau Nui o Māui (Hawai‘i — referenced).** The Hawaiian tradition also sees a fishhook: "the great fishhook of Māui." Polynesian navigators used Scorpius as a direction and season marker on voyages across the Pacific. Again: we reference, we do not teach. The Polynesian Voyaging Society holds this knowledge.

## Sky

Scorpius is one of the few constellations that looks exactly like its name: a scorpion with a curved tail and a red star for its heart. From Britain it never climbs high — the tail sometimes stays below the southern horizon — but the head and heart are findable on any clear summer evening.

- **The Head** — three stars in a short vertical line to the upper right: Graffias, Dschubba, Pi Scorpii. These are the claws.
- **Antares** — the heart, a warm red supergiant, unmistakable. It is the one obviously red star in the summer southern sky.
- **The Body and Tail** — a long curve of bright stars sweeping down from Antares and back up into a hook — the stinger. The two stars at the stinger are **Shaula** (Arabic *Al-Shaulah*, "the raised tail") and **Lesath** (*Al-Lasʿah*, "the sting"). From the UK, the stinger often skims the horizon or stays just below it. From southern latitudes it stands straight up.
- **Between the stinger and Sagittarius** lie some of the richest Milky Way fields in the sky — globular clusters, open clusters, emission nebulae. This region is where the centre of our galaxy lies.

**How to find it — from the Summer Triangle.** On a summer evening, find the Summer Triangle overhead (Vega, Deneb, Altair). Drop your line of sight from Altair down to the south. Low in the southern sky, you'll see a bright red star alone in a field of fainter ones — Antares. Scan above and below for the scorpion's curve.

**Low southern horizon matters.** From southern England, Antares sits maybe 10–15° above the southern horizon at its highest. This means you need a clear, unobstructed southern view — a south-facing beach, hilltop, or open field is ideal. Houses, trees, and haze will hide it. Summer holidays on the south coast are the time to catch it.

## Science

- **Antares** is a **red supergiant** — even bigger than Betelgeuse. If you put it at the Sun's position, its surface would reach past the orbit of Mars. It is 550 light-years away, give or take, and it is genuinely reaching the end of its life. Like Betelgeuse, it will go supernova on an astronomical timescale — within a few hundred thousand years. When it does, it will be briefly visible in daylight.
- **Antares has a small blue companion** hidden in its glare, unresolvable by eye but a delight in a telescope on a steady night.
- The **galactic centre** of the Milky Way lies in the direction of Sagittarius, just east of the scorpion's tail. At the centre is a supermassive black hole called **Sagittarius A***, with the mass of four million Suns. You cannot see it — dust blocks the visible light — but you are staring directly toward it on any summer night. The first images of Sagittarius A* were released in 2022 by the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration.
- The region near the stinger is one of the densest parts of our view of our own galaxy. With binoculars, slow-sweep above the stinger and you will see star clusters one after another — **M6** (the Butterfly Cluster), **M7** (Ptolemy's Cluster, catalogued by name nearly two thousand years ago), the **Bug Nebula**, the **Cat's Paw Nebula**. Ptolemy's Cluster is the oldest recorded star cluster in the Western tradition.

## Skill — Reading Low Summer, Orion's Counterweight

- **Antares is Orion's counterweight.** The two constellations are positioned on almost exactly opposite sides of the sky. When Orion rises, Scorpius sets. When Scorpius rises, Orion is gone. If you know this, you can use the absence of one to date the other — a rough seasonal check that works anywhere on Earth.
- **Antares as a southern marker at British latitudes.** At mid-northern latitudes, Antares is so far south that it tells you, within a few degrees, where due south is at its highest — around local midnight in late June, shifting earlier in the evening through July and August. Combined with Polaris, it gives you a rough compass in any weather.
- **The galactic centre lies near the stinger.** If you have made it to a dark-sky site, aim binoculars at the handful of sky between the scorpion's tail and the teapot of Sagittarius. You are scanning the most star-dense region visible from Earth. The Milky Way here is not a pale band — it is a dense blur of uncountable stars.

## Stretch — Challenges

*Six invitations to the low south. Scorpius never climbs high from Britain — catch him while summer holds him up.*

1. **Find Antares.** On a clear summer evening between 10pm and midnight, step outside with a clear view south and see if you can find the one red star low in the southern sky. Note the date, the time, and what the sky was doing. *Grade: Starter. Unlocks the rest of this chapter's field triggers.*
1. **Walk the scorpion.** On the same evening, trace as much of the scorpion's curve as your horizon allows. From southern England you may only get the head and half the body; from Cornwall, you might catch the full stinger. Sketch what you can see. *Grade: Starter.*
1. **Mars check.** Every couple of years, Mars drifts through this patch of sky near Antares. When it does, look at both. Which is redder? The Greeks named Antares *anti-Ares* for exactly this side-by-side comparison. See what you reckon. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Name the head.** See if you can learn Antares, Graffias, Dschubba, Shaula, Lesath — what each name means and where it sits on the scorpion. *Grade: Core.*
1. **Orion–Scorpius swap.** Over the course of a year, note the date you *first* see Orion clear of the eastern horizon after sunset (autumn) and the date you *first* see Antares clear of the southern horizon after sunset (spring). The two dates will sit almost exactly six months apart — an old enmity holding its distance. *Grade: Stretch.*
1. **Ptolemy's Cluster.** With binoculars, sweep the sky above the stinger and see if you can find M7 — a bright, dense knot of stars. You're looking at the oldest cluster catalogued by name in the Western astronomical record. *Grade: Stretch.*
